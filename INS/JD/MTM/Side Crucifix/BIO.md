## 2.0 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

### 2.1 What this instructional is (system thesis)
The Side Crucifix is presented as a short, high-leverage bridge from **pinning** to **upper-body submissions** by forcibly breaking the opponent’s primary defensive structure in side control: **elbow position**. The core idea is to control **both elbows at once**:
- Far-side elbow: controlled by a **far-side underhook**.
- Near-side elbow: controlled by a **leg-based pin** (front leg, back leg shin pin, or arm scissor).

When both elbows are displaced away from the torso, the opponent’s ability to re-guard, elbow-escape, or reinsert frames is heavily degraded, enabling high-percentage submission entries.

---

### 2.2 The canonical pipeline (default order of operations)
**Primary Series (default):**
1) **Acquire far-side underhook** (forced methods for disciplined opponents).
2) **Keep the underhook** (close-loop retention so the opponent can’t re-pummel).
3) **Pin the near-side arm** with legs (choose method based on arm length/discipline).
4) Convert the control into a **finishing hub** (most notably: Chancery).

This order is emphasized as the highest-percentage pathway: **underhook first → pin second**.

---

### 2.3 Why underhooks are easier before side control (strategic priority)
A recurring strategic point: as opponent skill rises, it becomes increasingly important to **win the underhook before you settle into side control**.
- In guard/neutral positions the opponent’s defense is leg-centric, so they “accept” underhooks more readily.
- In pinned side control, defense becomes elbow-centric and underhooks become harder to secure.
Therefore, the system prioritizes underhook acquisition from:
- half guard top,
- bodylock/tight waist/tripod passing,
- turtle top (cow-catcher-type drops),
- knee-cut entries that land with underhook already in place.

---

### 2.4 The main finishing hub: Chancery
The instructional’s premier control-to-finish bridge is **Chancery** (a leg-based head-and-arm chancery):
- Built most cleanly from the **arm scissor** variant of side crucifix.
- Emphasizes a high-control wedge around **head + shoulder line**, not just arm control.

Key tactical doctrine in chancery:
- **Stabilize first**: expect a brief high-intensity escape burst; use posting/base and riding mechanics before attacking.
- **Quality requirement**: elbow position relative to your leg structure is decisive; incorrect placement leads to escapes.

---

### 2.5 Primary attack families
From the Side Crucifix system, the dominant finishing families are:
- **Kimura family** (with multiple base/finish variations, including stronger breaking mechanics when sitting out).
- **Triangle family** (notably side triangle / yoko sankaku; plus reverse/rear triangle contingencies).
- **Armbar family** (as a balance-loss or transition punishment pathway).
- **North–South strangle** (notably when using the reverse-order entry series).

---

### 2.6 Alternate entry series and unification with back attacks
Two major “system expanders” are included:
- **Reverse-order series**: pin near-side arm first, then re-enter the classic side crucifix structure (often via wrist pass), or route to north–south strangle.
- **Back crucifix ↔ side crucifix link**: a positional bridge that unifies chest-to-back and chest-to-chest attack systems, with a dilemma-based routing:
  - If head can slip through: back crucifix → side crucifix → chancery.
  - If opponent’s bridging/arch denies head slip: route to **rear triangle**.

---

### 2.7 What success looks like (system-level success criteria)
You are “running the system” correctly when:
- Far-side underhook is acquired and retained with a closed loop (no re-pummel window).
- Near-side elbow cannot reinsert to hip-line (pin prevents elbow escape mechanics).
- You can cycle among pin variants without losing elbow control.
- You reach a finishing hub (especially chancery) with stable base and correct capture geometry, enabling repeatable submission finishes.

## 4.0 CORE CONCEPTS

### 4.1 Elbow Structure Is The Primary Defensive Frame
- In side pins, the opponent’s primary defense is elbow position: elbows close to the torso enable framing, elbow escapes, and guard recovery.
- The Side Crucifix is designed to break this defensive structure by drawing both elbows away from the body.

### 4.2 Side Crucifix = Two-Component Control Model (Both Elbows Controlled)
- A classic side crucifix always controls **both elbows at once**:
  - Far-side elbow via a far-side underhook.
  - Near-side elbow via a leg-based pin.
- When both elbows are displaced, defensive options collapse rapidly.

### 4.3 Primary Series Doctrine (Default Order)
- The default, highest-percentage order of operations is:
  **far-side underhook first → near-side arm pin second**.
- This sequencing ensures one elbow is secured before attempting to isolate the second.

### 4.4 Underhook Retention: “Closed Loop” Requirement
- The most common failure is leaving space between the chin and the underhooking hand.
- Retention requires a closed loop:
  - Chin connected to the hand,
  - Ear glued to the opponent’s bicep to deny elbow travel,
  - A posted hand for base so the head stays higher than the hips.

### 4.5 Underhooks Are Easier Before Side Control (Neutral-Position Priority)
- As opponent skill increases, underhooks become harder to secure once side control is established.
- From guard or neutral positions, opponents defend primarily with legs and are more willing to concede underhooks.
- The system therefore prioritizes winning the underhook **before** settling fully into side control whenever possible.

### 4.6 Pinning The Near-Side Arm: 3-Mode, Cycleable Control
- There are three canonical pinning modes:
  - Front leg pin,
  - Back leg pin,
  - Arm scissor.
- These are not preferences; they are a **cycle**. You should be able to move between them to maintain elbow displacement.

### 4.7 Hand-Assist Principle For Pin Entries (Against Disciplined Elbows)
- Against strong, disciplined elbow positioning, leg pins are completed with hand assistance:
  - If the arm is long, control the wrist at the end of the lever.
  - If the arm is short and glued to the body, insert the hand inside, control the bicep/shoulder junction, and lever the elbow out with knee–elbow joining mechanics.

### 4.8 Chancery As The Premier Control Hub
- Chancery is the system’s primary control-to-submission hub.
- By trapping the head and shoulder with the legs, it creates a level of helplessness that enables reliable upper-body finishes.
- The doctrine is stability first: expect resistance, establish base and ride control, then attack.

### 4.9 Hand–Elbow Height Heuristic (Underhook Availability Trigger)
- A simple visual rule governs underhook timing:
  - If the hand is at or below elbow height, the underhook is often available immediately.
  - If the hand is high, assume the underhook must be forced with a dedicated method.

### 4.10 Base Before Breaking (Stability Precedes Submission)
- Any time both hands leave the mat to attack, base narrows and balance becomes vulnerable.
- The system prioritizes maintaining base before committing to breaking mechanics.
- Balance-loss outcomes (armbars, triangles) are treated as planned follow-ups, not accidents.

### 4.11 “Win The Elbow, Then The Wrist” Ordering (Control Hierarchy)
- Against skilled opponents, elbow displacement is the priority.
- Wrist control without elbow control is unreliable.
- Wrist attacks are favored only once the elbow has been meaningfully displaced.

### 4.12 Stabilization Window Doctrine (Temporal Concept)
- After a major capture (especially chancery), expect a short burst of maximal resistance.
- High-commitment attacks are delayed until this initial escape window collapses.

### 4.13 Pin Cycling As Escape Insurance (Not Preference)
- Switching between front leg pin, back leg pin, and arm scissor is a defensive insurance policy.
- Cycling pins preserves elbow displacement and denies reinsertion escapes.

### 4.14 Direction-of-Failure Routing (Troubleshooting Logic)
- When the system destabilizes, the correction depends on what failed:
  - Structural failure (grips, re-pummeling) → reassert underhook loop and elbow displacement.
  - Rotational or balance failure → route into armbar or triangle follow-ups instead of forcing the same line.    

## 5.0 SYSTEM OVERVIEW

### 5.1 System Objective (What the system produces)
Build a control state where the opponent’s elbows are forcibly displaced away from the torso so that:
- Their elbow-escape and frame recovery are heavily degraded.
- Upper-body submissions become mechanically easier and faster.

This is achieved by controlling **both elbows simultaneously**:
- Far elbow: far-side underhook.
- Near elbow: leg-based pin (front leg pin, back leg shin pin, or arm scissor).

---

### 5.2 The Default Route (Primary Series: Underhook → Pin → Hub)
**Step 1 — Acquire the far-side underhook**
- If the opponent’s hand is low (hand ≤ elbow), take the underhook opportunistically.
- If the opponent’s hand is high/defensive, assume you must force the underhook with a dedicated method.
- If the opponent is actively posting/pushing on your shoulder, prefer the wrist-pass branch.

**Step 2 — Retain the underhook (closed-loop retention)**
- Close the pummel window: chin-to-hand connection + ear-to-bicep contact.
- Maintain base with a post so your head stays higher than hips.

**Step 3 — Pin the near-side arm (hand-assisted completion)**
Choose based on arm length/discipline:
- Long-arm case: isolate wrist (end of lever) → leg pin.
- Short-arm case (common vs good opponents): inside-hand/bicep control → lever elbow out → pin.
- Tripod-based completion when hands are open and posture can rise safely.

**Step 4 — Choose the control variant (pin type)**
- Front leg pin
- Back leg shin pin
- Arm scissor (highest control; best feed to chancery)
These are cycleable; switching pins is part of control maintenance.

**Step 5 — Route to a finishing hub**
- If arm scissor: route to **Chancery** (primary hub).
- If back leg shin pin: route to **Kimura / Juji lines**.
- If front leg pin: route to **Triangle lines**.

---

### 5.3 Chancery Hub (Primary Control-to-Finish Bridge)
**Purpose:** Convert side crucifix control into a head-and-arm capture that suppresses escape attempts and enables high-percentage finishes.

**Stability-first doctrine**
- Expect a brief “max resistance” phase right after capture.
- Stabilize with posting and ride control before initiating high-commitment breaks.

**Primary attack families from Chancery**
- Kimura family (multiple basing options; sit-out variants increase breaking leverage).
- Side triangle (yoko sankaku) entered with a “stay tall until strangle is on” approach.
- If balance breaks or opponent’s movement forces it: armbar and triangle follow-ups become structured continuations rather than accidents.

---

### 5.4 Alternative Route (Secondary Series: Pin → Underhook)
When the default order is blocked or when contingencies appear:
- Pin the near-side arm first (typically via reverse crossface / near-arm control)
Then choose:
- Wrist pass back into the classic side crucifix structure (re-establish underhook and proceed to chancery), or
- North–south strangle as an immediate submission route.

---

### 5.5 Back Crucifix Link (Unifying Chest-to-Back and Chest-to-Chest)
The system includes a bridge from back crucifix into side crucifix:
- If you can build height and hollow to let the opponent’s head slip through: transition to side crucifix → chancery.
- If the opponent bridges/arches and denies the head slip: switch direction and route to rear triangle (ushiro sankaku).

This creates a dilemma: head slips → side crucifix; head blocks → rear triangle.

---

### 5.6 Global Gates (What decides your branch)
**G1 — Underhook availability trigger**
- Hand low: take underhook now.
- Hand high: force underhook.
- Active shoulder post: wrist-pass branch.

**G2 — Underhook retention gate**
- If you cannot close the chin-to-hand loop, do not advance to pinning; re-secure retention first.

**G3 — Pin-selection gate**
- Long arm exposed: wrist control routes.
- Short arm glued: inside-hand/bicep routes.
- If posture can rise safely: tripod-based completions.

**G4 — Base gate**
- If your head drops below hips or you feel roll risk: post and widen base before attacking.

**G5 — Chancery stabilization gate**
- If opponent is in peak escape output: stabilize and ride; delay high-commitment finishing attempts until output decays.

---

### 5.7 Quality Checks (What “good” feels like)
**Side crucifix quality**
- Far elbow cannot reinsert to ribs (underhook is retained).
- Near elbow cannot return to hip-line (pin denies elbow escape mechanics).
- You can switch pin variants without losing elbow displacement.

**Chancery quality**
- Head/shoulder capture prevents the opponent from turning freely.
- Your base and posts prevent being rolled or forced off the capture.

---

### 5.8 Where each volume fits (System map to curriculum)
- Vol 1: forcing underhooks from side control + mount-based setups.
- Vol 2: underhooks from neutral/passing/turtle + retention + near-arm pinning methods.
- Vol 3: chancery construction + kimura family + side triangle routes.
- Vol 4: troubleshooting chancery + balance-loss follow-ups (armbar/triangle variants).
- Vol 5: shin-pin branches + reverse-order entry + north–south strangle.
- Vol 6: back crucifix link + full system summary and integration.

## 6.0 TECHNIQUE LIBRARY

### 6.1 Volume 1 — Underhook Acquisition From Side Control (Core Entries)

#### 6.1.1 Introduction (0:00 - 3:17)
- **Objective:** Establish Side Crucifix as a fast bridge to increase upper-body submission rate with minimal complexity/athleticism.
- **Core claim:** Upper-body submissions rise fastest when you can force an opponent’s **elbows away from their torso**.
- **System framing:** Side Crucifix is built to do this reliably with simple, repeatable mechanics.

#### 6.1.2 Reflections On Side Crucifix (3:17 - 9:33)
- **Objective:** Define the control problem and why Side Crucifix solves it.
- **Control problem:** You must control **two elbows**:
  - **Far side** elbow is controlled by the **underhook**.
  - **Near side** elbow is controlled by **pinning the arm with your legs**.
- **Strategic premise:** The move is valuable because it’s **localized and compartmentalized**—a short series that yields quick results once learned.

#### 6.1.3 How To Get A Underhook From Side Control — Ten Finger Method (9:33 - 16:50)
- **Objective:** Force a far-side underhook from side control against a disciplined defender.
- **When to use:** Opponent’s far-side hand is **high** (hand higher than elbow) and pummeling is hard.
- **Primary decision rule (visual cue):**
  - If **hand ≤ elbow height** → underhook can be taken directly.
  - If **hand > elbow height** → use a forcing method (this).
- **Steps (high percentage sequence):**
  1) Start in side control with a crossface; transition to a **one-handed control** when ready to insert.
  2) Turn the inserting hand **palm up** and feed it into the **space between wrist and bicep** (narrow hand fits where elbow cannot).
  3) **Do NOT lift your chest** to “reach” the insert (guard recovery/underhook concession risk).
  4) Lock a **ten-finger grip** configuration and use your elbow to **lever their elbow outward**.
  5) Put your **forehead to your own bicep**, then **pull your head back and circle it around** their forearm to complete the underhook lane.
  6) **Immediately close the retention loop** (chin to shoulder; ear to their bicep) to deny re-pummel.
- **Opponent reactions → responses:**
  - Opponent resists by tightening elbow to ribs → you still lever elbow outward through ten-finger connection (don’t abandon chest pressure).
- **Failure signatures → fixes:**
  - **Chest lifts** during insertion → keep shoulder pressure and insert with narrow hand only.
  - **Underhook achieved but lost** → tighten chin-to-shoulder and ear-to-bicep immediately.

#### 6.1.4 Reverse Overhook Method (16:50 - 22:27)
- **Objective:** Create an underhook even when opponent clamps elbow extremely tight to ribs.
- **When to use:** Ten-finger entry is blocked or feels mechanically inaccessible; opponent’s elbow is welded tight.
- **Key concept:** **Knife wedge** principle.
  - Your **wrist** is the “sharp edge” that can enter where the elbow cannot.
  - Once the wedge opens the space, the **elbow** follows (“spine of the knife”).
- **Steps:**
  1) From strong pin/crossface alignment, **slice your wrist underneath** the opponent’s elbow (no matter how tight).
  2) As your elbow and bicep enter, **lift their elbow outward**.
  3) Bring your **thumb wedge underneath the tricep** to lock the position.
  4) Walk/adjust to stabilize, then **punch your hand through** to complete the underhook.
  5) Return the opponent flat and settle into side pin with a **strong underhook**.
- **Failure signatures → fixes:**
  - Wedge too shallow → emphasize wrist “edge” first; don’t try to jam the elbow in early.
  - Underhook achieved but loose → immediately return to tight side pin and close retention loop.

#### 6.1.5 Wrist Pass Method (22:27 - 29:42)
- **Objective:** Convert an opponent’s active shoulder post into an underhook.
- **When to use:** Opponent is **posting/pushing on your shoulder** (active defense rather than static clamp).
- **Steps:**
  1) Anchor your hand to the opponent’s **shoulder**.
  2) As they push you, **go with the push** (don’t fight it head-on).
  3) Insert your hand **inside their wrist**.
  4) Push the wrist and **straighten your whole body** to pass their wrist from one shoulder **across the center line** to the other side.
  5) **Critical finish:** pass the wrist **under your shoulder** so your **shoulder pins their elbow**.
- **Quality check:** If their hand and elbow remain **above your shoulder**, they can pummel back and recover.
- **Failure signatures → fixes:**
  - “Half pass” (wrist stays near center) → pass across center line until elbow is pinned beneath your shoulder.
  - Loss of base during pass → post with free hand immediately.

#### 6.1.6 Using The Mounted Position To Get To Your Underhook (29:42 - 36:57)
- **Objective:** Use mount threats (or mount itself) to create predictable reactions that open the underhook.
- **Two methods:**

  **A) Start side control → threaten mount → opponent posts on your knee → underhook**
  - **Steps:**
    1) From side control, secure crossface (grip the lat) and put **shoulder-to-shoulder**, head low.
    2) **Walk** to make the opponent’s **knees point away** (if knees point in, mounting a good opponent is hard; they re-guard).
    3) Stabilize with **hand post + forehead on mat**; optionally post on opponent’s knee to keep it turned out.
    4) Walk back to clear their far elbow out of tight defense.
    5) Drop knee toward mount with **knee on floor** (do **not** plant foot; keep foot up).
    6) Opponent’s common defense is to **put a hand on your knee** to stop mount.
    7) As soon as they post on your knee, **put your hand inside their elbow** and “come back where you came from” into the underhook lane.
    8) Close retention: chin close to shoulder; ear to bicep.

  **B) Start mount → threaten arm walk-up → opponent clamps/locks hands → dismount to underhook**
  - **Steps:**
    1) From mount with crossface, attempt to **walk the arm up**.
    2) If opponent clamps/locks hands to stop the walk-up: **head to the floor**, dismount to the opposite side.
    3) The dismount places you into **side control with underhook**.
- **Failure signatures → fixes:**
  - Mount threat fails because knees still point toward you → first solve knee direction (knees away) before trying to mount.
  - Underhook gained but lost → close the loop immediately (chin-to-shoulder).

#### 6.1.7 Fake American Lock (36:57 - 39:21)
- **Objective:** Use an Americana threat to induce an extension reaction that yields the underhook.
- **When to use:** Opponent is defensively strong and won’t give pummel space; you need a “reaction-maker.”
- **Steps:**
  1) Cup the shoulder.
  2) Sit out partially and **drive forward with your shoulder** to secure the wrist and **threaten Americana**.
  3) Common defense: opponent bridges and **pushes the arm away/extends** to escape the Americana.
  4) You “lose” the Americana, but the extension **surrenders the underhook**.
  5) Immediately tighten retention: hand to chin; chin to shoulder; ear to bicep.
- **Failure signatures → fixes:**
  - Americana threat isn’t believable → ensure shoulder drive and wrist capture are real enough to force extension.
  - Underhook gained but loose → tighten immediately; make re-pummel require surrendering the Americana threat again (cycle).

### 6.2 Volume 2 — Underhooks Prior To Side Control + Underhook Retention + Near-Arm Pinning

#### 6.2.1 Getting Underhooks Prior To Side Control — Why It’s Easier From Neutral Than Side Control (0:00 - 2:41)
- **Objective:** Establish strategic priority: win underhooks earlier.
- **Key idea:** In neutral/passing phases, defense is often leg-centric and underhooks are easier; once pinned, defense becomes elbow-centric and underhooks are harder.
- **Actionable guidance:** If you can arrive to side control already owning the underhook, you shorten the system and reduce fights.

#### 6.2.2 Getting To An Underhook From Half Guard Top (2:41 - 6:16)
- **Objective:** Use half guard top as a reliable underhook-acquisition platform.
- **Core sequence (operator summary):**
  - Maintain head position and pressure so opponent can’t freely frame.
  - Use inside-hand insertion paths that win the underhook before settling into a pin.
- **Common failure:** trying to “settle side control” first, then hunt underhook against tight elbows.
- **Fix:** treat half guard top as the underhook-winning phase; then pass.

#### 6.2.3 Bodylock Tight Waist And Tripod Into Underhook (6:16 - 13:23)
- **Objective:** Use tight waist/bodylock and tripod posture to open underhook lanes.
- **Core mechanics:**
  - Tight waist control prevents hip escape while tripod posture changes shoulder angles.
  - As posture rises, the opponent’s elbow/hand relationship changes—making underhook entry easier.
- **Failure signature:** bodylock exists but opponent re-frames and re-inserts elbow.
- **Fix:** keep hips covered; win underhook during posture/angle change, not after you flatten.

#### 6.2.4 Underhook From Turtle Top (13:23 - 17:16)
- **Objective:** Build underhook while attacking turtle.
- **System role:** Turtle top attacks can produce underhook-based transitions that feed directly to side crucifix assembly.
- **Key theme:** control hips/waist, then use angle changes to insert the underhook before opponent squares back up.

#### 6.2.5 Knee Cut Passes To Underhook (17:16 - 20:14)
- **Objective:** Land knee cut with underhook already present (best-case arrival).
- **Decision rule:** Use knee cut lines that finish with shoulder position enabling underhook retention; avoid “knee cut then hunt.”
- **Failure signature:** knee cut completes but opponent wins inside elbow/frames.
- **Fix:** prioritize underhook ownership during the pass, not as a post-pass project.

#### 6.2.6 Keeping An Underhook (20:14 - 24:42)
- **Objective:** Prevent re-pummel after underhook capture (retention engine).
- **Key mechanics:**
  - Close the loop: chin on shoulder and close to the underhook hand.
  - Ear glued to opponent’s bicep.
  - Base: posting hand prevents head dropping below hips.
- **Failure signature:** visible space between chin and hand (“daylight”) → opponent pummels back in.
- **Fix:** close the loop first; do not advance to leg pinning until retention is stable.

#### 6.2.7 Long Arm/Wrist Control (24:42 - 27:59)
- **Objective:** Pin near-side arm when the opponent’s arm is long/extended.
- **Decision rule:** If the wrist is accessible at the end of the lever, control wrist first; it’s the most reliable handle.
- **Core mechanics:** lengthen the arm to expose wrist → shin/leg pin becomes easy because the elbow can’t recoil to ribs.

#### 6.2.8 Short Arm Bicep Control (27:59 - 33:21)
- **Objective:** Pin near-side arm when elbow is glued (wrist not available).
- **Decision rule:** Stop chasing wrist; insert hand inside and attach to bicep/shoulder junction.
- **Core mechanics:** inside-hand to bicep creates a lever to open the elbow line; once elbow line opens, knee/leg pin completes.

#### 6.2.9 Open Handed Lead Leg (33:21 - 38:54)
- **Objective:** Use open-handed control and lead-leg positioning to complete pin without over-committing grips.
- **Core idea:** posture/leg positioning can replace “death grips” when the opponent’s elbow line is already compromised.

#### 6.2.10 Underhook Method (38:54 - 43:06)
- **Objective:** A near-arm pin completion pathway that ties directly to underhook ownership.
- **System role:** Once underhook is stable, use it to create the angle/space needed to pin the near-side arm.

#### 6.2.11 The Three Methods Of Pinning The Near Side Arm (43:06 - 46:34)
- **Objective:** Define the three canonical pin modes and the requirement to cycle.
- **Rule:** If you have access to one, you should be able to access the other two; you must flow between them.
- **Methods:**
  1) **Front leg pin:** the leg closer to opponent’s head pins the near arm.
  2) **Back leg pin:** pommel over; switch pins by crossing ankles and replacing front with back (and vice versa).
  3) **Arm scissor:** pinch knees around shoulders and sit down; scissor (don’t squeeze):
     - bottom knee wedges under shoulder (lifts shoulder),
     - top knee wedges over elbow (traps elbow),
     - makes “limp arm” escape significantly harder.

### 6.3 Volume 3 — Chancery Hub + Kimura Family + Yoko Sankaku

#### 6.3.1 Chancery: The Best Side Pin Control For Upper Body Submissions — What Is Chancery (0:00 - 3:53)
- **Objective:** Introduce Chancery as the premier control-to-submission hub out of side crucifix.
- **Definition:** From side crucifix (typically with arm scissor + far-side underhook), you draw the opponent up and trap **head + arm/shoulder** under your legs.
- **Why it’s powerful:** It captures arm, head, and shoulder while you control the opposite arm—making twisting/turning escape extremely difficult.

#### 6.3.2 How To Properly Transition Into Chancery — Securing The Elbow Properly (3:53 - 9:37)
- **Objective:** Enter chancery with the elbow geometry that prevents escape.
- **Key mechanics:**
  - In arm scissor: bottom knee controls shoulder; top knee controls elbow.
  - As you come up, **the direction of your lower leg** is critical.
- **Primary quality rule:** The opponent’s trapped elbow must be **outside your calf**.
  - If elbow is **inside** your leg, you will not control them.
- **Secondary quality rules:**
  - Pull their body toward you so your **knees are tight to their shoulder line** (distance = failure).
  - When stepping over the head, your foot must point **inward**.
    - If foot points outward, opponent can slip elbow.
  - Cross ankles to reinforce and make elbow slip impossible.
- **Failure signatures → fixes:**
  - Elbow inside calf → re-angle and pull in; don’t proceed.
  - Foot points out → re-point in, then re-cross ankles with controlling leg on top.

#### 6.3.3 Building A Rock Solid Control From Chancery (9:37 - 15:28)
- **Objective:** Stabilize chancery before attacking.
- **Critical stabilizing details:**
  - Curl foot inward; control leg goes on top when crossing ankles (wrong lock orientation makes escape easier).
  - Expect a brief “berserk” period: opponent will thrash to escape.
  - **Do not attack kimura too early** because chancery base can be narrow.
  - Keep far-side underhook and use your hand as a base: **three posts** (two knees + hand) beats two posts (knees only).
- **Opponent reactions → responses:**
  - Opponent throws stomach toward floor to unbalance you → widen base with posting hand and ride; don’t abandon control.
- **Failure signatures → fixes:**
  - You fall during early attack attempts → delay attacks; stabilize with posting and underhook ride first.

#### 6.3.4 Conversion To Kimura (15:28 - 19:58)
- **Objective:** Convert stable chancery control into kimura.
- **System rule:** Stabilize first, then isolate arm for kimura; prioritize control over speed.
- **Key idea:** Once head/shoulder is controlled, kimura becomes the first finish option.

#### 6.3.5 Kimura Variations From Chancery (19:58 - 24:31)
- **Objective:** Provide multiple basing options to match resistance and alignment.
- **Three base models:**
  1) **Two knees on the ground:** strong wedge and stability for controlled finish.
  2) **One knee off the ground:** more height and ability to move up the arm through distance.
  3) **Full sit-out split:** strong, stable, high leverage for finishing power.
- **Failure signature:** opponent’s explosive movement breaks your base.
- **Fix:** choose base model that matches opponent output; don’t default to narrow-base attacks during the stabilization window.

#### 6.3.6 Dorsal Kimura And Sit Out Kimura (24:31 - 29:33)
- **Objective:** Strengthen kimura finishes via dorsal angle and sit-out mechanics.
- **Core idea:** sitting out and splitting base increases leverage and stability for finishing.

#### 6.3.7 Chancery To Yoko Sankaku (Side Triangle) (29:33 - 40:43)
- **Objective:** Convert chancery capture into a tight side triangle with minimal escape space.
- **Primary doctrine:** Don’t drop to the shoulder early.
  - Stay up on the elbow first; put the strangle on before committing body to the floor.
- **Leg-threading principle:** establish shoelace-on-top / feed bottom leg through, then adjust until the strangle is already active.
- **Failure signature:** dropping early allows explosive turn-in and chaos.
- **Fix:** remain tall until shoe-lace-to-knee-crook alignment is strong; then settle.

### 6.4 Volume 4 — Chancery Follow-Ups + Troubleshooting

#### 6.4.1 What To Do If You Lose Your Balance — Juji Gatame (Armbar) (0:00 - 4:57)
- **Objective:** Provide an immediate, high-percentage continuation when chancery base is compromised.
- **Problem:** Chancery base can be narrow; explosive opponents may force balance loss.
- **Primary response:** Switch into **juji gatame** rather than “falling into nothing.”
- **Operator rule:** If balance is going, redirect to an armbar structure with positive control.

#### 6.4.2 ¾ Juji Gatame (4:57 - 9:05)
- **Objective:** Provide a powerful armbar variant when conventional juji alignment isn’t ideal.
- **Core description:** Legs go across opponent’s head differently than conventional juji; both legs are over the shoulder line.
- **Common route described:** triangle threats can force posture; as they posture, shift leg configuration into ¾ juji.
- **Failure signature:** falling without control.
- **Fix:** always “fall into something positive” (armbar/triangle structure).

#### 6.4.3 Falling Into Reverse Triangle/Kimura (9:05 - 12:50)
- **Objective:** Convert balance loss during kimura attempts into reverse triangle + kimura finishing structures.
- **System theme:** When balance is lost, you can still land into strong finishing geometry instead of resetting.

#### 6.4.4 An Alternative To Chancery Reverse Triangle (Gyaku Sankaku) (12:50 - 18:22)
- **Objective:** Build a full reverse triangle configuration as an alternate to the crossed-ankle chancery structure.
- **Key mechanics:**
  - Start from classic side crucifix (underhook far side + arm scissor near side).
  - Step over head with a **head-blocking hand**.
  - **Rake the leg back** so the knee comes down; keep chest down and close (different from drawing opponent up).
  - Lock a full triangle (not just ankles crossed).
- **Failure signature:** loose triangle because knee didn’t rake back / distance too large.
- **Fix:** rake knee down and stay close to the floor; then lock.

#### 6.4.5 Troubleshooting — Opponent Grips Inside His Own Leg (18:22 - 22:33)
- **Objective:** Solve a common defense to kimura from chancery: opponent hides hand inside their own thigh.
- **Problem:** Hiding the hand inside the thigh blocks kimura grip progression.
- **System response (high-level):**
  - Transition between reverse triangle and side triangle orientations (“play back and forth”) to re-expose finishing angles.
  - Treat reverse triangle → leg switch → side triangle as a tight, repeatable conversion when grip defense blocks kimura.

### 6.5 Volume 5 — Shin Pin Options + Reverse-Order Entry + North–South

#### 6.5.1 Another Great Option From Side Crucifix: The Shin Pin — Back Leg Shin Pin Into Kimura (0:00 - 4:11)
- **Objective:** Provide a high-value alternative when perfect chancery is difficult.
- **When to use:** Shorter legs, opponent broad shoulders, or chancery entry resistance.
- **Core mechanics:**
  - Use shin pin as a “90% solution”: slightly less controlling than chancery but easier to reach.
  - Switch between front leg shin pin and back leg shin pin to maintain arm isolation.
  - From back leg shin pin, kimura becomes a natural first attack line.

#### 6.5.2 Back Leg Shin Pin Into Armbar (4:11 - 10:33)
- **Objective:** Route shin pin control to armbar when kimura is blocked or balance/angle demands it.
- **Key mechanics:**
  - Keep arm pinned continuously while transitioning (brief ankle cross as you pommel the second foot across).
  - Step over head as in chancery-style control, but maintain shin pinning of elbow.
- **Failure signature:** withdrawing control during transition allows arm extraction.
- **Fix:** treat ankle-cross moment as “insurance” while you move the other leg.

#### 6.5.3 Front Leg Shin Pin Into Triangle (10:33 - 17:18)
- **Objective:** Establish triangle finishes from front leg pin configuration.
- **Critical detail:** **Body alignment first.**
  - If you are too perpendicular, knee can’t insert into bicep.
  - Line hips up with opponent’s hips.
- **Core mechanics:**
  - Come up into tripod (head to mat) to connect **knee to your own elbow**—this creates the insertion lane.
  - Once knee is inside bicep, route across body into triangle.
- **Failure signature:** knee can’t get inside bicep.
- **Fix:** correct hip alignment and use tripod/head post to create insertion.

#### 6.5.4 Alternative Method To Entering Side Crucifix: Pin The Nearside Arm First — Reverse Crossface Into Wrist Pass (17:18 - 23:38)
- **Objective:** Reverse the order (near-arm pin first) when opponent makes far-side underhook extremely difficult.
- **When to use:** Skilled opponent preemptively denies underhook; you don’t want the system to stall on Step 1.
- **Core sequence:**
  1) Use **reverse crossface** to change head/shoulder alignment and open pinning opportunities.
  2) Walk body away from opponent’s elbow; sit to place head inside elbow line.
  3) Come up and **pin the near-side arm first**.
  4) From the pin, use **wrist pass** to regain far-side underhook and return to classic side crucifix structure.
  5) Primary continuation: arm scissor → chancery.

#### 6.5.5 Entry To North South Strangle (23:38 - 29:06)
- **Objective:** Provide a finish route when you have the near-arm pinned but cannot obtain an underhook/wrist pass return.
- **Core mechanics (operator summary):**
  - From reverse crossface + near-arm pin, bring chin to ribs and thread arm around head.
  - Use ribcage pressure against cheek so opponent cannot look back toward you.
  - Connect hands, walk down to north–south, and set strangle quickly.
- **Failure signature:** opponent turns head toward you and creates space.
- **Fix:** commit ribcage-to-cheek pressure while threading; keep angle “too far underneath” for them to face you.

### 6.6 Volume 6 — Back Crucifix Link + Full System Summary

#### 6.6.1 Back Crucifix Into Side Crucifix — 2 Directions Of Attack (0:00 - 9:26)
- **Objective:** Link back crucifix attacks to side crucifix system with a clear dilemma.
- **Key setup (back crucifix entry from turtle top):**
  - Control hips and far hip (tight waist/seatbelt style).
  - Post and wedge knee behind opponent’s elbow.
  - Bring forward foot around the corner, sit to hips to establish back crucifix.
  - Retract/back-heel feet to prevent elbow wiggle escapes.
- **Critical conversion detail:** **Head position** during conversion.
- **Conversion A (to side crucifix):**
  - Get a strong handle at armpit/hip line and come up to elbow/knees to build height.
  - **Hollow out your body** so opponent’s head slips through the corner.
  - Result: classic **side crucifix**, then default to **chancery**.
- **Conversion B (to rear triangle / ushiro sankaku):**
  - If opponent pushes with legs / bridges and wins height so head can’t slip:
    - Change direction and bring knee around behind, gather arm, transition to rear triangle.
- **Decision rule (dilemma):**
  - If opponent’s lower back stays on ground (hollowed) → side crucifix route.
  - If opponent arches/bridges (back off ground) → rear triangle route.
- **Failure signature:** trying to force head slip when opponent is winning height.
- **Fix:** switch direction immediately to rear triangle; don’t persist.

#### 6.6.2 Putting It All Together: Summarizing The Side Crucifix System (9:26 - 26:05)
- **Objective:** Provide a compact executable system summary.
- **Three core elements:**
  1) **Get far-side underhook** (free if available; forced via methods when disciplined).
  2) **Pin near-side arm** inside legs.
     - Primary approach: lengthen arm to expose wrist; if arm is short, insert hand inside and connect to bicep.
     - Three pin modes: front leg pin, back leg pin (switchable), arm scissor (preferred).
  3) **Enter submission hubs** based on pin type:
     - Arm scissor → **Chancery** → kimura family / side triangle.
     - Back leg shin pin → kimura / juji variations (including “double trouble” style control).
     - Front leg pin → triangle finishes (forward-fall or backward-fall variants).
- **Secondary series (reverse order):**
  - Reverse crossface → pin near arm first → wrist pass back into side crucifix → chancery.
  - If wrist pass isn’t available → north–south strangle.
- **Back crucifix link (system unifier):**
  - Back crucifix → side crucifix if head can slip.
  - If head can’t slip due to bridging/height, route to rear triangle.

[Q] CHECKS

  2) ear glued to opponent bicep (deny elbow travel)
  3) shoulder line tight (no reinsert lane)
  4) base present: posting hand available; head stays higher than hips

[G] FAIL-FAST RULE
  If any check fails -> pause progression -> rebuild loop before pinning near arm

  - post hand wide (3 posts beats 2)

  - ride turns; prevent belly-out/rise
   |
   v
ATTACK SECOND (after output decays)
  - kimura family (base options)
  - yoko sankaku (stay tall until strangle is on)
  - if balance breaks -> armbar/triangle continuations
~~~

### 7.8 Kimura Base Options (Selection by Stability)
~~~
KIMURA FROM CHANCERY — choose base to match resistance

(1) TWO KNEES DOWN
  - tight wedges
  - stable for controlled finish

(2) ONE KNEE UP
  - more height/mobility
  - better ability to walk arm

(3) SIT-OUT SPLIT
  - max leverage + stability
  - high finishing power

RULE:
  If balance is threatened -> downgrade to a more stable base before applying break
~~~

### 7.10 Back Crucifix Link (Dilemma Map) — Side Crucifix vs Rear Triangle

### 7.11 Control Hierarchy (Elbow-First vs Wrist-First)
~~~
CONTROL PRIORITY (prevents stalled pin attempts)

READ: IS THE ELBOW GLUED TO THE TORSO?
   |
   +-- NO (elbow displaced / long arm)  -> WRIST-FIRST acceptable
   |                                     - control end of lever
   |                                     - lengthen -> pin
   |
   +-- YES (elbow glued / short arm)     -> ELBOW-FIRST mandatory
                                         - inside-hand / bicep control
                                         - lever elbow line open
                                         - then wrist becomes available
~~~

### 7.13 Pin Cycling Failure-Recovery Loop (When Elbow Starts Slipping)
~~~
PIN CONTROL DRIFT (early warning)
  - elbow begins to slide toward hip-line
  - wrist/hand starts to retract
  - shoulder line rotates toward safety

RESPONSE LOOP
      |
      v
SWITCH PIN MODE (front ↔ back ↔ scissor)
      |
      v
RE-DISPLACE ELBOW (restore "two-elbow break")
      |
      v
RETURN TO HUB ROUTE (chancery / kimura / triangle)

Fail-fast:
  If you wait until elbow fully reinserts -> you'll restart from underhook acquisition.
~~~

### 7.14 Failure-Type Routing Map (Structural vs Rotational)
~~~
FAILURE OCCURS DURING CONTROL/ATTACK
    |
    +-- STRUCTURAL FAILURE
    |     (re-pummel, hands lock, grips blocked, elbow reinserts)
    |        -> REASSERT CONTROL HIERARCHY
    |           - close underhook loop
    |           - re-displace elbow
    |           - re-pin (cycle)
    |
    +-- ROTATIONAL / BASE FAILURE
          (you are being rolled, head drops below hips, base collapses)
             -> ROUTE INTO "POSITIVE FALL"
                - armbar (juji / 3/4 juji)
                - triangle (yoko / reverse)
             -> then rebuild control if finish fails
~~~
