## 2.0 Executive Summary

This report describes a closed-guard system organized around a single controlling objective: **win the posture battle**. From closed guard, the attacker’s first priority is to **break posture** by removing posts, forcing weight forward or backward, and using the legs as the primary engine to destabilize base.

Once posture is compromised, the system shifts to **entering dominant positions** using two variables—**height** and **angle**—to create asymmetric control. Dominant positions are treated as hubs that feed predictable outcomes: **back exposure, sweeps, and submissions**. A central hub is **Side Scissor**, structured as a trilemma that forces the opponent to concede one of: (i) height, (ii) post/base, or (iii) elbow position—each concession routes directly into attacks or a secondary hub (notably **Clamp**).

When extreme height/angle is unavailable, the system uses **weakly dominant** controls (e.g., **Top Lock, Trap Triangle, Elbow Overhook**) to maintain initiative and re-enter strongly dominant positions.

If the opponent successfully postures or stands (i.e., the posture battle is lost), the system transitions to a **lower-body compensation branch**: leg attacks and backward/off-balancing sweeps that exploit the opponent’s extended, leg-heavy posture.

The result is a closed-guard decision tree built to:
- **Force posture errors**, then
- **Convert those errors into dominant positional hubs**, and
- **Finish, sweep, or re-cycle** without stalling in neutral control.

## 4.0 Core Concepts

### 4.1 Primary objective: win posture
Closed guard is treated as a posture-control problem. Your first job is to prevent or reverse posture-building (head/chest rising, hips retreating, standing). If posture is won, attacks become high percentage; if posture is lost, upper-body attacks degrade and you must switch branches.

### 4.2 Clear “posture-won” signals (operational tests)
You are winning the posture battle when at least one of the following is true:
- The opponent must post with hands (forward or backward) to avoid being broken down.
- The opponent’s shoulders/head are driven below their hips or their hips are forced to the mat.
- The opponent cannot freely stand and reset without conceding balance.

These are not “nice-to-haves”; they are the trigger conditions to enter the next phase.

### 4.3 Phase structure: Break posture → build dominance → convert
The system is sequenced:
1) **Break posture** (create instability and remove posts).
2) **Work to a dominant position** (height + angle).
3) **Convert** (sweep, back exposure, or submission), or recycle if blocked.

This avoids “move hunting” and keeps the system decision-based.

### 4.4 Dominant positions are the unit of strategy (not individual techniques)
The report treats named positions as hubs that generate multiple outcomes. A hub is “valid” only if it:
- constrains posture or posts,
- gives repeatable access to at least two outcomes (e.g., sweep + submission),
- and has a defined failure response (what you do when defended).

### 4.5 Two global control variables: height and angle
Dominance is measured by:
- **Height:** whether you can rise relative to the opponent’s posture (or force their posture down).
- **Angle:** whether your hips/torso are offset enough to remove their alignment and posts.

More extreme height/angle produces strongly dominant control; insufficient extremes require weakly dominant controls to keep initiative.

### 4.6 Side Scissor is the primary decision hub (trilemma)
Side Scissor is structured to force a three-way concession:
- concede **height** (back exposure line),
- concede **post/base** (sweep line),
- or recover **elbow position** (which routes you into Clamp).

The point is not a single finish; it’s forcing the opponent to choose a bad option.

### 4.7 Clamp is the default response to elbow recovery
When the opponent “wins” the elbow battle (or prevents Side Scissor dominance), the system converts that defensive action into Clamp-based control and finishing routes. Elbow recovery is treated as a predictable trigger, not a failure.

### 4.8 Weakly dominant controls preserve initiative when extremes aren’t available
When you cannot get strong height/angle, you use controls that still constrain posture and connect to attacks:
- Top Lock
- Trap Triangle
- Elbow Overhook

These are holding patterns with teeth: they exist to re-enter strong dominance or finish.

### 4.9 Posture lost = branch switch (no stubbornness)
If the opponent successfully postures/stands, the system mandates a switch to the lower-body compensation branch:
- leg attacks, and/or
- backward/knockdown sweeps exploiting the opponent’s extended stance and heel-weighting.

### 4.10 Cycling is the anti-stall rule
If a route is defended, you do not “force the finish.” You recycle to the next hub or branch using predefined triggers (height denied → sweep line; elbow recovered → clamp line; posture won by opponent → legs/knockdown line).

### 4.11 Short-athlete adaptation is strategic, not technical
For shorter athletes, the system is expressed as a two-stage plan:
- Stage 1: immobilize and break posture to earn upper-body dominance.
- Stage 2 (if posture cannot be controlled): switch earlier to legs/knockdowns to regain usable height/angle.

### 4.12 Posts are the real currency
A posture battle is largely a post battle. Break posture by removing the ability to post (hands/elbows/feet), or by forcing posts to appear so you can exploit them (off-balance → post → attack the side without a post).

### 4.13 Inside vs outside position is a safety concept, not just an attack concept
When transitioning (especially as the guard opens), prioritize inside positioning and entanglements that prevent the opponent from freely entering leg locks or initiating clean passing sequences.

### 4.14 “Go first” in transitions
If closed guard is being opened or is likely to be opened, initiate your transition proactively. Late transitions are defensive transitions; early transitions preserve initiative.

### 4.15 Every hub needs a defined entry method
A position is only part of the system if you can reliably reach it from common realities: elbow-post frames, wrist control, collar/scoop situations, arm drags, underhooks, and misdirection. If you can’t name the entry, it’s not a system component.

### 4.16 Every hub needs a defined exit method
A hub is incomplete unless it includes:
- a primary conversion (sweep/back/submission), and
- a failure exit (what you do when defended), ideally into a neighboring hub.

### 4.17 Forward vs backward off-balancing is a deliberate choice
Use directionality as a control primitive:
- Forward off-balance tends to force hand posts and creates come-up opportunities.
- Backward off-balance tends to load heels and creates knockdowns/backward sweeps.

### 4.18 Standing posture is not “bad”—it’s a trigger
When the opponent stands, treat it as a signal to shift to the leg/knockdown branch rather than insisting on upper-body dominance that depended on broken posture.

### 4.19 Two-stage strategy (general form)
Independent of body type, the system supports a two-stage plan:
- Stage 1: seek posture break → dominant hub → conversion.
- Stage 2: if posture control is denied → attack legs / knockdown → re-enter dominance.

### 4.20 Constraint before submission
Submissions are downstream of constraint. If the opponent can freely realign posture or recover posts, the submission attempt is premature; return to dominance metrics (height/angle) first.

### 4.21 The elbow line is a recurring gate
Across Side Scissor and related hubs, elbow positioning functions as a gate condition:
- Outside elbow = offensive leverage and angle.
- Inside elbow recovery = clamp entry or reset; treat it as a predictable branch, not “they escaped.”

### 4.22 Immobilize the hips before expecting the upper body to cooperate
Many failures come from attacking the upper body while the opponent’s hips remain free to retreat/stand. Closed guard’s advantage is hip capture; use it to prevent disengagement before committing to upper-body lines.

### 4.23 Minimum goal when the guard opens
If closed guard is opened, the minimum objective is not “keep closed guard”; it is to exit with:
- inside position, and/or
- an entanglement that prevents immediate passing and leg attacks,
so you can re-attack or re-guard from a position of initiative.

### 4.24 Force the opponent to choose between mutually incompatible defenses
Good closed-guard attacks present dilemmas (e.g., posture vs base, elbow position vs height). If the opponent can defend everything with one movement, you are not presenting a real constraint.

### 4.25 “Angle” is hip position, not torso decoration
Angle is created and maintained by hip offset and alignment disruption. If your hips are square under the opponent, you do not have functional angle regardless of upper-body grips.

### 4.26 Strip frames before you attempt dominance
Frames (elbow-knee wedges, stiff arms, collarbone frames) are posture-support. Remove or bypass frames first; otherwise, “dominant positions” are superficial and collapse on contact.

### 4.27 Head control is posture control
When you can control the head/shoulder line, posture becomes expensive to rebuild. When you cannot, assume posture will be rebuilt and plan branches accordingly.

### 4.28 Prefer sequences that end in top position when submissions stall
When the opponent is defensively coherent, prioritize conversions that put you on top (sweeps/knockdowns). Top position is a system output, not a consolation prize.

### 4.29 Build redundancy: every major hub should connect to at least two other hubs
A hub is system-grade only if it has lateral connections (e.g., Hub A → Hub B on defense; Hub A → Hub C on posture reset). This prevents “single-point failure” games.

### 4.30 Re-guarding is a planned outcome, not an emergency
If the opponent breaks closed guard, your response should be a preselected re-guard/entanglement pathway that preserves inside position and denies immediate passing.

### 4.31 Timing principle: attack during posture construction, not after posture is built
The highest-return attacks occur while the opponent is mid-stand, mid-post, or mid-frame insertion. Once posture is established, switch to the branch that punishes that posture.

### 4.32 Hands-to-mat moments are conversion windows
Whenever the opponent is forced to post (forward or backward), treat it as a conversion window: either come up (height) or remove the remaining post (sweep/back exposure).

### 4.33 Finish selection should be constraint-matched
Choose finishes based on what you already control:
- If you have height/angle: prioritize back exposure and high-percentage strangles.
- If you have elbow isolation: prioritize armlocks.
- If posture is won by the opponent: prioritize legs/knockdowns.

## 5.0 System Overview

### 5.1 System definition (what this is)
A closed-guard system organized as a decision tree with one controlling problem (**posture**) and two conversion variables (**height** and **angle**). The output is consistent access to three outcome classes: **sweep**, **back exposure**, **submission**.

### 5.2 Phase model (state machine)
**Phase 1 — Posture battle**
- Goal: force posture compromise and remove posts.
- Output condition: opponent must post, collapses alignment, or cannot safely stand/reset.

**Phase 2 — Dominant position acquisition**
- Goal: achieve height and angle advantage.
- Output condition: strongly dominant hub established (preferred) or weakly dominant hub established (fallback).

**Phase 3 — Conversion**
- Goal: convert dominance into sweep/back/submission.
- Output condition: finish or top position; if blocked, recycle to another hub.

**Branch condition**
- If the opponent successfully postures/stands: switch to lower-body compensation branch.

### 5.3 Dominance taxonomy
**Strongly dominant**
- Defined by extreme height/angle that materially removes posts and alignment.
- Typical result: opponent becomes reactive and posture resets are difficult.

**Weakly dominant**
- Defined by insufficient height/angle for immediate forced reactions but enough control to constrain posture and connect to attacks.
- Typical result: you can still attack, but must be prepared to re-enter strong dominance.

### 5.4 Primary hubs and their roles

**Side Scissor (primary strongly-dominant hub)**
- Role: decision hub that forces a trilemma (height vs post/base vs elbow recovery).
- Conversion: back exposure, sweeps, or clamp-based submissions.

**Clamp (primary submission hub when elbow battle is lost)**
- Role: converts elbow recovery/inside-elbow defense into a controlled finishing environment.
- Conversion: triangle and arm-lock families, plus re-entry options if clamp fails.

**Double Underhooks (posture denial + advancement hub)**
- Role: connects posture break to forward dominance; provides “come-up” mechanics via the slide-by.
- Conversion: sweeps and advancement into top position.

**High Cross (attack lane hub)**
- Role: structured access to armbar/triangle/trimura/omoplata paths.
- Conversion: submission or forced reactions into sweeps/secondary attacks.

**Side Sit-Up / Hip Sweep (sweep hub)**
- Role: creates a reliable sweep engine with two force directions and re-attack structure when the sweep fails.
- Conversion: sweep, arm traps, or return to dominant hubs.

**Top Lock (weakly-dominant hub when angle/height is limited)**
- Role: provides attacks without needing large angle/height; includes standing-opponent handling.
- Conversion: armbars/triangles and transitions when opponent pulls out.

**Trap Triangle (weakly-dominant control hub)**
- Role: clamps upper body with crossed-feet triangle control and routes to triangle variants.
- Conversion: triangle upgrades and transitions.

**Elbow Overhook (weakly-dominant arm-control hub)**
- Role: creates a tight arm-lock fulcrum vs kneeling or standing opponents.
- Conversion: arm locks and transitions.

### 5.5 Failure routing (explicit rules)
These are mandatory decision points, not preferences:

- **If posture breaks →** do not “hunt moves” from neutral; immediately pursue a dominant hub (Side Scissor / Double Underhooks / High Cross / Side Sit-Up).
- **If height is denied in Side Scissor →** shift to the sweep line.
- **If elbow position is recovered (inside elbow) →** enter Clamp.
- **If opponent successfully postures/stands →** switch to the lower-body compensation branch (legs + knockdowns/backward sweeps).
- **If a hub stalls →** recycle to the next hub; avoid dead control.

### 5.6 Lower-body compensation branch (when posture is lost)
When the opponent wins posture (especially standing), upper-body dominance becomes unreliable. The system’s prescribed response:
- attack legs (entries to leg entanglements/heel hooks), and/or
- force knockdowns/backward sweeps by exploiting extended stance and heel-weighting.

### 5.7 Minimal viable closed-guard loop (training baseline)
A practical minimum loop that preserves the system logic:
1) posture break attempt → 
2) Side Scissor entry → trilemma outcome →
3) clamp or sweep or back exposure →
4) if posture resets → switch to legs/knockdown →
5) return to posture battle.

This baseline is the scaffolding for the technique library and drills.

### 5.8 Entry taxonomy (how hubs are reached)
Each hub is treated as reachable from a small set of recurring entry situations:
- **Elbow-post frames** (opponent posts elbow/forearm to build posture)
- **Wrist-control sequences** (opponent posts hands; you control wrists to destabilize)
- **Collar + scoop situations** (head/shoulder line controlled while hips are locked)
- **Arm-drag reactions** (opponent’s posting arm is redirected across the centerline)
- **Underhook access** (arm position allows you to connect to the torso and turn the corner)
- **Misdirectional off-balancing** (fake one direction to force a post, then attack the other)

The report treats these as the “on-ramps” that connect Phase 1 (posture battle) to Phase 2 (dominant positions).

### 5.9 Trigger table (what causes the system to branch)
The system branches on observable triggers:
- **Hands post to prevent collapse** → immediate conversion window (come-up/back exposure or sweep line).
- **Opponent denies height** → shift to no-post/sweep line.
- **Opponent recovers inside elbow** → clamp entry.
- **Opponent stands / hips retreat** → lower-body compensation branch.
- **Guard is being opened** → transition first; preserve inside position/entanglement.

### 5.10 Hub adjacency map (how hubs connect)
The hubs are not isolated; they have designed adjacency:
- Side Scissor ↔ Clamp (elbow recovery routes into clamp; clamp success can re-create angle/height).
- Side Scissor ↔ Sweeps (height denied routes into sweep line).
- Scoop/Knockdown ↔ Side Scissor / High Cross (knockdown outcomes feed back into dominant hubs).
- Double Underhooks ↔ come-up / sweep outcomes (slide-by creates advancement and top-position routes).
- Top Lock / Trap Triangle / Elbow Overhook ↔ re-entry into stronger dominance (weakly dominant hubs stabilize and re-route).

### 5.11 Transition governance (what “safe” means in this system)
When moving from closed guard to open guard (voluntary or forced), the transition is governed by:
- **Go first**: initiate the transition before the opponent completes the opening sequence.
- **Inside position priority**: do not concede inside lines that enable leg entries.
- **Entanglement preference**: use controlled leg/hip connections to prevent immediate passing.

### 5.12 Output standards (definition of success for each phase)
- **Phase 1 success**: posture compromised or posts forced.
- **Phase 2 success**: hub established with repeatable constraints (strongly or weakly dominant).
- **Phase 3 success**: sweep/top position, back exposure, or submission attempt with maintained control.
- **Branch success (posture lost)**: leg engagement or knockdown achieved before the opponent stabilizes passing.

### 5.13 Minimal system compliance (what must be present to call it “the system”)
A practitioner is “running the system” only if they can demonstrate:
- at least **two posture-break methods**,
- at least **one strongly dominant hub** with a defined trilemma/dilemma,
- at least **one weakly dominant hub** for stalled height/angle,
- at least **one posture-lost branch** (legs/knockdowns),
- and at least **one transition safety protocol** when the guard opens.

### 5.14 Common integration error (what breaks the system)
The most frequent failure is treating Phase 2 as optional—attempting submissions directly from a neutral closed guard without first establishing dominance metrics (height/angle) or without forcing posts. This produces “move hunting” instead of a decision tree and typically collapses when opponents posture or stand.


## 6.0 Technique Library

### 6.1 Posture Battle (Phase 1)

#### 6.1.1 Posture-break methods (core set)
- **Elbow-post breakdown**
  - Entry: opponent posts elbow/forearm to build posture.
  - Action: remove the post and pull with legs to collapse alignment.
  - Outcome: hands-to-mat or shoulders/head collapse.
  - Failure exit: switch to misdirection or transition to lower-body branch if opponent stands.

- **Wrist-control breakdown**
  - Entry: opponent posts hands to stabilize posture.
  - Action: control wrists, pull knees, and off-balance to force forward post.
  - Outcome: conversion window into come-up or angle creation.
  - Failure exit: if wrists slip and posture rises, switch branches early (legs/knockdown).

- **Collar + scoop posture collapse**
  - Entry: head/shoulder line accessible.
  - Action: connect to collar/head line while legs lock hips; pull opponent into compromised posture.
  - Outcome: posture collapse enabling dominant hub entry.
  - Failure exit: if opponent retreats hips/stands, transition to scoop/knockdown or leg branch.

---

### 6.2 Side Scissor (Strongly Dominant Hub)

#### 6.2.1 Entries
- **Elbow-post entry**
  - Trigger: elbow posts inside your guard.
  - Goal: establish outside-elbow advantage and start height/angle creation.

- **Arm-drag entry**
  - Trigger: opponent reaches or posts with extended arm.
  - Goal: redirect across centerline to remove post and turn the corner.

- **Underhook entry**
  - Trigger: underhook access appears during posture fight.
  - Goal: connect torso, climb angle, and establish scissor alignment.

- **Misdirectional entry**
  - Trigger: opponent braces against one direction.
  - Goal: fake one direction, force a post, then attack opposite to enter.

#### 6.2.2 Primary battles (invariants)
- **Outside elbow**: maintain a position that prevents the opponent from re-framing across your torso.
- **Height**: win the ability to come up relative to the opponent’s posture.
- **Angle**: keep hips offset so posts become unreliable.

#### 6.2.3 Trilemma outcomes
- **Height win → back exposure line**
  - Indicator: opponent’s posture collapses and you can rise/turn the corner.
  - Output: back exposure/back take pathway.

- **Post/base win → sweep line**
  - Indicator: opponent cannot post effectively on the threatened side.
  - Output: sweep(s) tied to the no-post side.

- **Elbow recovered (inside elbow) → clamp line**
  - Indicator: opponent circles elbow inside to re-frame.
  - Output: immediate clamp entry (treat as a trigger, not a reset).

#### 6.2.4 Failure exits
- If height stalls: route to sweep line.
- If elbow line is lost: route to clamp.
- If opponent postures/stands: route to lower-body compensation branch.

---

### 6.3 Clamp (Submission Hub)

#### 6.3.1 Primary entries
- **From Side Scissor elbow recovery**
  - Trigger: opponent wins elbow battle and frames inside.
  - Goal: convert elbow recovery into clamp structure.

#### 6.3.2 Clamp types
- **Side clamp**
  - Use when you have angle and can keep opponent tilted.

- **Vertical clamp**
  - Use when opponent squares up and you need vertical control to prevent posture.

#### 6.3.3 Primary attacks (families)
- **Triangle family**
- **Armbar family (including inverted variants)**
- **Trimura family**
- **Omoplata family (including roll-over outcomes)**

#### 6.3.4 Failure exits
- If clamp breaks: transition to alternative triangle controls or re-enter Side Scissor via re-angled posture break.
- If opponent postures/stands: switch to leg/knockdown branch.

---

### 6.4 Double Underhooks (Control + Advancement Hub)

#### 6.4.1 Entries
- Elbow-post method
- Wrist-control method
- Flower-style off-balance entry
- Collar & scoop method

#### 6.4.2 Primary action
- **Slide-by**
  - Role: principal advancement mechanic to win angle and come up.
  - Output: sweep/top position routes.

#### 6.4.3 Troubleshooting (common)
- If opponent blocks the slide-by: re-off-balance to re-force posts and retry, or switch hubs (Side Scissor / sit-up).

---

### 6.5 High Cross (Attack Lane Hub)

#### 6.5.1 Entry
- Obtain the high-cross configuration as posture collapses or a posting arm is redirected.

#### 6.5.2 Primary attacks
- High Cross armbar
- High Cross to triangle
- High Cross to trimura
- High Cross omoplata to leg-attack extension (where applicable)

#### 6.5.3 Failure exits
- If posture rebuild begins: switch to posture-break cycle or route to Top Lock (if angle is limited).

---

### 6.6 Side Sit-Up / Hip Sweep (Sweep Hub)

#### 6.6.1 Entries
- Standard entry to side sit-up
- Preferred entry variation
- Two-arm-position variants (context-dependent)

#### 6.6.2 Core action
- **Hip sweep**
  - Includes two force directions depending on opponent base.
  - Arm-trap variations to remove posts.

#### 6.6.3 Failure exits
- If hip sweep fails: follow the defined re-attack structure (re-trap arm, switch direction, or recycle into adjacent hub).

---

### 6.7 Scoop / Knockdown vs Standing (Standing-Posture Response)

#### 6.7.1 Core mechanic
- Scoop/handstand knockdown to force the opponent to the butt or collapse base.

#### 6.7.2 Follow-on attacks
- Hip sweep follow-on
- Triangle follow-on
- Armbar follow-on

#### 6.7.3 Integration
- After knockdown, route back into dominant hubs (Side Scissor / High Cross) rather than lingering in neutral.

---

### 6.8 Top Lock (Weakly Dominant Hub)

#### 6.8.1 Use case
- Applied when you lack usable height/angle but still need an attack-capable control.

#### 6.8.2 Variants
- Top lock armbar
- Overtie top lock
- Standing opponent handling
- Figure-4 top lock

#### 6.8.3 Failure exits
- If opponent pulls out: follow the prescribed retention/transition actions rather than chasing.

---

### 6.9 Trap Triangle (Weakly Dominant Hub)

#### 6.9.1 Core dilemma
- Collar vs wrist dilemma to force predictable reactions.

#### 6.9.2 Transitions
- Trap triangle → figure-4 triangle upgrades
- Short-legged adaptations as needed

---

### 6.10 Elbow Overhook (Weakly Dominant Hub)

#### 6.10.1 Configurations
- Kneeling opponent variant
- Standing opponent variant

#### 6.10.2 Output
- Tight arm-lock fulcrum with direct finishing routes.

---

### 6.11 Lower-Body Compensation Branch (Posture Lost)

#### 6.11.1 Leg-attack entries
- Spin-around leg entry into heel-hook routes
- Transition options into primary leg-entanglement structures

#### 6.11.2 Backward/knockdown sweeps
- Knockdown “formula” concepts to force the opponent to the butt.
- Backward sweep logic when weight is committed to heels.

#### 6.11.3 Re-entry rule
- After leg/knockdown success, re-enter dominant hubs immediately; do not reset to neutral.

### 6.12 Decision Matrix (posture state × your dominance state)

Use this as the primary routing tool.

| Opponent posture state | Your angle/height state | Run this hub/branch | Immediate objective |
|---|---|---|---|
| Posture compromised (hands posting / alignment collapsed) | Angle available | Side Scissor / High Cross | Establish strongly dominant hub, force trilemma/dilemma |
| Posture compromised | Angle not yet available | Double Underhooks / Side Sit-Up | Create angle and come-up pathway; convert to sweep/top |
| Posture stable (kneeling) | Angle available | Side Scissor / Clamp entry threats | Remove posts, isolate elbow line, force elbow recovery |
| Posture stable (kneeling) | Angle limited | Top Lock / Trap Triangle / Elbow Overhook | Create weak dominance with attack-capable control |
| Posture stable + standing | Any | Scoop/Knockdown OR Lower-Body Compensation | Deny clean guard opening: knockdown or legs immediately |
| Guard opening in progress | Any | Transition governance (inside position/entanglement) | Go first, deny pass/legs, preserve initiative |

---

### 6.13 Trigger Index (single-cue routing)

- **Hands forced to mat (forward)** → come-up / height line OR remove remaining post → sweep.
- **Hands post backward / weight to heels** → backward sweep / knockdown line.
- **Elbow circles inside to re-frame** → Clamp entry (do not re-fight the elbow in place).
- **Opponent re-squares hips to kill angle** → switch to weakly dominant hub (Top Lock / Trap Triangle / Elbow Overhook).
- **Opponent stands to open guard** → Scoop/Knockdown or Legs (do not chase upper-body finishes).
- **Guard opening is inevitable** → transition first; secure inside position or entanglement.

---

### 6.14 Hub Standards (what qualifies as “in-system”)

A technique is included in this library only if it satisfies all:
- **Entry is named** (you can reach it from a common posture-fight cue).
- **Controls are explicit** (what must be held to make it work).
- **At least two outputs** (sweep/back/submission) OR one output + one re-entry.
- **Failure exit is defined** (where you go next when defended).

---

### 6.15 Side Scissor — Execution Checklist (invariants + trilemma routing)

#### 6.15.1 Invariants (do not proceed without these)
- Outside elbow advantage is maintained (deny inside re-frame).
- Hips are offset (angle is real; you are not squared).
- You have a height pathway (come-up is mechanically possible).

#### 6.15.2 Trilemma routing (single decision)
- If **height wins** → back exposure line (come up; turn corner; deny re-post).
- If **height is denied** but angle remains → sweep line (attack the no-post side).
- If **elbow recovers inside** → clamp line immediately.

#### 6.15.3 Side Scissor failure exits
- If posture rebuild begins → return to posture-break (6.2) or shift to Top Lock (6.8) if angle collapses.
- If opponent stands → Scoop/Knockdown (6.9.3) or Lower-Body Compensation (6.10).

---

### 6.16 Clamp — Family Selection Rules (choose by opponent defense)

#### 6.16.1 Selection
- Opponent **stacks / postures to break clamp** → prioritize Triangle family.
- Opponent **connects hands / hides elbow** → prioritize Trimura/Omoplata families to punish connection/drive.
- Opponent **gives elbow exposure** → prioritize Armbar family (standard/inverted based on alignment).
- Opponent **backs out** → re-angle to Side Scissor or stabilize to Trap Triangle.

#### 6.16.2 Clamp failure exits (hard rules)
- Clamp opens due to posture rise → posture-break cycle or Top Lock (if angle is gone).
- Clamp opens due to retreat → Trap Triangle or re-entry to Side Scissor.

---

### 6.17 Double Underhooks — Slide-By Routing (what it is “for”)

#### 6.17.1 Purpose
A posture-denial hub whose primary output is **advancement/come-up** via slide-by.

#### 6.17.2 Routing
- Slide-by succeeds → arrive on top (preferred) or into sweep finish.
- Slide-by blocked by frame/base → re-off-balance to force a post, then re-run.
- Opponent retreats hips / stands → shift immediately to Scoop/Knockdown or Legs.

---

### 6.18 Side Sit-Up / Hip Sweep — Two-Force Model (direction selection)

#### 6.18.1 Direction selection
- If opponent is heavy one side → sweep to the light side.
- If opponent is square and posting → remove/trap the post first, then sweep.

#### 6.18.2 Failure tree (do not improvise)
- Post appears → trap/remove post → re-run same direction.
- Post disappears (arm retracts) → switch direction OR transition to Side Scissor / High Cross (based on elbow/angle).

---

### 6.19 High Cross — Output Priorities (attack lane governance)

#### 6.19.1 Primary outputs (ordered)
1) Armbar line when elbow exposure is clear.
2) Triangle line when posture defense is stack-like.
3) Trimura/Omoplata lines when opponent turns/drives to defend the first two.

#### 6.19.2 Failure exits
- If posture rebuild begins → re-enter posture-break (Phase 1) or Top Lock if you lose angle.
- If opponent stands → legs/knockdown branch.

---

### 6.20 Weakly Dominant Hub Selection (when extremes aren’t there)

Choose by what you can reliably hold:
- **Top Lock**: you have attachment but lack angle/height; want direct armlock/triangle pathways.
- **Trap Triangle**: you can impose a collar/wrist dilemma and keep upper-body clamped.
- **Elbow Overhook**: you can secure the overhook line (kneeling or standing) and build a fulcrum.

Hard rule: weak dominance must either **finish** or **re-create angle/height** back to strong dominance.

---

### 6.21 Standing-Posture Response Protocol (scoop/knockdown vs legs)

When the opponent stands:
- If you can maintain close attachment and disrupt base → **Scoop/Knockdown** first (recover dominance metrics).
- If distance increases or posture is too stable → **Lower-Body Compensation** (leg entries) immediately.
Do not chase upper-body submissions that require broken posture.

---

### 6.22 Guard-Opening Safety Protocol (transition governance)

If closed guard is being opened:
- Go first: initiate your transition before they complete the opening.
- Prioritize inside position and/or entanglement.
- Minimum output: a position that denies immediate passing and denies easy leg entries against you.

---

### 6.23 Quality Control (library compliance checks)

Before you keep a technique in your personal system, verify:
- Can you name the entry cue in one sentence?
- Can you list the required controls (2–3 items)?
- Can you state the primary output and one alternative?
- Can you name the failure exit without thinking?

If any answer is “no,” it is not yet system-grade for you.
