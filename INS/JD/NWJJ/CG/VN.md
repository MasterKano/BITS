## VN01 — Volume 1: Posture Battle + Side Scissor Entries

### Volume purpose
- Defines closed guard as a posture-first system with observable gates.
- Introduces Side Scissor mechanics as the first major dominant hub.
- Provides four primary entry families into Side Scissor (elbow post, arm drag, underhook, misdirection).

### Volume routing summary (one-line)
- In closed guard: **force posture-won signals → enter Side Scissor → build angle/height; if posture is lost (especially standing), branch immediately.**

### 1.1 Introduction — 0:00 - 3:26
- Objective
  - Establish this volume as a system: posture battle → dominant hub entry → conversion; with a defined failure branch.
- Primary decision rule
  - Treat closed guard as a **posture-control problem first**. If posture is not degrading, you are not yet in the attacking phase.
- Gates/constraints
  - No “finish-first” sequences from neutral closed guard.
  - If posture cannot be meaningfully degraded and the opponent builds to standing, you must switch branches (standing protocol / legs).
- Common errors / failure signatures
  - Jumping directly to submissions while opponent posture remains stable.
  - Treating a failed posture attempt as “reset” instead of a branch trigger.

---

### 1.2 Advantages and Disadvantages of Closed Guard — 3:26 - 20:16
- Objective
  - Define why closed guard is strategically valuable and where it can fail if used incorrectly.
- Primary decision rule
  - Use closed guard to **control distance and posture**, then convert—do not “camp” without a conversion plan.
- Gates/constraints
  - Closed guard must produce one of:
    - posture-won signals → dominant hub entry, or
    - a proactive transition (“go first”) if the guard is opening.
- Common errors / failure signatures
  - Long static holding without forcing posts or posture collapse.
  - Confusing “holding them in guard” with winning the exchange.

---

### 1.3 The Biggest Theme: Closed Guard is Always a Battle for Posture — 20:16 - 25:03
- Objective
  - Define posture as the controlling battle that governs all closed-guard offense.
- Primary decision rule
  - If the opponent’s head/chest is rising away, your next action must be posture degradation (forward or backward), not finishing attempts.
- Gates/constraints
  - Posture battle precedes dominance; do not skip steps.
  - Accept two posture targets:
    - **Forward collapse** (hands forced to mat / chest connection),
    - **Backward collapse** (hips/butt forced to mat / shoulders drop).
- Common errors / failure signatures
  - Attacking while opponent posture is improving.
  - Treating grips as “progress” when posture has not changed.

---

### 1.4 How to Win Posture + 4 Infallible Signs You’re Winning — 25:03 - 48:38
- Objective
  - Provide observable indicators (“win signals”) that confirm you are winning the posture battle and should advance into dominance.
- Primary decision rule
  - Treat the four signs as **gates**: if you can reliably produce any of them, convert immediately to a dominant hub rather than continuing to hand-fight in place.
- Gates/constraints (the 4 signs)
  - **Sign #1: Hands to the mat (forward or backward).**
    - Gate meaning: the opponent is forced to post; posture is being compromised.
    - Constraint: convert during the posting window—do not pause.
  - **Sign #2: Chest-to-chest contact.**
    - Gate meaning: alignment is collapsed; posture recovery becomes expensive.
    - Constraint: maintain connection long enough to enter a dominant hub.
  - **Sign #3: Hips/butt to the mat.**
    - Gate meaning: you are denying posture-build and destabilizing base.
    - Constraint: treat this as a conversion moment (sweep/advance), not a reset.
  - **Sign #4: Shoulders below hips (notably during stand attempts).**
    - Gate meaning: vertical posture is failing; you are structurally winning posture.
    - Constraint: if they restore shoulders above hips, branch early (standing protocol / legs) rather than insisting on posture-dependent attacks.
- Common errors / failure signatures
  - Claiming posture is “broken” without any of the four signs present.
  - Creating a sign (especially a post) but converting too slowly and letting posture rebuild.
  - Overusing arms to pull posture instead of using legs as the primary engine.
  - Ignoring the “shoulders below hips” gate during stand attempts and losing the timing window.
- Key concepts (operational)
  - Forward/backward off-balancing is the driver: remove posts, then use leg pull to force one of the four gates.

---

### 1.5 Side Scissor — Mechanics — 48:38 - 59:24
- Objective
  - Define Side Scissor as a dominant hub and specify the mechanics that stabilize it and make it productive.
- Primary decision rule
  - Build Side Scissor by securing structure first (elbow relationship + hip position), then pursue height/angle progression.
- Gates/constraints
  - You must “climb” to functional control: hip position and body orientation must prevent easy posture recovery.
  - Do not accept a shallow version where the opponent’s head/shoulders can rise and re-square.
- Common errors / failure signatures
  - Entering the position but failing to improve hip position (opponent quickly postures).
  - Treating the entry as “the move” instead of treating Side Scissor as a hub that must be stabilized.

---

### 1.6 Side Scissor Entry — Elbow Post Method — 59:24 - 1:11:22
- Objective
  - Provide the primary conversion from closed guard into Side Scissor when the opponent’s posting structure makes it available.
- Primary decision rule
  - When you can affect the elbow line and off-balance with legs, use elbow-post entry to secure outside-elbow advantage and begin angle.
- Gates/constraints (sequence gate)
  - Off-balance first (legs), then clear/redirect the elbow line, then establish outside-elbow control, then develop angle into Side Scissor.
  - Entry is incomplete until you are outside the elbow and building the position (not just “pushing an arm”).
- Common errors / failure signatures
  - Attempting elbow post without meaningful off-balance (no displacement).
  - Getting outside elbow briefly then allowing inside-elbow recovery.
  - Stalling after entry instead of building height/angle.

---

### 1.7 Side Scissor Entry — Arm Drag Method — 1:11:22 - 1:19:12
- Objective
  - Provide an alternative entry optimized for when opponent hands are low and elbow posting is less available.
- Primary decision rule
  - Use arm drag preferentially when opponent hand position is low near hips and you can pull across to expose the line.
- Gates/constraints
  - Arm drag must convert immediately into the same Side Scissor stability requirements (outside elbow + angle build).
  - The drag is not the finish; it is the doorway to the hub.
- Common errors / failure signatures
  - Dragging without sufficient connection (arm snaps back; no control).
  - Completing the drag but failing to build angle, allowing re-square and posture recovery.

---

### 1.8 Side Scissor Entry — Underhook Method — 1:19:12 - 1:25:09
- Objective
  - Provide a high-resistance “scrappy” entry when cleaner routes are denied.
- Primary decision rule
  - When clean entries are blocked, accept friction and use underhook-based attachment to create angle and progress.
- Gates/constraints
  - This method requires persistence through messy resistance; do not abandon because it feels less clean.
  - Underhook attachment must still produce angle; without angle you cannot stabilize Side Scissor.
- Common errors / failure signatures
  - Quitting early because the position becomes messy.
  - Achieving attachment but failing to convert it into angle, resulting in a stall.

---

### 1.9 Side Scissor Entry — Misdirectional Method — 1:25:09 +
- Objective
  - Create entry by inducing predictable defensive reactions and taking the exposed line.
- Primary decision rule
  - If the opponent braces to stop your first direction, change direction to force a reaction (post/weight shift), then enter during the window.
- Gates/constraints
  - Misdirection must still end in Side Scissor requirements: outside elbow + angle build + height pathway.
  - The misdirection is successful only if it produces a usable conversion window.
- Common errors / failure signatures
  - “Faking” without producing a real defensive commitment.
  - Creating the reaction but switching back too late (window closes).
  - Entering without securing outside elbow, allowing immediate re-frame.

## VN02 — Volume 2: Side Scissor Trilemma — Back Takes, Sweeps, Armlocks/Strangles

### Volume purpose (what this volume adds)
- Side Scissor becomes the system’s primary hub by formalizing:
  - the three governing battles (elbow / height / angle),
  - the trilemma routing (height win → back exposure; height denied → sweeps; elbow recovered inside → clamp trigger),
  - and the main conversion families (back takes, sweeps, armlocks/strangles).

### Volume routing summary (one-line)
- In Side Scissor: **read the defense → run the matching line** (do not insist on a favorite move).

---

### 2.1 Tactical elements of Side Scissor: elbow, height, angle — 0:00 - 8:36
- Objective
  - Define the three variables that determine Side Scissor quality and what outputs are available.
- Primary decision rule
  - Solve **the limiting variable** first (elbow OR height OR angle) before attempting an output.
- Gates/constraints
  - If elbow is not controlled (inside frame available), outputs are premature.
  - If angle is lost (hips square), the hub is unstable—rebuild angle or switch to weak dominance.
  - If height is denied, stop chasing come-up/back and pivot to sweeps.
- Common errors / failure signatures
  - “Attacking from Side Scissor” without a defined win in any variable.
  - Treating height/angle as optional details rather than the control model.

---

### 2.2 Side Scissor trilemma — 8:36 - 15:25
- Objective
  - Establish Side Scissor as a decision trap that forces predictable concessions.
- Primary decision rule
  - Identify which concession the opponent is making, then take the free line:
  - concede height → back exposure
  - deny height → sweeps
  - recover elbow inside → clamp trigger
- Gates/constraints
  - Trilemma must be real: opponent cannot defend all three with one movement.
  - You must keep pressure on at least two problems at once.
- Common errors / failure signatures
  - Forcing the height line while height is being denied.
  - Missing the elbow-recovery moment and “re-fighting” instead of converting.

---

### 2.3 Best moves when you win height: taking the back — 15:25 - 22:05
- Objective
  - Convert height advantage into back exposure/back take.
- Primary decision rule
  - When height is won, **come up immediately**; do not stay flat and allow posture rebuild.
- Gates/constraints
  - Coming up must deny the opponent’s ability to re-post and re-square.
  - If opponent regains a stable post mid-come-up, switch to sweep line.
- Common errors / failure signatures
  - Pausing after height win (window closes).
  - Coming up without managing posts (opponent stabilizes and resets).

---

### 2.4 Wrist sweep — 22:05 - 26:37
- Objective
  - Provide a sweep conversion keyed to wrist/post removal.
- Primary decision rule
  - Sweep toward the side where the opponent **cannot post**.
- Gates/constraints
  - Wrist control must function as post removal, not just a grip.
  - If the opponent can freely post on the sweep side, reset constraints first.
- Common errors / failure signatures
  - Sweeping into a strong post.
  - Trying to “muscle” rotation without off-balance.

---

### 2.5 Elbow sweep — 26:37 - 29:28
- Objective
  - Convert elbow control into a no-post sweep.
- Primary decision rule
  - If elbow positioning denies posting, rotate them toward the denied-post side.
- Gates/constraints
  - Elbow control must remain intact through the turning moment.
- Common errors / failure signatures
  - Losing elbow control mid-rotation.
  - Starting rotation before the base is compromised.

---

### 2.6 Elbow sweep, part 2 — 29:28 - 34:16
- Objective
  - Troubleshoot common defenses and stabilize reliability.
- Primary decision rule
  - If first attempt fails, change **the constraint** (post removal/base direction), not effort.
- Gates/constraints
  - A second attempt must be preceded by a clear improvement: better post removal, better angle, or better off-balance.
- Common errors / failure signatures
  - Repeating identical attempts into identical defenses.
  - Allowing the opponent to re-square before reattempting.

---

### 2.7 Armbar (Juji Gatame) — 34:16 - 38:57
- Objective
  - Convert elbow exposure into a high-percentage armbar from the hub.
- Primary decision rule
  - Attempt the armbar only when elbow isolation is real; otherwise, return to trilemma pressure.
- Gates/constraints
  - If opponent can reconnect hands or re-frame inside, the armbar is not yet available—recreate the gate first.
- Common errors / failure signatures
  - Chasing the arm while losing elbow/angle control.
  - Turning a hub advantage into a scramble by attacking too early.

---

### 2.8 Rear triangle (Ushiro Sankaku) — 38:57 - 45:19
- Objective
  - Provide a strangle conversion when back exposure and neck alignment are available.

  - If elbow is recovered inside → Clamp → submission family.

### 3.1 Losing the Battle for the Elbow: The Clamp Series - The Clamp — 0:00 - 10:11

  - Head control is a priority: keep opponent’s head down between your knees to prevent posture rebuild.
- Common errors / failure signatures
  - Staying in Side Scissor and repeatedly trying to re-win elbow position while opponent stabilizes and resets.

  - Triangle entries require the opponent’s posture to stay broken: keep the head down and deny the base as you lock.

## VN07 — Volume 7: Weakly-Dominant Attacks + Losing-Posture Protocols (Trap Triangle, Elbow Overhook, Go-First Defense)
