## 1.0 Volume 1

<a id="v1-11"></a>
### 1.1 Introduction
**Purpose:** frame half guard bottom as a system with a single dominant constraint: prevent “debilitating pressure” (flattening) so you can move, connect, and attack.

**Key claims**
- Half guard bottom is viable *because* it allows strong connection and under-CG leverage — but it collapses if you accept flattening pressure.
- Underhook (and related upper-body grips) is positioned as a primary enabling grip for bottom effectiveness.

**Operational takeaway**
- If the opponent’s pressure removes your ability to rotate/move → treat it as an emergency (recover structure first, then attack).

---

<a id="v1-12"></a>
### 1.2 New Wave guard play: 3 key insights
**Core model:** a complete guard must threaten three directions:
- **Submissions**
- **Sweeps**
- **Come-up / stand-up**

**Why it matters**
- Without a real come-up threat, the opponent has one job: pass.
- With a come-up threat, the opponent must: pass **and** keep you down.

---

<a id="v1-13"></a>
### 1.3 Why you must have a strong half guard (3 reasons)
**Reason set (system-level)**
1. **Direct attack viability** from bottom with close-set structure.
2. **Connection advantage**: easier to impose bottom position and control distance.
3. **Kuzushi advantage**: you operate underneath the opponent’s center of gravity, making off-balancing/lifting more available.

**Practical implication**
- Half guard is not “half a guard.” It’s a specialized platform that trades open distance for connection + leverage.

---

<a id="v1-14"></a>
### 1.4 Half guard advantage: connection
**What connection buys you**
- Distance control without relying on flexibility/speed.
- Ability to “impose bottom position” (i.e., make your guard matter) instead of waiting for the opponent’s mistake.

**Failure mode**
- If you lose connection *and* lose structure, you get flattened or the opponent disengages and passes.

---

<a id="v1-15"></a>
### 1.5 Half guard advantage: kuzushi from underneath
**Claim**
- Kuzushi is easier when you are under the opponent’s center of gravity; it becomes dramatically harder when you are separated at distance.

**Practical implication**
- Preference should shift toward entries and grips that place you under the hips/CG instead of reaching from far away.

---

<a id="v1-16"></a>
### 1.6 Half guard is 3-directional
**The three directions (explicit)**
- **Sweep**
- **Submit**
- **Come up** (to knees/feet) → takedown / front headlock / back exposure

**Constraint**
- The come-up threat must be credible, not theoretical — otherwise it doesn’t create strategic pressure.

---

<a id="v1-17"></a>
### 1.7 Halfway to the back, halfway to a takedown
**System idea**
- Half guard positioning naturally shortens the route to:
  - **Back exposure** (angle + hip escape + connection)
  - **Takedown/stand-up** (heist to knees/feet)

**Practical implication**
- When you win the come-up window, you should already know which of the two routes you’re taking based on opponent reaction.

---

<a id="v1-18"></a>
### 1.8 MMA translation
**Why it translates**
- Less dependency on gi-based friction/grips.
- Close-set structure reduces space-management problems that become risky under strikes.

---

<a id="v1-19"></a>
### 1.9 Relationship to pin escapes
**Core link**
- The primary way half guard fails is via **flattening**, which functionally resembles a pin.
- Therefore, half guard competence tracks closely with pin-escape competence (ability to restore mobility/rotation).

---

<a id="v1-110"></a>
### 1.10 Fundamental reality of guard play
**Strategic rule**
- If you can’t threaten to come up, your opponent’s optimization problem is simple.
- If you can come up, they must allocate resources to containment — creating openings.

---

<a id="v1-111"></a>
### 1.11 The half guard paradox
**Paradox**
- Half guard top can be elite passing.
- Half guard bottom can be elite attacking.
- The deciding factor is who wins the key upper-body control + structure battle first.

---

<a id="v1-112"></a>
### 1.12 Resolving the paradox: outside shoulder + outside hip
**Governing law (binary)**
- If your **outside shoulder** or **outside hip** gets pinned (often via crossface + underhook), you trend toward “hell” (flattening → pass).
- If you keep **outside shoulder + outside hip** free via frames/structure, you trend toward “heaven” (mobility → offense).

**Practical test**
- If you cannot rotate and keep those lines off the mat, offense is paused; recovery is mandatory.
