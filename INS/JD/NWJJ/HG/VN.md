## 1.0 Volume 1

<a id="v1-11"></a>
### 1.1 Introduction
**Purpose:** frame half guard bottom as a system with a single dominant constraint: prevent “debilitating pressure” (flattening) so you can move, connect, and attack.

**Key claims**
- Half guard bottom is viable *because* it allows strong connection and under-CG leverage — but it collapses if you accept flattening pressure.
- Underhook (and related upper-body grips) is positioned as a primary enabling grip for bottom effectiveness.

**Operational takeaway**
- If the opponent’s pressure removes your ability to rotate/move → treat it as an emergency (recover structure first, then attack).

---

<a id="v1-12"></a>
### 1.2 New Wave guard play: 3 key insights
**Core model:** a complete guard must threaten three directions:
- **Submissions**
- **Sweeps**
- **Come-up / stand-up**

**Why it matters**
- Without a real come-up threat, the opponent has one job: pass.
- With a come-up threat, the opponent must: pass **and** keep you down.

---

<a id="v1-13"></a>
### 1.3 Why you must have a strong half guard (3 reasons)
**Reason set (system-level)**
1. **Direct attack viability** from bottom with close-set structure.
2. **Connection advantage**: easier to impose bottom position and control distance.
3. **Kuzushi advantage**: you operate underneath the opponent’s center of gravity, making off-balancing/lifting more available.

**Practical implication**
- Half guard is not “half a guard.” It’s a specialized platform that trades open distance for connection + leverage.

---

<a id="v1-14"></a>
### 1.4 Half guard advantage: connection
**What connection buys you**
- Distance control without relying on flexibility/speed.
- Ability to “impose bottom position” (i.e., make your guard matter) instead of waiting for the opponent’s mistake.

**Failure mode**
- If you lose connection *and* lose structure, you get flattened or the opponent disengages and passes.

---

<a id="v1-15"></a>
### 1.5 Half guard advantage: kuzushi from underneath
**Claim**
- Kuzushi is easier when you are under the opponent’s center of gravity; it becomes dramatically harder when you are separated at distance.

**Practical implication**
- Preference should shift toward entries and grips that place you under the hips/CG instead of reaching from far away.

---

<a id="v1-16"></a>
### 1.6 Half guard is 3-directional
**The three directions (explicit)**
- **Sweep**
- **Submit**
- **Come up** (to knees/feet) → takedown / front headlock / back exposure

**Constraint**
- The come-up threat must be credible, not theoretical — otherwise it doesn’t create strategic pressure.

---

<a id="v1-17"></a>
### 1.7 Halfway to the back, halfway to a takedown
**System idea**
- Half guard positioning naturally shortens the route to:
  - **Back exposure** (angle + hip escape + connection)
  - **Takedown/stand-up** (heist to knees/feet)

**Practical implication**
- When you win the come-up window, you should already know which of the two routes you’re taking based on opponent reaction.

---

<a id="v1-18"></a>
### 1.8 MMA translation
**Why it translates**
- Less dependency on gi-based friction/grips.
- Close-set structure reduces space-management problems that become risky under strikes.

---

<a id="v1-19"></a>
### 1.9 Relationship to pin escapes
**Core link**
- The primary way half guard fails is via **flattening**, which functionally resembles a pin.
- Therefore, half guard competence tracks closely with pin-escape competence (ability to restore mobility/rotation).

---

<a id="v1-110"></a>
### 1.10 Fundamental reality of guard play
**Strategic rule**
- If you can’t threaten to come up, your opponent’s optimization problem is simple.
- If you can come up, they must allocate resources to containment — creating openings.

---

<a id="v1-111"></a>
### 1.11 The half guard paradox
**Paradox**
- Half guard top can be elite passing.
- Half guard bottom can be elite attacking.
- The deciding factor is who wins the key upper-body control + structure battle first.

---

<a id="v1-112"></a>
### 1.12 Resolving the paradox: outside shoulder + outside hip
**Governing law (binary)**
- If your **outside shoulder** or **outside hip** gets pinned (often via crossface + underhook), you trend toward “hell” (flattening → pass).
- If you keep **outside shoulder + outside hip** free via frames/structure, you trend toward “heaven” (mobility → offense).

**Practical test**
- If you cannot rotate and keep those lines off the mat, offense is paused; recovery is mandatory.

<a id="vn02-0"></a>
## VN02.0 — Volume Thesis
Volume 2 operationalizes the “iron law” (outside shoulder + outside hip) with three install layers:
1) **Knee shield** to stop chest-to-chest and prevent the outside shoulder getting pinned.
2) **Recovery (half elbow escape)** when the outside shoulder *has* been pinned: win the lower-body battle to regain distance by **pushing**, not pulling.
3) **Frames + kuzushi** as the bridge from survival to offense: frames prevent head control; kuzushi is “proven” when **hands or hips go to the floor**, creating conversion windows.

---

<a id="vn02-1"></a>
## VN02.1 — Knee Shield: prevent chest-to-chest + protect outside shoulder/hip
**Core problem stated:** chest-to-chest + far-side underhook lets top pressure head/shoulder and pin your **outside shoulder**; your hip “tends to follow,” violating the law. Knee shield is built to stop that initial chest-to-chest contact.

### Structure options (explicit)
- **High knee shield vs low knee shield**
  - Both can block chest-to-chest when opponent “bows forward.”
- **Open vs closed knee shield**
  - Low knee shield can be **closed** (feet locked) as a risk-control if opponent is strong at sitting back into leg locks.
  - High knee shield is typically **open** because opponent’s forward pressure reduces sit-back risk.

### Non-negotiable constraint: anti-walk tension
Opponent can “walk” your body so knees face the ceiling and still walk your **hip** to the mat *even without chest-to-chest*.  
You must maintain **mild leg/body tension**—not rigid, not floppy—to prevent being tilted onto outside hip/shoulder.

### Placement rule (high knee shield)
Do not let the knee drift across the opponent’s centerline. Keep it inside shoulder/bicep lane; drifting lets it be pressured down and trapped.

### Offense bridge (why it matters)
Knee shield creates a predictable space lane to pummel to **underhook/tight waist**:
- High shield: move your whole body up (“heart of balance”) to bring ear-to-chest and come around the waist.
- Low shield: often easier to thread underhook because the knee controls the space; opponent’s pummel “means nothing” if they can’t close the space.

---

<a id="vn02-2"></a>
## VN02.2 — Recovery: Half Elbow Escape to re-create push-space
**Trigger condition:** outside shoulder pinned, chest-to-chest; you can’t pull arms in effectively. Danaher frames this as two-front battle: **upper body + lower body**. If you lose upper-body battle, “go downstairs” and win hips.

### Goal
Put your knee into the opponent’s **hip pocket** so you can **push** to regain distance.

### Key sequence (explicit cues)
- Hand to near hip pocket + second hand thumb-post on opposite hip.
- Turn and point your knee to the mat → bring the knee **inside** → knee goes into the **hip pocket**.
- Diagnostic: when your knee is **in front of the hip**, you can now push back as they drive forward.
- Once space exists: “paddle back” under the arm to reclaim underhook; head returns to chest; you’re back to a winning half guard posture.

### Failure mode callout
If you remain in a “pull-only” lower-body state (no knee in front of hip), you cannot manage distance; you need the push.

---

<a id="vn02-3"></a>
## VN02.3 — Arm Frames: cross-frame + head denial
**Context:** if there are no frames and opponent achieves chest-to-chest, effectiveness collapses at equal skill.

### Frame principles installed
- **Frames generally cross the centerline** (not always, but usually).
- **Cross-frame configuration:** one arm controls both shoulders/head (elbow-to-shoulder, hand-to-shoulder).
- **Head denial is mandatory:** do not allow strong crossface; do not even allow collar-tie head control.

### Quality standard (frame durability)
- Do not “stiff arm” with hand only. If opponent swims under your thumb and drives, the frame collapses.
- Keep **elbow forward**, and keep elbow relatively close to your head so forward pressure doesn’t crush it.
- If opponent drives low/down your body line, the frame is more crushable; you want the elbow taking them “over” rather than getting collapsed.

---

<a id="vn02-4"></a>
## VN02.4 — Kuzushi: success criteria + why half guard amplifies it
**Thesis:** half guard makes kuzushi easier than most guards because you are underneath and connected.

### Success criteria (binary)
You know kuzushi is real when:
- **hands** go to the floor (most common), or
- **hips** go to the floor (less common)

This is the “symptom” that the opponent is out of balance; the defensive reactions to recover balance are your conversion window.

---

<a id="vn02-5"></a>
## VN02.5 — Tight Waist Tilt Kuzushi (forces the posting hand)
**Entry:** you’ve managed to get underneath the arm and lock a **tight waist**.
- Opponent must apply a **whizzer**; without it, you can push off your foot, send the arm by, and take the back.

### Mechanism (as described)
- Go down toward the opponent’s shoulder, pull with your arm as your legs turn.
- If opponent doesn’t post/adjust, they fall to their back.
- In practice, the whizzer hand is forced to **post to the mat** in response to the tilt.

### Output
Posting hand to mat = proven kuzushi + opens downstream attacks (later volumes).

---

<a id="vn02-6"></a>
## VN02.6 — Windmill Kuzushi (strip crossface; re-enter frames/grips)
**When it’s needed:** opponent has a strong crossface; they’ve turned hips in and lifted knee off the mat:
- No elbow escape lane (by implication).
- No knee lever available because the knee is not on the floor.

### Setup constraints (explicit)
- You **cannot** do this with triangle legs.
- One leg holds at the opponent’s knee; other foot posts on the floor.
- Arm placement: bicep to opponent’s ear; second arm out.

### Mechanism
- Arms trace a windmill circle:
  - one chops at the knee to prevent basing,
  - the other crossfaces at the temple,
- combine with a bridge to off-balance.
Opponent must base with the only available hand → that hand is forced to the mat.

### Output
Hand forced to mat = proven kuzushi; crossface pressure is relieved; opponent re-enters and you can re-establish preferred frames/grips.

---

<a id="vn02-7"></a>
## VN02.7 — End-state checklist (Vol 2 loop)
1) **Install knee shield** (high/low; open/closed) to stop chest-to-chest and protect outside shoulder/hip.
2) If outside shoulder is pinned: **half elbow escape** to put knee in hip pocket and regain push-distance.
3) **Rebuild frames** (cross-frame + head denial) to prevent crossface consolidation.
4) Apply **kuzushi** until success criteria appear (hands/hips to floor).
5) Use the defensive reaction to enter the next layer (tight waist / stand-up / attacks developed in later volumes).

## VN03.0 — Volume Thesis
Volume 3 installs the **tight waist series** as the highest-percentage half-guard conversion layer:  
1) enter tight waist from **seated** or **supine**,  
2) lock a robust connection (ear-to-chest, outside hook, knee-down, elbow-prop),  
3) upgrade the leg control via **lower leg shift** (ankle as long lever),  
4) run a forced-reaction chain: **roll-through → ankle pick / pass-off / duck-under**,  
5) finish by consolidating **body lock** and converting either to top control/passing or to coming-up finishes.

---

<a id="vn03-1"></a>
## VN03.1 — Getting to Tight Waist (Seated + Supine)
### Seated entry (staggered butterfly)
- Approach with one foot outside / one foot inside.
- Push opponent’s ankle outward to create space, throw foot over to establish outside hook (cosoto hook).
- Pummel arm under armpit; **ear to chest**; hand to far waist/hip.
- **Bring knee down toward floor** (do not leave knee pointing up to ceiling).

### Supine entry (from knee shield)
- Start from high knee-shield shape.
- Use knee shield to off-balance and force a **hand-to-mat** post.
- Follow through to sit up into far waist, then pass foot over the top to outside hook, knee down.

---

<a id="vn03-2"></a>
## VN03.2 — Tight Waist Connection Pillars (4-point checklist)
This volume repeatedly treats these as “pillars” of a robust tight waist position:
1) **Outside hook** around opponent’s leg + **knee facing down to floor** (prevents knee from sliding through to pass).
2) **Tight waist** control of far hip/waist.
3) **Ear-to-chest** (prevents head being pushed down into front headlock/quarter nelson threats).
4) **Elbow prop behind you** (elbow behind = strong base; elbow in front = you topple when pressured).

Operational rule: if you don’t have all four, don’t treat it as a stable “working hub” yet—rebuild.

---

<a id="vn03-3"></a>
## VN03.3 — Lower Leg Shift (the lever upgrade)
### What it is
- From tight waist + outside hook, you come down toward the leg and **punch your bottom leg through** so opponent’s knee goes **to the floor**, and you control **at the ankle** (end of the lever).

### Why it works (key claim)
- Knee-to-floor is “bad” only when top has crossface + shoulder pin (hell).
- When *you* have tight waist and ear-to-chest denying crossface, knee-to-floor becomes “heaven” because you now control the long lever of the leg (ankle → hip) and can corkscrew/rotate the hip even against posting.

### Safety note (explicit)
- Don’t do sudden explosive movements in training; the lever is powerful enough to injure partners.

---

<a id="vn03-4"></a>
## VN03.4 — Tight Waist Roll-Through (primary sweep)
### Objective
Rotate opponent over by combining:
- lower leg shift,
- knee-off-mat creation,
- reverse shrimp → conventional shrimps,
until opponent’s “stomach faces the ceiling” (critical point), then you win the top battle.

### Key mechanical cues
- If opponent’s knee is “heavy” on mat, **pressure with hips** to lift it so your leg can pass through.
- Reverse shrimp: toes/ball of foot to mat → pull hips to your own foot.
- Conventional shrimps: step sequence to keep rotating opponent beyond their basing capacity.

### Robustness clause
- Easiest when opponent keeps whizzer trapped; still works when opponent posts hand early—your goal becomes forcing them down to an **elbow** (critical point), then you come over the top.

### Finish standard
- As you come over the top, upgrade to **body lock** quickly to prevent opponent standing up and to maintain hip control for passing.

---

<a id="vn03-5"></a>
## VN03.5 — Ankle Pick (reaction to early post / resistance)
### Trigger
Opponent recognizes roll-through danger and posts hand for base; you feel them resist by pushing away in the opposite direction.

### Core mechanic
- Reposition from scoop to shoelaces grip.
- Threaten roll-through to force the defensive post, then **reverse direction** and come up to knees.
- Pull the foot up toward your hip to dump them; immediately go over the knee into body lock passing structure.

---

<a id="vn03-6"></a>
## VN03.6 — Pass-Off Ankle Pick (handoff mechanics)
### Key principle
Control at **shoelaces** (end of lever). Holding shin is biomechanically weak against sprawl; holding shoelaces is strong.

### Mechanics
- First hand punches through to shoelaces.
- Second hand drops to where buttocks meet hamstring, then **passes the ankle from one hand to the other** to maintain shoelaces control through the rise.
- Use second pass-off (hand to floor behind, re-grip) to complete dump and re-enter body lock.

---

<a id="vn03-7"></a>
## VN03.7 — Duck-Under (anti-whizzer: ear-to-back rule)
### Trigger
During roll-through attempt, opponent bases with outside arm more and more to stop the turn.

### Key rule (repeated)
When you post and come out: **you must be able to see the back**, then place **ear on the back** (not on the stomach).
- If you come up looking down at the stomach, opponent gets easy whizzer.
- Ear-to-back blocks full whizzer; at worst they get a reverse overback grip (not sufficient to stop you).

### Two variants
1) **Go up to them**: post → come out → ear-to-back + base hand behind you.
2) **Pull them down to you**: keep waist grip and pull; makes whizzer harder and shortens path to ear-to-back.

---

<a id="vn03-8"></a>
## VN03.8 — Body Lock: construction + “unlock to post” rule
### Construction
- Back arm palm down; thumb line reaches toward belly button.
- Shoulder travels behind opponent; lock palm-to-palm / wrist-to-wrist as appropriate.

### Non-negotiable rule
Body lock has a cost: strong whizzer pressure can throw you down if you refuse to post.  
Operational rule: **you are allowed to unlock briefly to post and then re-lock**. Do not treat body lock as a “must never open” commitment.

### Anti-whizzer hip orientation
Turn hips “against” opponent’s hips; facing in makes whizzer stronger against you.

---

<a id="vn03-9"></a>
## VN03.9 — Body Lock Suck-Back (stand + pull-through)
From body lock on knees:
- Stand over opponent (outside foot up first; hips turned correctly).
- **Pull opponent’s body through your legs** (“suck back”).
- Chin/head over shoulder, drive forward into mount/top control.

Best when opponent remains committed on at least one knee; if they stand to disengage, transition to the coming-up rules below.

---

<a id="vn03-10"></a>
## VN03.10 — Coming Up to Feet (head/hips rule; takedown finishes)
### Strategic emphasis
This volume explicitly reinforces “3-direction guard”: sweeps + subs + **stand-up/come-up**. Half guard’s contact makes following a disengaging opponent easier.

### The governing stand-up rule (head/hips relationship)
- If opponent’s **head is down** (forward) → you want your hips **in front** (avoid stepping behind and getting thrown).
- If opponent’s **head is up** (driving you back) → you want your hips **behind** (backward takedown options).

### Primary takedown finishes shown (body lock)
- Shoulder behind hip first (anti-uchi-mata safety).
- Walk hips forward (hip-to-hip contact), then:
  - **knee drop** finish,
  - **lift** finish,
  - **knee sweep** finish.

---

<a id="vn03-11"></a>
## VN03.11 — Front-of-Hips Option (head-forward case)
When you cannot win shoulder-behind-hip and opponent’s head/hips configuration makes stepping behind risky:
- Step aggressively **in front** and circle them down.
- If they keep whizzer, you power-twist to put them to a hip and finish without getting rolled.
- Better opponents often release whizzer and go four-point to avoid points; that yields back exposure routes (push foot out → take back).

---

<a id="vn03-12"></a>
## VN03.12 — Outside Knee Pick
### Entry condition
You come up to knees with tight waist and see opponent’s knee on the mat (typical when they are whizzering and basing).

### Mechanics
- Catch shoelaces/heel.
- Drive hips lower toward opposite hip.
- Pull knee in two stages: (1) bring knee close, (2) elbow over knee.
- Lock hands, put outside leg up, circle down to finish.

If opponent lifts knee early (better whizzer posture), start the capture earlier with a pass-off style control so the leg can’t be raised.

---

<a id="vn03-13"></a>
## VN03.13 — Inside Scoop Sweep (momentum + branches + “Navy”)
### Starting premise
This sweep variant uses **falling body weight** (momentum) rather than pure biomechanical reverse-shrimp mechanics.

### Core mechanics
- From lower leg shift and inside scoop control, come up to knees.
- If opponent whizzers hard, you **fall into them** (not just down), throwing your body under theirs and finishing chest-down with weight over weight.

### Branch A: opponent posts hand to stop the roll
- You reverse direction and come up behind → then immediately switch ear across to the correct side to avoid getting re-whizzered.
- Walk around to back control.

### Branch B: opponent stands up to escape
- Use wrestling “Navy” style finish: hook behind ankle, hand across, drive down.

---

<a id="vn03-14"></a>
## VN03.14 — Limp Arm (whizzer removal)
### Core claim
From tight waist on knees, you’re “halfway to the back.” The only obstacle is the **whizzer**.

### Mechanics (basic form)
- Turn your hand **palm up** (reduces friction).
- Bring elbow **to your own body** first (do not start with elbow far across their body).
- Drop your shoulder **below** their shoulder (“limp shoulder” more than limp arm).
- Slip through to the back; place **correct ear** on the back (wrong ear lets them re-whizzer).

1) installs whizzer-specific solution pathways (circle body lock, rotational finishes, pins),  
2) expands the stand-up layer into an explicit **single leg framework** (backside/frontside + forward heist),

<a id="vn04-1"></a>
## VN04.1 — Tight Waist vs Whizzer: Event Definition + Decision Rule
### Event definition
Opponent overhooks (whizzer) and turns hips to stop your rise and re-square your hips/shoulders.

### Governing decision rule
- **Whizzer installed → you do not continue the same “stand up” line.**
- Treat whizzer as a branch trigger: switch to a whizzer solution that **punishes the overhook commitment** by turning it into either:
  - a rotational finish,
  - a body lock angle,
  - a pinning sequence,
  - or a leg-line solution (outside scoop / single leg).

### Failure signature
- You keep driving upward into the whizzer and stall; energy burn + re-flatten risk increases.

---

<a id="vn04-2"></a>
## VN04.2 — Circle Body Lock (Primary Solution)
### Objective
Convert the whizzer’s hip turn into a path to **body lock control** without losing connection.

### Core idea
- Create a circular angle to take away the whizzer’s stopping power.
- Your hips and head position must rotate so the overhook becomes a liability (they can’t keep hips square and block your rise).

### Output
- Body lock dominance → takedown finish or top-control consolidation.

---

<a id="vn04-3"></a>
## VN04.3 — Uki Goshi (Rotation Finish)
### Trigger
Whizzer defender is committed and their balance is oriented to prevent your upward line.

### Mechanism (high level)
- Use rotational entry: the whizzer’s hip turn supplies the rotation you need.
- Convert the defender’s turning pressure into a throw/hip-rotation finish rather than a stalemate.

### Output
- Immediate top position or pin-ready landing.

---

<a id="vn04-4"></a>
## VN04.4 — Far-Side Walk (Angle + Pin Route)
### Objective
Walk around to the far side to reduce the whizzer’s leverage and improve pinning angles.

### Why it works
- The whizzer is strongest when they square you and keep you in front.
- Far-side walking changes the line of force: their overhook becomes “late” and their hips can’t follow cleanly.

### Output
- Transition into pin sequence, half nelson options, or body lock finishes.

---

<a id="vn04-5"></a>
## VN04.5 — Half Nelson Pin (Consolidation Standard)
### Purpose
Set a “finish quality” standard: whizzer solutions must end in **control**, not just motion.

### Use case
- After creating angle (circle body lock / far-side walk), the half nelson functions as:
  - a pinning tool,
  - a posture-break tool,
  - and a route to stabilize top control.

---

<a id="vn04-6"></a>
## VN04.6 — Outside Scoop (Leg-Line Solution Inside Whizzer Exchange)
### Objective
When torso control is contested by whizzer mechanics, shift emphasis to controlling the **leg line** with an outside scoop.

### Why it matters
- This creates a controlled “secondary lane” when body lock rotation isn’t immediately available.
- The outside scoop can feed single leg or takedown finishing sequences.

<a id="vn04-7"></a>
## VN04.7 — Single Leg vs Tight Waist (Control Philosophies)
### Tight waist (torso-first)
- Primary handle: torso/waist connection.
- Primary benefit: continuous connection through rotation and stand-up.

### Single leg (leg-first)
- Primary handle: isolate the leg and finish with wrestling mechanics.
- Primary benefit: strong finishing model once the leg is cleanly captured.

### Operational rule
Use tight waist to create the conditions for single leg; switch to single leg when torso attachment is being neutralized or when the leg is “given.”

## VN04.8 — Backside Single Leg: Finish Model
### Core goal
Get behind the leg (“backside”), where:
- the opponent’s ability to sprawl is reduced,
- the whizzer becomes less effective,
- and finishing angles are cleaner.

### Finish logic (high level)
- Control the leg and keep posture aligned to prevent them turning you back into frontside defense.
- Finish to top position with control standard (not scramble).

<a id="vn04-9"></a>
## VN04.9 — Backside Single Leg from Half Guard
### Trigger
From half guard stand-up layer, opponent pressure and your rise create a leg-access lane.

### Key constraint
You must build base (heist mechanics) while keeping connection—do not “reach” for the leg and lose structure.

### Output
Backside capture → backside finish model (VN04.8).

<a id="vn04-10"></a>
## VN04.10 — Backside Single Leg Limp Arm (Hand-Fighting Module)
### Purpose
Strip or neutralize obstructing grips during the single leg exchange (including overhooks/whizzer-like interference).

### Output
Leg isolation improves; re-routes back to backside finish model.

---

<a id="vn04-11"></a>
## VN04.11 — Forward Heist Mechanics (Base-Building Bridge)
### What it is
The mechanical bridge from half guard bottom to a stable wrestling base:
- build elbow → knee → feet sequence under connection,
- hips come underneath you before you commit to finishes.

### Why it’s central
It converts “I have a grip” into “I can stand and finish” while preventing stalling.

---

<a id="vn04-12"></a>
## VN04.12 — Frontside Single Leg Finishes from Half Guard
### Use case
Opponent squares up and denies backside angle; you’re forced to finish from the frontside.

### Constraints
- Frontside finishes require tighter alignment and better posture control; otherwise sprawl and down-blocks win.

### Output
Frontside finish pathways to takedown/top control.

---

<a id="vn04-13"></a>
## VN04.13 — Coming Up to Feet (Single Leg Layer)
### Goal
Complete the come-up from half guard with the leg controlled so the opponent can’t disengage and re-pass.

### Standard
- Come up with base first (heist), then finish—avoid scrambling upright without stabilization.

<a id="vn04-14"></a>
## VN04.14 — Hiza Gatame Series (Control/Finish Family)
### Role in the system
Hiza gatame is introduced as:

Controlled takedown-to-pin finishes.

<a id="vn04-15"></a>
## VN04.15 — Rolling Hiza Gatame (Momentum Finish)
### Trigger

### Output
Rotation into top control with hiza gatame control line.

## VN04.16 — Lower Leg Shift + Hiza Gatame Integration
### Integration claim
Lower leg shift principles (from tight waist chain) continue to matter in hiza gatame finishes:

Higher percentage consolidation; less scramble reliance.

<a id="vn05-0"></a>
## VN05.0 — Volume Thesis
Volume 5 formalizes **knee lever** as a repeatable **base-denial engine** from half guard bottom:
- You control the opponent’s **knee line** so they cannot freely post/step to recover balance.
- The purpose is not “a sweep,” but a **forced-reaction machine** that reliably yields:
  - come-up finishes (heist → single),
  - back exposure (arm drag / 2-on-1 post routes),
  - or returns into other hubs (tight waist / butterfly).

Success standard: you create a scramble that is **structurally biased** in your favor because the opponent’s knee line (and thus base) is compromised.

---

<a id="vn05-1"></a>
## VN05.1 — Knee Lever Mechanics (Core Shape)
### Objective
Displace the opponent’s knee line so their hips can’t stabilize over base.

### Non-negotiables (shape)
- **Knee line control > ankle chasing.** The lever is the knee; the ankle is only useful if it serves knee displacement.
- **Your hips participate.** If you try to do it “arms only,” the opponent’s weight remains stable.
- **Your legs clamp / direct.** Your lower body prevents them simply stepping out into a new base.

### Proof you have leverage
- Their knee line becomes light/rotatable or is forced toward the mat.
- Their hands begin to post or their free leg begins to step to rebuild base.

---

<a id="vn05-2"></a>
## VN05.2 — Head Position (Steering + Safety)
### Function
Head position determines:
- direction of displacement (forward/diagonal/rotational),
- whether you can keep outside-shoulder/hip alignment while the scramble starts.

### Operational rule
If your head position allows your shoulder line to be turned (crossface pressure), knee lever becomes chaos. Head position must support **outside alignment** while you displace the knee.

---

<a id="vn05-3"></a>
## VN05.3 — Grip Families (2-on-1, 1-on-1, Scoop)
### Purpose
Grip choice is not stylistic; it’s how you control posts while you displace the knee.

- **2-on-1 wrist control**
  - Highest reliability for **post denial** (you can limit hand-to-mat posting).
- **1-on-1 grips**
  - Used when 2-on-1 is not available; requires better head/hip alignment to keep control under resistance.
- **1-on-1 scoop**
  - Used to “own” the leg line and keep the knee from escaping laterally.

Selection rule: choose the grip that best prevents the opponent’s preferred stabilization reaction.

---

<a id="vn05-4"></a>
## VN05.4 — “You Always Win the Scramble” (Forcing Logic)
### Statement of intent
Knee lever is an engine that creates a scramble where:
- the opponent’s base is compromised first,
- therefore their scramble options are reactive and limited.

### Practical meaning
If you enter the scramble while maintaining:
- outside alignment,
- knee line control,
- and at least one post-denial handle,
you are not “scrambling”; you’re converting a forced reaction.

Failure signature: you lose knee line control first → opponent’s base returns → scramble becomes neutral.

---

<a id="vn05-5"></a>
## VN05.5 — Lat Post + Knee-to-Mat (Post Denial)
### Goal
Reduce the opponent’s ability to stabilize with posts by:
- controlling the limb that wants to post (lat post / elbow line logic),
- and driving the knee line toward the mat.

### Output
When the knee line is forced down, the opponent must:
- widen base awkwardly,
- post with a different limb,
- or turn hips to square.
Each is a **predictable** conversion trigger.

---

<a id="vn05-6"></a>
## VN05.6 — Elbow Lever (Secondary Lever)
### Role
Elbow lever acts as a secondary lever when:
- wrist control is stripped,
- or the opponent posts in a way that exposes elbow structure.

### Output
Elbow control helps you:
- redirect posting,
- reopen knee displacement,
- or link directly into back exposure (via post removal).

---

<a id="vn05-7"></a>
## VN05.7 — Fail-Chain Links (Heist / Single / Arm Drag / Back)
### Principle
Knee lever is designed with **planned failures**:
if the primary displacement doesn’t finish, it should *immediately* feed a higher-percentage conversion.

Primary links installed:
- **Forward heist → single leg**
  - When opponent steps to recover base, you come up into their leg.
- **Arm drag from failed knee lever**
  - When their posting arm becomes committed/extended, drag to angle and take the back line.
- **2-on-1 elbow post knee lever → back**
  - Post denial creates the lane for back exposure control.

Rule: never “reset to nothing.” A failed knee lever should fall into a defined link.

---

<a id="vn05-8"></a>
## VN05.8 — Knee Lever as Entry to Tight Waist + Butterfly Hook
### Hub interoperability
- **Knee lever → tight waist**
  - If knee displacement forces torso rotation/connection, you can capture tight waist and enter the Volume 3/4 stand-up layer.
- **Knee lever → butterfly hook**
  - If the leg line opens and you can insert hook safely, you can transition to butterfly-based elevation pathways.

Decision rule: choose the hub you can stabilize *first* (tight waist connection vs hook insertion), not the one you “prefer.”

---

<a id="vn05-9"></a>
## VN05.9 — Operational Checklist (Vol 5 loop)
1) Establish outside alignment and identify knee line exposure.  
2) Enter knee lever with a grip that denies the most likely post (prefer 2-on-1 when available).  
3) Displace knee line until you force a reaction (post/step/turn).  
4) Convert immediately:
   - step → forward heist → single,
   - post → arm drag / back exposure,
   - torso rotation → tight waist entry,
   - leg line opens → butterfly insertion.  
5) If control degrades, return to a hub (structure / tight waist / recovery) rather than chase a neutral scramble.

- you can predict and “catch” their base rebuild (step/backstep) rather than chasing.

- and into leg positions when the opponent’s weight and base reactions allow hook insertion.

### Output
Hook insertion can feed:
- cross-ashi routes,
- stand-up links,
- or re-entry into scoops/spins depending on reaction.
