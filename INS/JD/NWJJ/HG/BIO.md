## 2.0 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

### System Definition
This series treats no-gi half guard bottom as a **forced-transition system**: use structure and connection to prevent flattening, apply kuzushi from underneath, then convert reactions into predictable exits—**stand-up**, **back exposure**, **top position**, or **leg entanglements**.

### Governing Law
- **Maintain outside shoulder + outside hip.**
  - When you have it: half guard is a stable attacking hub.
  - When you lose it (crossface + hip collapse): you are being converted into a pin; offense pauses until you recover alignment.

### Primary Problem the System Solves
- Half guard’s paradox: **high connection = high offensive potential**, but also **high risk of flattening**.
- Solution: frames + knee shield + head/shoulder alignment to keep the outside relationship while you build attachment and off-balance.

### Core Hubs (Repeatable Control States)
- **Knee shield + arm frames**: default structure; denies crossface/flattening and preserves working distance.
- **Recovery hub**: **half-elbow escape + re-framing** to restore the outside shoulder/hip condition.
- **Stand-up conversion hub**: **tight waist / body lock / single leg**, including specific answers to the **whizzer**.
- **Knee lever hub**: a dedicated kuzushi mechanism that creates scrambles you are structured to win.
- **Leg-lock branches**
  - **Knee shield → scoop elevation** (far-leg attacks, movement control, spins, kani basami, ankle-pick → irimi ashi, half-butterfly links).
  - **Deep half → inside sankaku / 50-50**, including knee-lever/outside-spin entries and counter-trees.

### Engine: Kuzushi Toolkit (What Forces Reactions)
- Scissor, bump, scoop, scorpion/half-butterfly elevators, knee lever, tight-waist tilt, windmill.
- Purpose: force posting/stepping/turning so transitions become **forced** rather than opportunistic.

### Win Conditions (End-States)
1. **Stand-up to finish** (takedown completion from tight waist / single leg / body lock).
2. **Back exposure / back take** (often via knee lever, arm drag, heist links).
3. **Sweep-to-top that lands into control/pins** (not just “resetting to open guard”).
4. **Controlled leg entanglement** (inside sankaku / 50-50 variants with structure).

### Primary Failure Condition
- Allowing **crossface + hip collapse → flattening**, then attempting offense without first recovering structure and outside alignment.

### Closed Loop (How It’s Used)
**Structure → Connection → Kuzushi → Conversion (stand/back/top/legs)**  
If the opponent breaks structure: **Recover (elbow escape + frames) → re-enter loop**.

## 4.0 CORE CONCEPTS

### 4.1 The Half Guard Paradox
Half guard is simultaneously:
- **Offensively rich** because it creates *tight connection* to the opponent’s hips/center of gravity (high-quality kuzushi from underneath).
- **Defensively fragile** because the opponent can convert that same connection into **flattening pressure** (crossface + hip collapse) that turns half guard into a pin.

**System implication:** you do not “play half guard” as a static guard; you use it as a conversion platform that either (a) attacks under the governing law or (b) immediately switches to recovery when the law is violated.

---

### 4.2 Governing Law: Outside Shoulder + Outside Hip
**Definition (functional):** your torso and hips must align such that you can reliably win the *outside lane* with your shoulder line and hip line—preventing the opponent from stapling your shoulders flat and collapsing your hips.

**Practical tests**
- You can keep your head/shoulder line from being pinned and you can turn onto your side.
- Your frames are structurally meaningful (not “bent arms” absorbing weight).
- Your knee shield / inside-leg positioning can be maintained without being folded and flattened.

**Failure signature**
- Crossface establishes and your near-side shoulder is pinned.
- Opponent’s hip line dominates your hip line (you are square/flat).
- Your frames collapse and you cannot rotate to your side → half guard becomes a pin.

---

### 4.3 Structure Before Movement
**Rule:** build a stable structure (frames + knee shield + head/shoulder alignment) *before* you attempt elevation, entries, or stand-ups.

**Why:** movement without structure typically gives the opponent the angles they need to consolidate crossface/flattening and pass.

---

### 4.4 Frames: Purpose and Quality
Frames are not “pushing.” They are **alignment tools** that:
- Route pressure away from your chest/face line,
- Preserve shoulder/hip outside alignment,
- Create the space needed to reconnect and off-balance.

**Quality criteria**
- Bone alignment (stacked joints), not muscular resistance.
- Elbows and shoulders positioned so the opponent cannot collapse you into your back.
- Frames must support *rotation* (getting onto your side), not just distance.

---

### 4.5 Knee Shield as the Default Structural Layer
Knee shield is treated as the primary “working structure” that:
- Blocks chest-to-chest pressure,
- Prevents easy crossface consolidation,
- Creates a predictable lane for pummeling, underhooks/attachments, and kuzushi setups.

**Core priorities**
- Knee shield integrity is maintained until you are ready to convert (kuzushi → stand-up/back/legs).
- If the knee shield is beaten and you are being flattened, you transition to recovery protocols rather than forcing offense.

---

### 4.6 Connection: The Advantage Half Guard Offers
Half guard is valued because it permits **greater connection** than many guards:
- You can attach directly to the opponent’s hips/leg line.
- You can operate under their center of gravity (better kuzushi potential).
- You can steer their base because you are already “inside” their structure.

**Rule:** connection is only useful if it is maintained under outside shoulder/hip alignment.

---

### 4.7 Kuzushi as the Engine (Not an Accessory)
**Definition:** kuzushi is deliberate off-balancing that forces the opponent to **post, step, widen base, or rotate**—creating predictable openings.

**System role**
- Kuzushi is what converts half guard from “holding” into “forcing.”
- The series’ kuzushi toolkit exists to generate reactions that feed:
  - Stand-up conversion (tight waist / body lock / single leg),
  - Back exposure (arm-drag/heist links),
  - Sweeps to top control,
  - Leg entanglement entries.

---

### 4.8 The 3-Directional Nature of Half Guard
Half guard is treated as a **3-directional guard**, meaning it can threaten and transition in three primary directions:
- **Forward** into stand-up/takedown finishes (tight waist / single leg / body lock).
- **Around** into back exposure (drag/angle changes, scramble wins).
- **Under/through** into leg entanglements and elevation-based sweeps.

**Rule:** choose direction based on opponent reaction to kuzushi and their pressure strategy.

---

### 4.9 Half Guard as a Bridge: Back and Takedown Proximity
Half guard is “halfway” to:
- **The back** (angle + hip escape + connection creates back exposure pathways),
- **A takedown** (tight waist / single leg / body lock conversions).

**Implication:** stand-up conversion is not “optional”; it is a primary system endpoint.

---

### 4.10 Pin-Escape Relationship
Half guard bottom is tightly linked to pin escapes because:
- The main loss condition is being flattened (a pin-like state),
- The main recovery is built from elbow-escape mechanics and re-framing.

**Rule:** if you cannot maintain outside shoulder/hip alignment, you are in a pin-escape problem and must behave accordingly.

---

### 4.11 Conversion Loop (Operational Algorithm)
1. **Establish structure** (knee shield + frames + side orientation).
2. **Build connection** (attach to hips/leg line without giving crossface).
3. **Apply kuzushi** (force posting/stepping/turning).
4. **Convert** to an end-state:
   - Stand-up finish,
   - Back exposure,
   - Sweep-to-pin,
   - Controlled leg entanglement.
5. If structure fails: **recover** (half-elbow escape + re-framing) → re-enter the loop.

---

### 4.12 Primary Failure Pattern (Concept-Level)
The dominant conceptual failure is:
- Treating half guard as a place to “fight hard” while flattened, rather than a system that requires **outside shoulder/hip** as the precondition for offense.

**Corrective heuristic:** offense is conditional; recovery is mandatory when the law is violated.

### 4.13 Head Position as a Steering Wheel
Head position is treated as a primary steering mechanism for both:
- **Knee lever / kuzushi quality**, and
- **Stand-up conversions** (tight waist / single leg / body lock).

**Functional rule:** your head should support outside shoulder alignment (preventing the opponent from folding you) and should be placed to amplify the direction of your intended kuzushi (forward, diagonal, or rotational). Poor head position typically produces “dead” kuzushi and invites flattening.

---

### 4.14 Inside Leg Control vs Outside Alignment (Two Separate Jobs)
Half guard bottom requires solving two different tasks simultaneously:
1. **Inside leg control** (your entangling leg and clamps/hooks prevent the opponent from freely stepping out).
2. **Outside alignment** (outside shoulder + hip denies crossface/flattening).

**Common error:** over-committing to leg clamp while losing outside alignment, resulting in a strong “leg trap” but a flattened torso.

**Heuristic:** if you must choose, prioritize *outside alignment* first, then rebuild inside-leg control from a position you can rotate and frame.

---

### 4.15 Posture, Posts, and Base: What Kuzushi Must Break
Kuzushi in this system targets one of three stability elements:
- **Posture** (torso angle / head over hips),
- **Posts** (hands, elbows, head, knees used to prevent tipping),
- **Base** (foot/knee spacing, stance width, and hip placement).

**Rule:** do not “off-balance in general.” Identify which stability element is easiest to compromise given your current attachments.

---

### 4.16 Reaction Farming (Predictable Responses)
The kuzushi toolkit is valuable because it reliably forces a small set of reactions:
- **Post with the near hand**
- **Widen base**
- **Step the trapped leg back or forward**
- **Turn hips to square up**
- **Drive crossface to flatten**
- **Whizzer to prevent stand-up/body lock**

**Operational rule:** you are not choosing moves at random; you are choosing kuzushi that predictably causes the reaction that unlocks your next hub.

---

### 4.17 The “Flattening Cascade” (Early Warning System)
Flattening rarely happens instantly; it cascades:
1. Knee shield collapses or becomes mis-angled.
2. Frames bend (arms no longer skeletal).
3. Head/shoulder line gets turned away (crossface begins to win).
4. Hip line gets squared (you lose the ability to stay on your side).
5. Opponent consolidates chest-to-chest and starts passing.

**Rule:** intervene at step 1–2. Once step 3–4 occurs, you are in recovery mode, not offense mode.

---

### 4.18 Recovery Priority: Rotation First, Distance Second
A key conceptual distinction:
- Creating distance without rotation often lets the opponent re-enter and flatten you again.
- **Rotation onto your side** is the precondition for any meaningful re-guarding and re-framing.

**Recovery rule:** restore side orientation (elbow escape + frames) first; then rebuild knee shield and attachments.

---

### 4.19 Direction Selection: When to Stand, When to Go Under, When to Go Around
Half guard offers three directional outcomes; selection should be reaction-based:

- **Stand-up direction** (tight waist/single leg/body lock) is favored when:
  - You can come to your elbow/knee line cleanly, or
  - The opponent’s pressure is forward-heavy and gives you “under-connection” to rise.

- **Back exposure direction** is favored when:
  - The opponent posts hard to stop kuzushi, or
  - Their hips turn to square up, creating an angle for drag/heist/back exposure.

- **Leg-entanglement direction** is favored when:
  - Opponent’s base is compromised (knee/foot available) and
  - You can enter without sacrificing outside alignment or giving an easy pass.

**Rule:** direction is a response to opponent structure, not personal preference.

---

### 4.20 The “Hub” Concept (Why the Series Feels Coherent)
A **hub** is a repeatable control state you can reliably reach and reset to:
- Knee shield + frames (structure hub)
- Half-elbow escape (recovery hub)
- Tight waist (stand-up hub)
- Whizzer resolution positions (counter-hub)
- Knee lever (scramble engine hub)
- Scoop elevation (leg-lock access hub)
- Deep half (underneath access hub)

**Rule:** technique choice is secondary; the priority is reaching a hub you can reproduce under resistance.

---

### 4.21 Finish Quality Standard: Convert to Control, Not Just Motion
A defining theme is conversion to end-states **with control**:
- Sweeps should land into pins/pressure, not simply “top but loose.”
- Stand-ups should progress to finishes (takedown completion), not stalling upright.
- Back exposure should lead to harnessing and control, not a momentary angle.
- Leg entries should be controlled entanglements, not “diving” that concedes passing.

**Heuristic:** if your conversion produces a 50/50 athletic scramble with no structure, you skipped the hub logic.

---

### 4.22 Counter-Logic as Part of the System (Not an Appendix)
Key counters are treated as structural events:
- Crossface/flattening attempts (trigger recovery)
- Whizzer defense (trigger whizzer solution branch)
- Sit-out / thumb-post style defenses in leg-lock transitions (trigger counter-tree)

**Rule:** counters are not exceptions—they are predictable system checkpoints.

---

### 4.23 Integration Principle: Same Attachments Feed Multiple End-States
The series repeatedly uses the same attachments (e.g., tight waist, scoop grip, 2-on-1 control) to access different outcomes:
- Stand-up finishes
- Sweeps
- Back exposure
- Leg entries

**Rule:** learn attachments as “connectors.” Once the connector is stable, outcomes become modular.

---

### 4.24 Minimum Viable Half Guard (MVHG)
If you strip the system to essentials, functional half guard bottom requires:
- A way to **stay on your side** (outside shoulder/hip alignment)
- A **frame set** that does not collapse
- A **leg structure** (often knee shield) that denies chest-to-chest
- A **kuzushi method** that forces a reaction
- A **conversion** plan (stand/back/top/legs)
- A **recovery** plan when alignment is lost

This MVHG is the conceptual checklist for diagnosing why half guard “doesn’t work” in live rounds.

### 4.25 Kuzushi Families (High-Level Definitions)
The system uses multiple kuzushi families. Each is defined by *how* it breaks base (direction + lever).

- **Scissor Kuzushi**
  - Breaks base by “cutting” the opponent’s stance line using opposing leg forces.
  - Typical reaction: widen base or post to prevent tipping.

- **Bump Kuzushi (Half Butterfly Bump)**
  - Breaks base by a short, upward-and-forward impulse that lifts or displaces the opponent’s center.
  - Typical reaction: hands post; hips shift back to re-stack posture.

- **Scoop Kuzushi**
  - Breaks base by lifting or “scooping” the opponent’s far-side leg/hip line so their stance cannot stabilize.
  - Typical reaction: step out, turn hips, or post to stop the elevation.

- **Elevator Kuzushi (Scorpion / Half-Butterfly Elevator)**
  - Breaks base via a lifting hook that elevates one side of the opponent while you steer the torso.
  - Typical reaction: counter-hooking, widening knees, or dropping hips to kill lift.

- **Knee Lever Kuzushi**
  - Breaks base by controlling and displacing the opponent’s knee line so their ability to post/step is removed.
  - Typical reaction: posting with arms, turning hips to square, or stepping the free leg to create a new base.

- **Tight Waist Tilt Kuzushi**
  - Breaks base by rotating the opponent’s torso around a tight waist attachment, forcing weight transfer off the trapped leg.
  - Typical reaction: whizzer/hip turn to prevent you coming up.

- **Windmill Kuzushi**
  - Breaks base by rotational force: you induce a “wheel” effect where posts must chase the rotation.
  - Typical reaction: scramble posting sequence; opponent tries to re-center hips and head.

**Concept rule:** each family is chosen to force a *predictable defensive reaction* that unlocks a hub (stand-up, back exposure, or legs).

---

### 4.26 The “Knee Line” Principle
Half guard is not only a torso battle; it is a knee-line battle.
- If you can control the opponent’s knee line (via clamp, scoop, lever, or tilt), you can **deny stable base** and force posts.
- If the opponent controls *your* knee line (pinning it, stapling it, or walking it down), they can **flatten and pass**.

**Diagnostic:** if sweeps fail repeatedly, you likely lack knee-line control more than “strength.”

---

### 4.27 Attachment Hierarchy (What Matters Most)
Attachments are ranked by their ability to produce *forced* outcomes:
1. **Tight waist / torso attachment** (high conversion to stand-up and sweeps)
2. **Leg-line attachments** (scoop grips, knee line control)
3. **Arm control attachments** (2-on-1 and posting hand capture)
4. **Pure frames without attachment** (necessary for survival but low forcing power)

**Rule:** frames buy time; attachments create outcomes.

---

### 4.28 Tight Waist as a Control Philosophy
“Tight waist” is not merely a grip; it’s a control philosophy:
- You attach to the torso so the opponent cannot freely turn hips away or disengage.
- You convert underneath connection into a *get-up* pathway that does not require separating.

**Key property:** it preserves connection while you move, which is why it dominates the stand-up layer.

---

### 4.29 The Whizzer as a System Event
The whizzer is treated as a structural event that changes incentives:
- It is the opponent’s primary mechanism to prevent you coming up and to re-square hips.
- It often appears precisely when your tight waist / stand-up conversion begins working.

**Concept rule:** when the whizzer appears, you do not “keep doing the same stand-up.” You switch to the whizzer solution branch (re-angle, circle body lock logic, or alternate finishes) so the whizzer becomes a liability rather than a stopper.

---

### 4.30 Single Leg vs Tight Waist (Why Both Exist)
- **Tight waist** emphasizes torso attachment and rotational control (excellent for sweeping and connected stand-ups).
- **Single leg** emphasizes leg isolation and finish mechanics (excellent when you can get to the leg cleanly and the opponent’s torso attachment is contested).

**Rule:** the system treats them as complementary: tight waist often *creates* the conditions where single leg finishes become easy.

---

### 4.31 The Elbow Escape Family as “Alignment Restoration”
Elbow escape is not a generic escape; in this system it is the *primary method to restore the governing law*:
- Re-establish side orientation,
- Rebuild frame integrity,
- Recreate knee shield or equivalent structure,
- Re-enter the loop (structure → connection → kuzushi → conversion).

**Rule:** if you cannot rotate to your side, you are not “in half guard”; you are in a pin-escape problem.

---

### 4.32 Ude Gatame as a Control-to-Submission Connector
Ude gatame is treated as a connector that converts:
- **Control of an arm/shoulder line** into
- A submission pathway (reverse armbar / triangle / kimura / juji) and
- A mechanism to prevent the opponent from freely driving crossface pressure.

**Concept rule:** it is both offensive and protective: it can interrupt flattening by turning the opponent’s pressure into an arm-structure liability.

---

### 4.33 The Scorpion Concept (Leg Configuration as Mobility)
The scorpion family is treated as a leg configuration that:
- Creates elevation or mobility without sacrificing torso alignment,
- Enables positional advancement (including passing/pinning pathways) off half-guard dynamics.

**Rule:** treat scorpion as a *mobility and angle tool*, not just a “move.”

---

### 4.34 Scoop Elevator as a Leg-Lock Access Hub
Scoop elevation is treated as a hub because it provides:
- A repeatable way to get under and elevate the opponent’s leg line,
- Movement control (opponent can’t simply step away),
- Predictable transitions to spinning entries, far-leg attacks, and kani basami-style transitions.

**Concept rule:** leg locks are not entered by diving; they are entered by first **owning the opponent’s movement** through scoop-based control.

---

### 4.35 Deep Half as an “Underneath Route” With a Cost
Deep half provides a powerful underneath route to leg entanglements and rotational entries, but it carries a cost:
- If you go deep without outside alignment and without control of posts, you risk being collapsed and passed.

**Rule:** deep half is activated when posture/posts have been compromised, not as a default entry under heavy flattening pressure.

---

### 4.36 “Leg Locks as Centerpiece” (Interpretation)
When the series frames leg locks as a centerpiece of half guard, the claim is not “always attack legs.”
It means:
- Half guard reliably exposes legs once kuzushi compromises base,
- Leg entanglements provide high-leverage finishing and control outcomes,
- The leg-lock branches are structured endpoints of the same conversion loop—not separate games.

---

### 4.37 Age/Athleticism Constraint (System Design Feature)
The knee-lever/outside-spin framing for less athletic/older athletes signals a design goal:
- Minimize reliance on speed/explosiveness,
- Maximize reliance on alignment, levers, and predictable reactions.

**Rule:** preference is given to mechanics that remain reliable under fatigue and over time.

---

### 4.38 Decision Gates (Simple If/Then Rules)
- **If** outside shoulder/hip is intact → apply kuzushi and convert.
- **If** knee shield is failing but frames remain → rebuild knee shield/structure.
- **If** crossface + hip collapse begins → switch to elbow escape recovery.
- **If** tight waist stand-up meets whizzer → enter whizzer solution branch.
- **If** base is compromised and leg line is owned → enter scoop/deep-half leg-lock branches.
- **If** conversion produces uncontrolled scramble → return to a hub (tight waist, knee lever, knee shield) rather than chase.

## 5.0 SYSTEM OVERVIEW

### 5.1 System Thesis (One Sentence)
Half guard bottom is a **structure-led, kuzushi-driven conversion loop** governed by outside shoulder/hip alignment, whose purpose is to force transitions to **stand-up**, **back exposure**, **top control**, or **leg entanglements**.

---

### 5.2 The Default Operating Loop (Live-Round Algorithm)
**Phase 1 — Structure**
- Establish side orientation with **knee shield + frames** so crossface/flattening cannot be consolidated.
- Maintain the governing condition: **outside shoulder + outside hip**.

**Phase 2 — Connection**
- Upgrade from “blocking” to “attaching” (torso attachment, knee line control, or posting-hand control).
- Connection is the prerequisite for high-quality kuzushi.

**Phase 3 — Kuzushi**
- Apply the kuzushi family that best targets the opponent’s current stability element:
  - posture, posts, or base (especially knee line).
- Purpose: force predictable reactions (post/step/turn/widen base).

**Phase 4 — Conversion (Choose an End-State)**
- Convert the reaction into one of four controlled endpoints:
  1. **Stand-up finish** (tight waist / body lock / single leg completion)
  2. **Back exposure/back take** (arm-drag/heist links, scramble wins)
  3. **Sweep-to-pin** (top position with control, not reset)
  4. **Leg entanglement** (scoop/deep-half access into inside sankaku / 50-50 variants)

**Phase 5 — Recovery (When the Law Is Violated)**
- If crossface + hip collapse begins to flatten you:
  - switch immediately to **half-elbow escape + re-framing**
  - rebuild knee shield and side orientation
  - re-enter Phase 2 (connection) once alignment is restored

---

### 5.3 System Hubs (Repeatable Control States)
The system is organized around hubs that you can reliably return to:

- **Structure Hub:** knee shield + arm frames (default)
- **Recovery Hub:** half-elbow escape + re-framing (when flattened threat appears)
- **Stand-Up Hub:** tight waist → body lock/single leg (primary conversion layer)
- **Whizzer Event Hub:** whizzer solutions (forced branch when opponent defends stand-up)
- **Scramble Engine Hub:** knee lever (reliable destabilization + conversion links)
- **Leg-Lock Access Hub A:** knee shield → scoop elevation (movement control → entries)
- **Leg-Lock Access Hub B:** deep half → inside sankaku / 50-50 (underneath route + counters)

**Connector principle:** the same attachments (tight waist, scoop grip, 2-on-1) connect multiple hubs and endpoints.

---

### 5.4 Volume-to-System Assembly Map (What Each Volume Builds)

- **Volume 1 — Governing Logic**
  - Defines half guard’s paradox and installs the governing law: **outside shoulder/hip**.
  - Establishes why half guard is connection-rich and inherently conversion-oriented.

- **Volume 2 — Structural Layer + Kuzushi Toolkit**
  - Builds the default structure (knee shield, frames, half-elbow escape).
  - Installs the core off-balancing families used throughout the series.

- **Volume 3 — Stand-Up Conversion I (Tight Waist Chain)**
  - Introduces tight waist as the primary stand-up/sweep bridge.
  - Builds the initial “get-up to finish” chain (ankle pick, duck-under, body lock stand-up).

- **Volume 4 — Stand-Up Conversion II + Counter Integration**
  - Treats the **whizzer** as the main defensive event and provides solutions.
  - Expands the stand-up layer into explicit single-leg finish mechanics and adds hiza gatame integration.

- **Volume 5 — Scramble Engine (Knee Lever)**
  - Formalizes knee lever as a repeatable destabilization tool.

### 5.5 Priority Stack (What the System Optimizes For)
1. **Do not get flattened** (outside shoulder/hip + frames + knee shield).
2. **Attach before you move** (connection creates forcing power).
3. **Kuzushi to force reaction** (reaction unlocks conversion).
4. **Convert to a controlled end-state** (stand/back/top/legs).
5. **If structure fails, recover immediately** (elbow escape + re-frame → restart loop).

### 5.7 Default Gameplan (If You Only Remember One Plan)
1) **Establish structure** (knee shield + frames + side orientation).  
2) **Win outside shoulder/hip** (deny crossface + hip collapse).  
3) **Attach** (tight waist / knee line / 2-on-1 post control).  
4) **Kuzushi** (force a post/step/turn).  
5) **Convert** to one end-state with control: stand-up, back, top-pin, or legs.  
6) **If flattened threat appears**: stop attacking → elbow-escape recovery → rebuild structure.

### 5.8 Opponent Archetypes (How the System Adapts)

**A) Crossface Pressure Passer (Chest-to-chest + head control)**
- Their win condition: flatten you, kill knee shield, consolidate head/shoulder line.
- Your priority stack:
  1. Frames + knee shield preservation (structure)
  2. Outside shoulder/hip maintenance (side orientation)
  3. Recovery on first flattening cascade signals (elbow escape)
- Best conversion families (conceptually):
  - Kuzushi that disrupts posture forward (they’re heavy forward).
  - Stand-up conversions once you’re on elbow/knee line.

**B) Hip-Back “Float” Passer (Spacing, mobility, disengage-and-pass)**
- Their win condition: deny your connection and pass around the legs.
- Your priority stack:
  1. Upgrade from frames to attachment quickly (connection)
  2. Knee line control to prevent easy step-out disengagement
  3. Use kuzushi to force posts/steps that create re-connection
- Best conversion families:
  - Tight waist / body lock attachment emphasis.
  - Scoop/knee lever engines that punish retreating hips.

**C) Whizzer-First Defender (Stops get-ups immediately)**
- Their win condition: block stand-up conversion and re-square hips.
- Your priority stack:
  1. Recognize whizzer as a branch trigger (not a “problem to muscle through”)
  2. Enter whizzer solution branch (angle + re-attachment logic)
  3. Convert to alternate finish or sweep as whizzer commits their structure
- Best conversion families:
  - Tight waist → whizzer solutions.
  - Transitions to single leg mechanics where torso attachment is contested.

**D) Leg-Lock Aware Top Player (Hides legs, counters entries)**
- Their win condition: force you to “dive” for legs and then pass.
- Your priority stack:
  1. Leg entries only after posture/posts/base are compromised (kuzushi first)
  2. Movement control before entanglement (scoop elevation as a hub)
  3. Return-to-hub discipline if entry is blocked (don’t chase)
- Best conversion families:
  - Scoop elevation routes that control movement.
  - Deep-half entries only when you can preserve alignment and deny collapse.

---

### 5.9 Branch Triggers (When the System Switches Modes)

**Trigger 1 — Crossface starts to win**
- Signal: shoulder line turns away; frames bend; hips square.
- Action: switch to **Recovery Hub** (half-elbow escape + re-frame) until you regain side orientation.

**Trigger 2 — You reach elbow/knee line cleanly**
- Signal: you can support your weight and rise while connected.
- Action: enter **Stand-Up Hub** (tight waist/body lock/single leg).

**Trigger 3 — Opponent posts hard to stop kuzushi**
- Signal: hand hits mat, base widens, head shifts to stabilize.
- Action: choose **Back exposure or stand-up conversion** based on angle.

**Trigger 4 — Whizzer appears**
- Signal: overhook + hip turn blocks coming up.
- Action: enter **Whizzer Solution Branch** (do not continue the same get-up).

**Trigger 5 — Opponent’s base is compromised and knee/foot line is available**
- Signal: leg is light, posture broken, posts committed.
- Action: enter **Leg-Lock Access** (scoop elevation or deep half route).

### 5.10 Decision Tree (High-Level)
Start: **Do you have outside shoulder/hip?**
- **No →** Recovery (elbow escape + re-frames) → rebuild structure → re-test.
- **Yes →** Do you have a meaningful attachment?
  - **No →** build connection (tight waist / knee line / 2-on-1) while maintaining frames.
  - **Yes →** apply kuzushi (target posture/posts/base).
    - If opponent **posts/steps/turns** → convert:
      - **Stand-up** if you can rise with connection.
      - **Back exposure** if angle opens via posting/turning.
      - **Sweep-to-pin** if kuzushi rotates torso and kills base.
      - **Legs** if you own movement and leg line is exposed.
    - If opponent **crossfaces/flattens** → Recovery branch.

### 5.11 “Hub Discipline” Rules (Prevents System Breakdown)
- If you lose structure, **do not chase outcomes** (especially legs). Return to a hub.
- Treat each hub as a *reset point* you can re-enter repeatedly:
  - knee shield + frames
  - elbow escape
  - tight waist
  - knee lever
  - scoop elevation
  - deep half

### 5.12 Performance Metrics (How You Know It’s Working)
Use these as training diagnostics:

**Structure metrics**
- You can stay on your side > you are not repeatedly flattened.
- Knee shield survives first contact; frames remain skeletal, not bent.

**Connection metrics**
- You routinely upgrade from frames to attachment within a few seconds (not prolonged “blocking”).
- Opponent cannot freely disengage and step out.

**Kuzushi metrics**
- You regularly force one of: post, widened base, hip turn, step.
- Failed sweeps still create predictable reactions that feed a second attempt.

**Conversion metrics**
- You reach one of the four end-states in live rounds with control:
  - stand-up finish, back exposure, sweep-to-pin, controlled entanglement.

**Failure metrics**
- If most losses occur via flattening, your issue is outside shoulder/hip and recovery timing (not “more moves”).
- If most losses occur via opponent disengagement, your issue is attachment speed and knee line control.

### 5.13 Common System Misreads (Concept-Level Errors)
- **Mistaking frames for offense:** framing without attachment produces delay, not outcomes.
- **Chasing legs from bad structure:** diving for legs while losing outside alignment invites passing.
- **Treating the whizzer as strength test:** it’s a branch trigger; solve it with system logic.
- **Scramble addiction:** if every success requires athletic scramble, you skipped hubs and kuzushi quality.

### 5.15 Situational Starting Positions (Mini-Gameplans)

**Start A — Knee Shield Already Established (Ideal Entry)**
- Objective: upgrade from structure to attachment without losing outside alignment.
- Sequence (conceptual):
  1) stabilize frames + knee shield angle
  2) attach (tight waist / knee line / 2-on-1)
  3) kuzushi to force post/step
  4) convert (stand/back/top/legs)

**Start B — No Knee Shield, But You’re Still On Your Side**
- Objective: rebuild a structural layer before launching offense.
- Priority:
  1) re-frame to prevent crossface consolidation
  2) re-insert knee shield or equivalent space-maker
  3) re-enter the default loop

**Start C — Flattening Threat Has Begun (Crossface + Hip Collapse Emerging)**
- Objective: treat as a pin-escape problem immediately.
- Priority:
  1) stop chasing attacks
  2) elbow-escape recovery + re-framing
  3) restore side orientation
  4) rebuild structure hub, then re-attach

**Start D — Tight Waist Already Achieved**
- Objective: convert before opponent installs whizzer/hip turn defense.
- Priority:
  1) lower-leg shift / angle management to protect the attachment
  2) kuzushi/tilt to force weight transfer
  3) convert to stand-up finish or sweep-to-control
- Branch trigger: whizzer appears → enter whizzer solution logic.

**Start E — Opponent Posting Hard to Kill Elevation**
- Objective: interpret posts as “reaction confirmation,” then take the offered lane.
- Priority:
  1) secure the post you can control (2-on-1 or block)
  2) convert to back exposure or stand-up based on angle
  3) if posture breaks and leg line opens → leg-lock access hubs

**Start F — Deep Underneath Position Is Available (Deep Half Window)**
- Objective: only proceed if alignment and post-control are present.
- Priority:
  1) prevent collapse (outside shoulder/hip still matters)
  2) compromise posts/base first
  3) enter sankaku/50-50 routes with control
- Abort rule: if you feel collapsing pressure + no post-control → return to recovery/structure.

### 5.16 No-Gi Constraint Model (Why the System Uses “Hubs”)
Because no-gi limits friction and static grips:
- prolonged static guard retention is less reliable,
- disengagement and re-engagement happen faster,
- “micro-wins” (alignment, posts, knee line, attachments) matter more than holding.

**System adaptation:** favor repeatable hubs (knee shield, tight waist, knee lever, scoop elevation) that:
- do not require cloth grips,
- generate forcing power through alignment + levers,
- survive sweat/friction loss by design.

---

### 5.17 Gripping/Attachment Philosophy (No-Gi)
Attachments are chosen to be:
- **structural** (they hold under motion),
- **directional** (they create specific kuzushi),
- **convertible** (they feed multiple endpoints).

Key attachment categories:
- **Torso attachment:** tight waist / body lock concepts (stand-up and sweep bridge)
- **Knee line attachment:** lever/shift/scoop controls (base denial)
- **Posting-hand control:** 2-on-1 and related controls (reaction locking)

**Rule:** if you cannot name what your attachment is *controlling* (torso, knee line, or post), you are not attached—you are just framing.

---

### 5.18 MMA Translation (Why Half Guard Fits MMA)
The system’s emphasis aligns with MMA realities:
- **Connection + underneath control** reduces striking exposure compared to loose open guards.
- **Stand-up conversion** (tight waist/body lock/single leg) maps directly to wrestling outcomes.
- **Pin-escape integration** matters because MMA top pressure is amplified by strikes.
- **Leg-lock branches** exist, but the broader theme is conversion to top/stand/back rather than prolonged bottom play.

Conceptual MMA heuristic:
- prioritize structure that protects your head line,
- stand-up conversion becomes a primary “safe exit,”
- treat flattening as urgent due to strike volume risk.

---

### 5.19 System Interoperability (How Hubs Feed Each Other)
Hubs are not linear; they are a graph:

- Knee shield hub feeds:
  - kuzushi toolkit → stand/back/top
  - scoop elevation → leg-lock access
  - recovery (if flattened threat starts)

- Tight waist hub feeds:
  - stand-up finishes
  - sweeps/tilts
  - whizzer branch (when defended)
  - knee lever links (when opponent’s base reconfigures)

- Knee lever hub feeds:
  - heist → single leg
  - arm drag → back exposure
  - returns to tight waist / butterfly routes
  - leg access when base collapses

- Recovery hub feeds:
  - back to knee shield structure
  - re-entry into attachment acquisition

**Rule:** a failed attempt should fall into *another hub*, not into “nothing.”

---

### 5.20 Troubleshooting Map (If X Happens, Fix Y)
- **You get flattened repeatedly**
  - Fix: outside shoulder/hip awareness, earlier recovery timing, frame integrity, knee shield angle retention.

- **Opponent disengages and passes around**
  - Fix: attachment speed, knee line controls, transition from frames → connection sooner.

- **Kuzushi feels ineffective**
  - Fix: identify target (posture/posts/base), improve attachment quality, ensure you’re breaking *knee line* not just “pushing.”

- **Stand-ups stall**
  - Fix: tighten torso attachment pathway, improve lower-leg shift/angle logic, anticipate whizzer event.

- **Whizzer shuts everything down**
  - Fix: treat whizzer as branch trigger; enter whizzer solution logic rather than persisting with the same rise.

- **Leg entries lead to getting passed**
  - Fix: only enter legs after posture/posts/base are compromised; prioritize movement control hubs (scoop elevation) and abort to hubs when blocked.

- **Everything becomes uncontrolled scrambles**
  - Fix: return-to-hub discipline; treat hubs as reset points; convert only when structure is present.

### 5.21 “Minimal Script” for Live Rolling (Practical Execution)
- Default: knee shield + frames → attach → kuzushi → convert.
- Under pressure: if crossface starts → elbow escape recovery → rebuild knee shield.
- If you get elbow/knee line → stand-up hub (tight waist/body lock/single leg).
- If defended by whizzer → whizzer branch.
- If base breaks and leg line opens → scoop/deep-half leg-lock access (controlled only).

### 5.24 System Contract (Non-Negotiables That Keep Everything Working)
1. **Outside shoulder/hip is a prerequisite for offense.**  
   If it is being lost, you are in recovery—not attacking.

2. **Frames are for alignment; attachments are for outcomes.**  
   You must upgrade from frames → attachment to create forcing power.

3. **Kuzushi must force a reaction.**  
   If it doesn’t cause post/step/turn/widening, the issue is usually attachment quality or target selection (posture/posts/base).

4. **Conversions must land in control.**  
   If outcomes are consistently “scramble-only,” return to hubs and rebuild forcing conditions.

5. **Branch events are expected.**  
   Crossface/flattening → recovery branch. Whizzer → whizzer branch. Sit-out/thumb-post defenses → counter branch inside leg-lock hubs.

## 8.0 APPLICATION SCENARIOS

### 8.1 Scenario A — Crossface Pressure Passer (Chest-to-chest, flattening focus)
**Opponent objective:** win crossface + hip collapse → flatten → pass.  
**Your primary objective:** preserve outside shoulder/hip; treat early flattening as a recovery gate.

**Playbook**
1. **Structure first:** establish/maintain knee shield + frames. Deny head control.
2. **Attachment upgrade:** move from frames to connection (tight waist or knee line) *without* squaring hips.
3. **Kuzushi selection:** favor off-balances that punish forward pressure (bump/elevator families).
4. **Conversion choice:** if you reach elbow/knee line → stand-up hub; if posting occurs → back exposure or sweep-to-pin.
5. **Recovery gate:** if crossface starts turning your shoulder line → immediate elbow escape + re-frame → rebuild knee shield.

**Do-not-do**
- Do not chase legs while shoulder line is being turned.
- Do not accept chest-to-chest with bent arms.

---

### 8.2 Scenario B — Hip-Back “Float” Passer (Disengage-and-pass, mobility)
**Opponent objective:** deny your connection; step out and pass around.  
**Your primary objective:** attach quickly and control knee line to prevent disengagement.

**Playbook**
1. **Rapid attachment:** frames are temporary—upgrade to tight waist / knee line control fast.
2. **Kuzushi selection:** scoop/knee lever families that remove the opponent’s ability to step away.
3. **Conversion choice:** prioritize stand-up finishes (tight waist/body lock/single leg) once connection is established.
4. **Fail-safe:** if opponent disengages successfully, re-establish structure hub and re-attach rather than “reaching.”

**Do-not-do**
- Do not remain frame-only while opponent backs hips away.
- Do not overextend upper body to chase connection (invites passing angles).

---

### 8.3 Scenario C — Whizzer-First Defender (Stops your rise immediately)
**Opponent objective:** whizzer + hip turn blocks your stand-up and re-squares.  
**Your primary objective:** treat whizzer as a branch trigger and convert it into a liability.

**Playbook**
1. **Entry:** reach stand-up hub with tight waist connection.
2. **Branch trigger:** whizzer appears → stop the default rise pathway.
3. **Whizzer branch:** use rotational/circle-body-lock style solutions and linked finishes (pins/top control).
4. **Fallback:** if whizzer stalls conversion, shift to single leg logic or re-enter knee lever engine to re-break base.

**Do-not-do**
- Do not “strength test” the whizzer by continuing the same get-up line.

---

### 8.4 Scenario D — Strong Base / Heavy Posts (Hard to tip, posts early)
**Opponent objective:** stabilize with early posts and widened base.  
**Your primary objective:** “reaction farm” posts into back exposure, stand-up, or knee-line denial.

**Playbook**
1. Apply kuzushi specifically to force a **predictable post** (scissor/windmill/tilt families).
2. Once posting occurs, switch from “trying to tip” to **taking the lane created by the post**:
   - back exposure if angle opens,
   - stand-up if elbow/knee line is available,
   - knee lever if the knee line becomes light/shifted.
3. If posts remain dominant, prioritize knee line control (knee lever family) to remove the base at its source.

**Do-not-do**
- Do not repeatedly attempt the same kuzushi without changing the targeted stability element.

---

### 8.5 Scenario E — Opponent Attempts to Smash Knee Shield (Fold + crossface)
**Opponent objective:** collapse your knee shield and connect head/shoulder line.  
**Your primary objective:** maintain structural integrity or switch to recovery before flattening completes.

**Playbook**
1. Reinforce frame quality (skeletal alignment) and re-angle knee shield.
2. If knee shield begins to fold, rebuild it immediately or transition to elbow escape recovery.
3. After recovery, re-enter with structure → attachment → kuzushi.

**Do-not-do**
- Do not wait until you are flat to begin recovery.

---

### 8.6 Scenario F — You Are Already Flattened (Late-stage defense)
**Opponent objective:** consolidate pin and pass.  
**Your primary objective:** treat as pin escape: restore side orientation and rebuild structure.

**Playbook**
1. Enter recovery hub: elbow escape mechanics + re-framing.
2. Restore side orientation (outside shoulder/hip).
3. Rebuild knee shield (structure hub).
4. Only after structure is rebuilt: re-attach and re-enter the main loop.

**Do-not-do**
- No leg entries, no stand-ups, no “offense” while flat.

---

### 8.7 Scenario G — Opponent Gives You Elbow/Knee Line During Pressure
**Opponent objective:** often accidental—pressure shifts give you base.  
**Your primary objective:** convert immediately to the stand-up hub while connected.

**Playbook**
1. Secure attachment (tight waist/body lock or single leg capture).
2. Build base underneath (heist mechanics).
3. Finish: stand-up completion or sweep-to-control depending on opponent reaction.

**Do-not-do**
- Do not pause to “reset”—the opening is timing-dependent.

---

### 8.8 Scenario H — Leg-Lock Aware Top Player (Hides legs, counters)
**Opponent objective:** deny leg entries, bait you into diving, then pass.  
**Your primary objective:** only enter legs from controlled access hubs with movement control.

**Playbook**
1. Use structure + attachment to compromise posture/posts/base first.
2. Enter Leg Access A (knee shield → scoop elevation) when you can control movement.
3. If deep half route appears, ensure alignment + post control before committing.
4. If the opponent counters (sit-out/thumb post style behaviors), switch to the counter-tree anchors and return-to-hub discipline.

**Do-not-do**
- No leg entries without prior movement control or base compromise.

---

### 8.9 Scenario I — Your Kuzushi “Works” but You Don’t Convert
**Problem pattern:** you get reactions but fail to reach end-states.  
**Fix objective:** connect reaction → conversion with pre-chosen endpoints.

**Playbook**
1. Identify which reaction you’re consistently getting (post, step, hip turn).
2. Pre-assign a conversion:
   - Post → back exposure or angle to stand-up.
   - Step-out → knee lever or re-attachment to tight waist.
   - Hip turn/whizzer → whizzer branch or single leg finish.
3. Drill the conversion link (reaction → endpoint) rather than adding new kuzushi.

---

### 8.10 Scenario J — “Scramble Addiction” (Everything becomes chaos)
**Problem pattern:** success depends on athletic scrambles; failures lead to passes.  
**Fix objective:** restore hub discipline and control standards.

**Playbook**
1. Define your reset hub (knee shield, tight waist, knee lever, or recovery).
2. When scramble begins and structure is not present: return to a hub immediately.
3. Convert only when you can satisfy the system contract (outside alignment + attachment + reaction forced).

**Do-not-do**
- Do not chase outcomes past the point where structure is lost.


## 9.0 DRILLING PROGRESSIONS

### 9.1 Drilling Design Rules (How These Progressions Are Built)
All drills follow this progression:
1) **Static reps** (mechanics + alignment)  
2) **Progressive resistance** (partner gives structured resistance)  
3) **Constraint sparring** (limited goals and branches)  
4) **Full sparring with metrics** (track outcomes)

Primary constraints:
- Offense is only allowed when outside shoulder/hip is intact.
- If flattening begins, the bottom player must switch to recovery.
- Every rep must end in a defined endpoint: stand, back exposure, top/pin, or controlled leg entanglement.

---

### 9.2 Structure Maintenance Drill (Knee Shield + Frames)
**Goal:** hold outside shoulder/hip and prevent flattening while maintaining a functional knee shield.

- **Start:** bottom has knee shield + frames; top applies steady pressure and attempts to fold the shield and establish crossface.
- **Constraint:** bottom cannot attack—only maintain structure and re-angle.
- **Success criteria:** bottom stays on side; top fails to consolidate crossface for the duration.
- **Progression:**
  - P1: top pressure is slow and linear
  - P2: top adds head position + crossface attempts
  - P3: top adds hip-walk/pass attempts after winning head control

---

### 9.3 Flattening Gate + Recovery Drill (Half Elbow Escape)
**Goal:** automatic switching to recovery when the flattening cascade begins.

- **Start:** top begins with partial crossface and hip pressure (near-flattened).
- **Constraint:** bottom must recover to side orientation and rebuild knee shield before any offense is allowed.
- **Success criteria:** bottom restores outside shoulder/hip and returns to structure hub.
- **Progression:**
  - P1: top provides moderate pressure, no passing finish
  - P2: top tries to pass as soon as bottom turns
  - P3: top chains re-crossface attempts during rebuild

---

### 9.4 Frame-to-Attachment Upgrade Drill (Connection Acquisition)
**Goal:** transition from frame-only to a meaningful attachment without losing alignment.

- **Start:** bottom in structure hub (knee shield + frames). Top is stable but not crossfacing hard.
- **Task:** bottom upgrades to one attachment:
  - tight waist OR
  - knee line control OR
  - 2-on-1 post control
- **Constraint:** bottom must keep side orientation; if flattened threat begins, reset.
- **Success criteria:** bottom achieves and holds the attachment for a set count.
- **Progression:** reduce time allowed to attach (e.g., 10s → 7s → 5s).

---

### 9.5 Kuzushi Reaction Farming Drill (Post/Step/Turn)
**Goal:** learn to force predictable reactions with a chosen kuzushi family.

- **Start:** bottom has attachment (tight waist or knee line). Top maintains a stable base.
- **Task:** bottom applies one kuzushi family repeatedly until one reaction is forced:
  - post, step, hip turn, or widened base.
- **Constraint:** bottom must name the target (posture/posts/base) before each rep.
- **Success criteria:** reaction occurs within the time cap; bottom maintains outside alignment.
- **Progression:** top becomes better at “absorbing” without overreacting.

---

### 9.6 Reaction → Conversion Link Drill (Two-Step Chain)
**Goal:** eliminate “kuzushi works but nothing happens.”

- **Start:** bottom has attachment; top agrees to give one reaction (e.g., posting with near hand).
- **Task:** bottom executes the pre-assigned conversion for that reaction:
  - Post → back exposure OR angle to stand-up
  - Step-out → knee lever OR re-attachment to tight waist
  - Hip turn/whizzer → whizzer branch OR single leg finish
- **Constraint:** bottom must convert within a short window after reaction.
- **Success criteria:** reach a controlled end-state (E1–E4).
- **Progression:** top gives more realistic, mixed reactions.

---

### 9.7 Stand-Up Conversion Drill (Tight Waist / Single Leg Finish Line)
**Goal:** build reliable finishing once elbow/knee line is reached.

- **Start:** bottom begins with tight waist already secured.
- **Task:** bottom converts to:
  - stand-up completion OR
  - sweep-to-top control
- **Branch:** if whizzer appears, bottom must switch to whizzer branch (no strength testing).
- **Success criteria:** finish is completed with control, not scramble-only.
- **Progression:** top adds (a) whizzer early, (b) late hip turn, (c) disengage attempts.

---

### 9.8 Whizzer Branch Drill (Event Recognition + Solution)
**Goal:** automatic branch switching on whizzer installation.

- **Start:** bottom in stand-up hub; top immediately installs whizzer as bottom rises.
- **Task:** bottom identifies the event and enters whizzer solution pathway to a finish/top pin.
- **Constraint:** bottom may not continue the default rise line once whizzer is present.
- **Success criteria:** reach E1 or E3.
- **Progression:** top varies whizzer intensity and timing; adds posts/disengagement.

---

### 9.9 Knee Lever Engine Drill (Forced Scramble Wins)
**Goal:** use knee lever to create a reaction, then link to a chosen endpoint.

- **Start:** knee lever entry position established.
- **Task:** bottom forces a reaction, then chooses one link:
  - heist → single leg
  - arm drag → back exposure
  - return to tight waist
  - butterfly insertion
- **Constraint:** bottom must keep outside alignment during the scramble.
- **Success criteria:** reach E1/E2 or re-enter a hub with control.
- **Progression:** top becomes reactive: posting, stepping out, hip turning.

---

### 9.10 Leg Access A Drill (Knee Shield → Scoop Elevation → Movement Control)
**Goal:** enter legs only via controlled access with movement control.

- **Start:** knee shield in place; bottom can access scoop elevation.
- **Task:** bottom achieves scoop elevation and demonstrates movement control (top cannot freely step out).
- **Then:** choose one transition (spin, far-leg, ankle pick → irimi ashi).
- **Constraint:** if movement control is lost, bottom must abort back to structure hub.
- **Success criteria:** controlled entry to E4 or clean abort to H0.
- **Progression:** top becomes better at retracting leg and disengaging.

---

### 9.11 Leg Access B Drill (Deep Half → Sankaku/50-50 + Counter Anchors)
**Goal:** disciplined deep-half entries with counter readiness.

- **Start:** deep-half entry available under controlled conditions.
- **Task:** bottom enters inside sankaku or 50/50 route.
- **Branch:** top uses a designated counter behavior (e.g., thumb-post/sit-out style defense).
- **Response:** bottom applies the counter anchor and stabilizes position.
- **Success criteria:** controlled entanglement maintained (no pass conceded).
- **Progression:** top varies counters and adds passing threats.

---

### 9.12 “Return-to-Hub” Drill (Anti-Chase Discipline)
**Goal:** train abort logic to prevent system breakdown.

- **Start:** bottom attempts any conversion; top’s job is to create a messy scramble and threaten passing.
- **Task:** bottom must choose and return to a hub:
  - structure (knee shield),
  - stand-up (tight waist re-attach),
  - knee lever,
  - or recovery (elbow escape).
- **Constraint:** bottom must verbally call the chosen hub during training (reinforces decision gating).
- **Success criteria:** bottom resets to a hub without conceding flattening/pass.

---

### 9.13 Measurement (What to Track Weekly)
- % rounds where you are flattened (should trend down)
- Average time from structure → attachment (should trend down)
- Reactions forced per minute (post/step/turn counts)
- Conversions to endpoints (E1–E4 count)
- Abort success rate (return-to-hub without conceding pass)

### 9.17 Constraint Sparring Rulesets (Plug-and-Play)

**Ruleset 1 — Outside Alignment Gate**
- Bottom may only attack if they can demonstrate side orientation (outside shoulder/hip).
- If flattened, bottom must recover before attacking again.
- Win condition: bottom reaches any end-state (E1–E4) or resets to a hub under pressure.

**Ruleset 2 — Attachment First**
- Bottom cannot attempt sweep/entry until they secure one attachment:
  - tight waist OR knee line control OR 2-on-1.
- Purpose: eliminates “move hunting” from frames.

**Ruleset 3 — Reaction Required**
- Bottom must force and identify a reaction (post/step/turn/widen base) before converting.
- Purpose: trains kuzushi as engine, not decoration.

**Ruleset 4 — Stand-Up Only Day**
- Bottom can only finish via E1/E3 (stand-up finish or sweep-to-top).
- No leg locks allowed.
- Purpose: strengthens the primary conversion layer.

**Ruleset 5 — Leg Discipline Day**
- Leg entries only allowed via:
  - knee shield → scoop elevation hub, OR
  - deep half → sankaku/50-50 hub.
- If entry is blocked or control is lost: mandatory abort to a hub.

- **Start:** bottom is near-flattened; top begins crossface/hip pressure.
- **Task:** bottom recovers to side orientation + rebuilds structure.
- **Then (immediately):** bottom must upgrade to an attachment and force one kuzushi reaction.
- **Success:** recovery completes and bottom reaches at least [H1] connection or forces a reaction within a time cap.

---

### 9.19 Scorpion Mobility Micro-Progression (Vol 6)
**Goal:** integrate scorpion layer as a controlled mobility/advancement module.

- **Start:** scorpion configuration is set by partner.
- **Task:** bottom performs the mobility pathway (pass / walk-around / hip-heist concept) while maintaining alignment.
- **Constraint:** if alignment is lost, reset; no “racing” through motion.
- **Success:** positional upgrade achieved without conceding re-guarding frames.

### 9.20 Weekly KPI Targets (Concrete Benchmarks)
These are practical targets to aim for:

- Flattened events: reduce to **≤1 per round** on average.
- Structure → attachment time: **≤7 seconds** from knee shield start.
- Reactions forced: **≥3 meaningful reactions per 2-minute round**.
- End-states: **≥1 controlled endpoint per round** (E1–E4).

## 11.0 FAILURE PATTERNS

### 11.1 Failure Pattern: Flattened Half Guard (Crossface + Hip Collapse)
**What it looks like**
- Your head/shoulders are turned away; you are on your back.
- Your frames are bent and absorbing weight.
- Knee shield is folded or bypassed; opponent is chest-to-chest.

**Why it happens**
- Outside shoulder/hip condition was lost and you continued attacking.
- Knee shield angle degraded without immediate rebuild.
- You attempted entries (stand-up/legs) without structure.

**Fix (hub-level)**
- Immediate switch to [H2] Recovery: elbow escape + re-framing.
- Rebuild [H0] Structure (knee shield + frames) before re-attacking.

---

### 11.2 Failure Pattern: Frame-Only “Stalling Guard”
**What it looks like**
- You are safe for a moment but never force reactions.
- Opponent either eventually smashes through or disengages and passes.

**Why it happens**
- Frames are used as a destination instead of a bridge.
- No upgrade to attachment (tight waist / knee line / 2-on-1).

**Fix**
- Set a time cap: structure → attachment within X seconds.
- Run 9.4 (Frame→Attachment) and Ruleset 2 (Attachment First).

---

### 11.3 Failure Pattern: Kuzushi Without Conversion
**What it looks like**
- You bump/tilt/elevate and the opponent posts/steps—but nothing follows.
- You reset and repeat, creating fatigue without endpoints.

**Why it happens**
- No pre-assigned conversion for the reaction you’re getting.
- You’re not recognizing reaction type (post vs step vs hip turn).

**Fix**
- Reaction → conversion mapping (9.6).
- Choose one reaction per week and build the link until automatic.

---

### 11.4 Failure Pattern: Kuzushi Feels “Weak” (No Reaction)
**What it looks like**
- Off-balancing attempts feel like pushing.
- Opponent’s base remains stable; no post/step/turn is forced.

**Why it happens**
- Poor attachment quality (you’re not controlling torso/knee line/post).
- Wrong target (trying to break posture when base is the issue, etc.).
- Head position and alignment not supporting the direction of force.

**Fix**
- Upgrade attachments (tight waist or knee line) before kuzushi.
- Identify the stability element you intend to break (posture/posts/base).
- Use knee-line denial to attack base rather than “moving the torso.”

---

### 11.5 Failure Pattern: Stand-Up Attempts Get Stalled
**What it looks like**
- You rise but cannot finish.
- Opponent re-squares, down-blocks, or you fall back to half guard without progress.

**Why it happens**
- You rise without sufficient attachment (connection breaks mid-rise).
- You delay and allow the opponent to install defensive structure.

**Fix**
- Emphasize tight waist/body lock integrity during rise.
- Train time-window conversions: once elbow/knee line is achieved, commit to the finish chain.

---

### 11.6 Failure Pattern: Whizzer Shuts Everything Down
**What it looks like**
- You get tight waist and begin rising; opponent whizzers and you stall.
- You keep driving up and burn energy.

**Why it happens**
- Treating whizzer as a strength test rather than a branch event.
- Continuing the same line after the event occurs.

**Fix**
- Mandatory branch recognition drill (9.8).
- Enter whizzer solution pathways that punish the committed overhook and hip turn.

---

### 11.7 Failure Pattern: Opponent Disengages and Passes (“Float Pass” Problem)
**What it looks like**
- You maintain frames but opponent backs hips away and circles.
- Your half guard connection never becomes real.

**Why it happens**
- Too slow upgrading from structure to attachment.
- Lack of knee-line control to prevent step-out.

**Fix**
- Set strict attachment time caps (≤7 seconds).
- Use attachments that punish retreat (tight waist, knee line control, scoop concepts).

---

### 11.8 Failure Pattern: Knee Lever Creates Chaos (You Lose the Scramble)
**What it looks like**
- Knee lever initiates scramble but you end up losing position or getting passed.
- Outcomes depend on speed/athleticism.

**Why it happens**
- You attempt knee lever without maintaining outside alignment.
- No preselected link (heist/drag/return-to-hub); you chase mid-scramble.

**Fix**
- Knee lever with “return-to-hub” constraint: if link fails, reset to structure or re-attach tight waist.
- Use 9.9 + 9.12 pairing until scramble outcomes become structured.

---

### 11.9 Failure Pattern: Leg Entries Lead to Getting Passed
**What it looks like**
- You reach for legs; opponent retracts and passes.
- You lose alignment while chasing.

**Why it happens**
- Entering legs without movement control or before base is compromised.
- Diving from a position where outside shoulder/hip is already threatened.

**Fix**
- Legs only via access hubs:
  - [H4] knee shield → scoop elevation
  - [H8] deep half → sankaku/50-50
- Abort rule: if movement control is lost, return to [H0]/[H2] immediately.

---

### 11.10 Failure Pattern: “Top but Not Safe” (Sweep Without Control)
**What it looks like**
- You sweep but immediately lose top due to scramble or lack of pinning.
- Opponent re-guards or comes up on a leg.

**Why it happens**
- Prioritizing the sweep moment rather than the landing control.
- Not converting to pins/pressure as an endpoint.

**Fix**
- Finish standard: every sweep must land into a control state (pin/pressure or stabilized top).
- Use end-state metrics: count “sweep-to-pin” not “sweep-to-scramble.”

---

### 11.11 Failure Pattern: You Ignore the Recovery Gate (Late Switching)
**What it looks like**
- You wait too long to recover; you attempt offense while already flattened.
- Recovery becomes exhausting and unreliable.

**Why it happens**
- Failure to recognize early flattening cascade signals (knee shield fold, frame bend).
- Ego-driven “I can still attack” behavior.

**Fix**
- Train early switching: intervene at cascade steps 1–2, not 3–4.
- Use 9.3 with explicit gate: once crossface starts turning shoulders, recovery is mandatory.

---

### 11.12 Failure Pattern: System Fragmentation (You Have Moves, Not a System)
**What it looks like**
- You know techniques but can’t decide what to do under pressure.
- You jump between unrelated attempts.

**Why it happens**
- No hub-based mental model.
- No adherence to the system contract.

**Fix**
- Rehearse the loop verbally in training:
  structure → attach → kuzushi → convert (or recover).
- Use constraint sparring (Ruleset 1–3) to enforce system behavior.

## 13.0 TERMINOLOGY

### 13.1 Positional Laws and Tests
- **Outside Shoulder + Outside Hip (Governing Law):** The required outside alignment of your shoulder line and hip line that prevents flattening and enables offense. If lost, offense pauses and recovery is mandatory.
- **Outside Alignment Test:** A binary check: “Can I stay on my side with functional frames and rotate?” If no → you are in recovery mode.
- **Flattening:** Opponent consolidates crossface + hip collapse until your shoulders/hips square and you are pinned-like (half guard becomes a pin rather than a guard).

### 13.2 Structural Components
- **Structure Hub:** The default half-guard operating state (knee shield + frames + side orientation) from which you attach and attack.
- **Knee Shield:** Primary leg structure that blocks chest-to-chest pressure and preserves working distance for frames/attachments.
- **Frames:** Skeletal alignment tools (not pushing) that preserve your shoulder line, manage distance, and prevent crossface consolidation.
- **Frame Integrity:** Frames remain “stacked” (bone-aligned) rather than bent and absorbing weight.

### 13.3 Control, Connection, and Attachments
- **Connection:** Meaningful attachment to opponent structure (torso/knee line/posts) that enables forcing power.
- **Attachment (Connector):** A specific control handle that reliably transfers force and creates predictable reactions (e.g., tight waist, scoop, 2-on-1).
- **Torso Attachment:** Control centered on the opponent’s torso (tight waist/body lock logic) enabling tilt and stand-up conversion.
- **Knee Line Control:** Control of the opponent’s knee line (via levers/shifts/scoops) to deny stable base and stepping.
- **Post Control:** Control of an opponent’s posting limb (often via 2-on-1) to prevent stabilization and open conversion lanes.

### 13.4 Kuzushi and Stability Elements
- **Kuzushi:** Deliberate off-balancing designed to force predictable stabilization reactions (post/step/turn/widen base).
- **Stability Elements:** The three targets of kuzushi:
  - **Posture:** torso/head alignment over hips
  - **Posts:** hands/arms/knees/head used to prevent tipping
  - **Base:** stance width/foot-knee spacing and hip placement
- **Reaction Farming:** Using kuzushi intentionally to produce a known reaction that unlocks a planned conversion.
- **Kuzushi Families (labels used in this report):** scissor, bump, scoop, elevators (scorpion/half-butterfly), knee lever, tight-waist tilt, windmill.

### 13.5 Hubs, Branches, and End-States
- **Hub:** A repeatable control state you can reliably reach and reset to under resistance (structure, recovery, tight waist, knee lever, scoop elevation, deep half).
- **Branch Event:** A predictable opponent action that forces a system change (e.g., whizzer appears; crossface/flattening begins).
- **End-States (E1–E4):**
  - **E1 Stand-up finish:** rise to takedown completion (single leg/body lock/tight waist finish)
  - **E2 Back exposure/back take:** angle creates access to the back and control follows
  - **E3 Sweep-to-pin:** sweep that lands into stabilized top control/pins
  - **E4 Controlled entanglement:** leg position secured with control (inside sankaku/50-50 variants)

### 13.6 Recovery Vocabulary
- **Recovery Hub:** The mode used when outside alignment is threatened or lost (elbow escape + re-framing → rebuild structure).
- **Elbow Escape:** Primary alignment restoration method; used to return from flattening threat to side orientation and structure.
- **Half Elbow Escape:** A partial recovery/reframing stage used to prevent full flattening and rebuild knee shield.

### 13.7 Stand-Up Layer Vocabulary
- **Stand-Up Conversion:** The process of rising from half guard bottom while maintaining connection to finish takedowns or sweeps.
- **Tight Waist:** Torso attachment/control state used as a primary bridge to get-ups, tilts, and finishes.
- **Lower Leg Shift:** A leg-position adjustment used to improve leverage/angle for tight waist conversions.
- **Heist / Forward Heist:** Base-building rise mechanics used to come up into single leg or connected finishes.

### 13.8 Counter-Events and Defensive Terms
- **Crossface:** Head/shoulder control that turns your shoulder line away and drives flattening.
- **Hip Collapse:** Opponent pressure that squares your hips and removes your ability to stay on your side.
- **Whizzer:** Overhook defense commonly used to stop your rise/stand-up conversion; treated as a branch event requiring specific solutions.
- **Disengagement / Step-Out:** Opponent exits your connections to pass around (float-passing style); punished by faster attachments and knee line control.

### 13.9 Leg-Lock Access Vocabulary
- **Scoop Elevation:** Leg-access hub from knee shield that elevates/controls opponent leg line before entries.
- **Movement Control (Leg Access):** The requirement that the opponent cannot freely step away before you commit to entanglement.
- **Deep Half:** Underneath route used for leg-lock access and rotational entries; only used when alignment and post/base compromise are present.
- **Inside Sankaku:** Primary strong entanglement endpoint referenced in the deep-half leg-lock branch.
- **50/50 (variants):** Secondary entanglement family accessed from deep half depending on opponent reaction.
- **Outside Spin:** Rotational entry mechanic (often linked from knee lever) used to access entanglements.
- **Counter Anchors (Leg Locks):** Stabilizing responses to common defenses (e.g., thumb post, double knee lever, butterfly hook counter, reverse underhook counter).

### 13.10 Process Terms (Used Throughout the Report)
- **System Contract:** The non-negotiables that keep the system functional (alignment prerequisite, attachment before outcomes, reaction-based conversion, return-to-hub discipline).
- **Return-to-Hub:** Abort logic: when control degrades, reset to a hub (structure/stand-up/knee lever/recovery) rather than chase.
- **Conversion Window:** The brief timing interval immediately after a forced reaction where endpoint conversion is highest percentage.

## 14.0 ADDITIONAL NOTES

### 14.1 Implementation Heuristic: “One Hub Per Exchange”
A practical way to prevent fragmentation is to choose one hub as the focus for an exchange:
- structure hub (knee shield + frames),
- recovery hub (elbow escape + re-frames),
- stand-up hub (tight waist / single leg),
- knee lever hub,
- leg access hub (scoop elevation or deep half).

If your attempt fails, you do not improvise endlessly; you return to a hub and restart the loop.

---

### 14.2 The “Attachment Clock”
A useful constraint for training:
- from the moment structure is established, you have a short time window to upgrade to a real attachment.
If the opponent is allowed to pressure, disengage, and re-enter while you remain frame-only, the exchange trends toward either flattening or float passing.

Practical rule: **structure → attachment within ~7 seconds** (adjust to skill level).

---

### 14.3 End-State Standard (What Counts as “Success”)
Count only controlled outcomes:
- **E1:** stand-up completion to finish (not merely “I stood up”)
- **E2:** back exposure that becomes control (not merely “I got an angle”)
- **E3:** sweep that lands into stabilized top/pin (not merely “I reversed for a second”)
- **E4:** entanglement entered with movement control and stabilization (not merely “I grabbed a leg”)

This standard prevents the system from degrading into scramble-chasing.

---

### 14.4 “Abort Conditions” (When to Stop a Line)
Abort and return to a hub when:
- outside shoulder/hip alignment is compromised,
- your frames are bent and collapsing,
- your attachment is lost mid-transition,
- a leg entry no longer has movement control,
- an exchange becomes purely athletic scramble without structure.

---

### 14.5 Common Training Pairings (High ROI Combinations)
- **Structure maintenance (9.2)** immediately followed by **attachment upgrade (9.4)**.
- **Recovery gate (9.3)** immediately followed by **reaction farming (9.5)**.
- **Reaction → conversion (9.6)** immediately followed by **stand-up finishing (9.7)**.
- **Knee lever engine (9.9)** immediately followed by **return-to-hub discipline (9.12)**.
- **Leg access A/B (9.10/9.11)** paired with **abort discipline (9.12)**.

---

### 14.6 Integration Notes: Where This System “Lives” in a Broader Game
Half guard bottom here is not isolated; it connects directly to:
- wrestling-style stand-ups (tight waist/body lock/single leg),
- pin-escape fundamentals (elbow escape),
- leg entanglement systems (sankaku/50/50 variants).

This means:
- It can be used as a primary guard, or
- As a “safety net” guard that converts quickly rather than retaining indefinitely.

---

### 14.7 Style Matching (Choosing End-States by Personal Preference)
The system supports preference, but not at the expense of the contract:
- If you prefer wrestling: emphasize tight waist, heist, single leg finish lines.
- If you prefer back takes: bias reaction farming into angles and drag/heist links.
- If you prefer leg locks: emphasize scoop elevation and deep-half access—only from controlled hubs.

Preference selects **end-state**, not the prerequisite structure.

---

### 14.8 Minimal Solo Work (If Partner Time Is Limited)
Even without live partner drilling, you can reinforce:
- the conceptual loop (structure → attach → kuzushi → convert / recover),
- the attachment clock discipline,
- return-to-hub decision making.

Solo work won’t replace timing, but it helps prevent conceptual drift.

### 14.10 Quality Control Checklist (Quick Self-Audit)
