# 2.0 Executive Summary

This series presents open guard as a repeatable control system, not a collection of isolated techniques. The aim is to run a loop that reliably produces entry windows, converts to leg-dominant control, and prevents disengagement until a finish is secured.

At a high level, the operating loop is:

**Read → Connect → Retain (deny pass-build) → Break base → Enter legs → Stabilize hub → Prevent reset → Finish → Recycle**

Selection is governed by a small decision model (“context laws”) that keeps choices tight and consistent. Against **kneeling** opponents you read **shoulders vs hips**. Against **standing** opponents you read **heels vs toes** (felt through grips and frames). Head posture provides the third filter: **head high** favors going **under/elevation**, while **head low/hips back** shifts preference toward **coming up** (go-behind/rise/reattach). These reads determine which action family is appropriate before you commit to movement.

The first operational requirement is **connection**. Guard does not begin with movement; it begins with building attachments that survive steps, posture changes, and tempo. Grips are treated as **handles**—structural controls that let you steer the opponent’s posture and feet, not merely “hold.” The system prefers redundancy: multiple points of control that remain effective even when one attachment is peeled.

Guard retention is treated as the **first skill** and is framed as **knowledge/denial**. Instead of defending named passes, you identify which requirement the passer is building and deny it before it chains forward. Passing is modeled as a requirements chain:

**Distance → Grip → Angle → Level → Penetration → Pin**

Your job is to recognize the link being built right now, deny it, and immediately re-center. This turns defense into a diagnostic process and makes retention reproducible under pressure.

Retention alone is considered unstable over repeated exchanges, so the system mandates a second layer: **constant threat**. Every successful denial should produce counter-offense. The strategic engine is persistent threat against the legs—the one target the opponent cannot fully hide through stance and posture changes. Constant threat slows the passer’s tempo, forces hesitation, and creates the base errors needed for clean entries.

Entries are governed by a validity principle: high-yield entries appear after **measurable base compromise**—posting/hands-down reactions, forced steps, collapse, or predictable retreats (backstep/flee) that can be followed and punished. Entries are organized into posture/range families (standing distance attacks, kneeling entries, and bridge positions such as X-guard/RDLR/HQ), plus DLR and closed-guard entries when clamps and alignment make them reliable. This organization keeps selection simple and prevents “hope-based” attempts.

After entry, the system emphasizes stabilization through **control hubs**. Entry is not success; stabilization is success. Hubs are chosen to suppress the opponent’s most common exits (turn-away, backstep, flee, heavy-leg/high-leg clears) and to keep the opponent carrying your structure while you progress.

Finally, finishing is treated as **anti-exit governance**. The opponent’s primary objective is to reset distance; your objective is to follow disengagement, occupy space immediately, and maintain the leg-control structure until the finish is forced. The outcome is an integrated game in which retention creates opportunities, constant threat generates errors, disciplined entries secure access, hubs stabilize control, and anti-exit rules convert control into finishes.

# 4.0 Core Concepts

## 4.1 Open Guard as a Control System
Open guard is treated as a repeatable loop, not a collection of techniques. Each exchange is run through:
**Read → Connect → Retain (deny pass-build) → Break base → Enter → Stabilize → Prevent reset → Finish → Recycle.**

## 4.2 Connection First (Grip as the First Action)
Guard play begins by creating connection that survives movement. Movement without attachment is treated as low-percentage because the opponent can simply step away, change posture, or clear frames.

## 4.3 Four-Limb Gripping (Redundancy)
Connection is built with coordinated use of hands and feet/legs. The objective is redundancy: if one attachment is peeled, the structure remains intact through secondary contact points.

## 4.4 2-on-1 as a Primary Handle
2-on-1 control is treated as a foundational “handle” because it limits the opponent’s ability to freely post, strip, and reposition. It also creates predictable reactions that can be converted into base breaks and entries.

## 4.5 Handles (Steering, Not Holding)
A grip is valuable only insofar as it functions as a handle: it should let you steer posture, steps, and alignment. Handles are evaluated by whether they:
- persist through steps,
- force reaction (posting/stance change),
- and open a clear next action (base break or entry).

## 4.6 Tempo (Who Sets the Rate of Change)
Tempo is a governing variable. The system favors grip-first sequences because grips let you dictate when the opponent must react, which creates the timing window for movement and entry.

## 4.7 Inside vs Outside Position vs Control
“Position” is not automatically “control.” Inside/outside relationships matter, but only count when they materially restrict posture, steps, or access to key lanes (hip line, angles, and penetration paths).

## 4.8 Dynamic Energy Theory (Push/Pull Commitment)
Passing and guard exchanges are treated as an energy management problem. You read whether the opponent is driving in, withdrawing, or redirecting—and choose actions that convert their commitment into predictable imbalance.

## 4.9 The Three Context Laws (Selection Filters)
Three reads simplify decision-making:
- **Shoulders vs hips** for kneeling opponents.
- **Heels vs toes** for standing opponents.
- **Head under/head over** as a posture/height filter that determines whether going under or coming up is more reliable.

## 4.10 Retention as Knowledge/Denial (Requirements Chain)
Guard retention is executed by denying the passer’s build, modeled as:
**Distance → Grip → Angle → Level → Penetration → Pin**
You defend by identifying the active link and breaking it before it chains forward.

## 4.11 Demarcation (Where the Guard is Being Lost)
Retention is managed by recognizing “where the fight is drifting” (line/zone loss) and immediately re-centering. Demarcation prevents slow, invisible loss of alignment that later becomes unrecoverable.

## 4.12 Essential Movement Vocabulary (Recovery Tools)
A compact movement set is prioritized because it can be applied repeatedly under pressure to re-establish alignment, reinsert legs, and recover frames without overcommitting or turning away from the opponent.

## 4.13 Constant Threat (Second Skill)
Retention is stabilized by constant offense. Persistent threat—especially against the legs—changes opponent behavior, slows passing tempo, and forces base compromises that create real entry windows.

## 4.14 Entry Validity (Base-Break Requirement)
Entries are governed by a proof condition: enter when the opponent’s base is measurably compromised (posting/hands-down reactions, forced steps, collapse, or predictable retreat patterns). This prevents “hope-based” entries.

## 4.15 Counter-Offense Doctrine (Retention Must Convert)
A denial cycle should not end in neutral. The governing rule is: successful retention should immediately become counter-offense, so the opponent pays for every pass-build attempt.

## 4.16 Stabilization Hubs (Entry is Not Success)
The system distinguishes between:
- **Entry:** access to legs/position,
- **Stabilization:** control that suppresses exits.
Hubs exist to convert access into stable control that survives motion and stripping.

## 4.17 Anti-Exit Governance (Finishing is Containment)
The opponent’s primary defense is reset/disengagement (turn-away, backstep, flee, heavy-leg/high-leg clears). Finishing is framed as preventing separation while progressively tightening control until the finish is forced.

# 4.0 Core Concepts (Expanded)

## 4.18 Range Management (Distance Breakers)
Guard success depends on forcing the exchange into a range where your attachments matter. Distance breakers are the methods that stop “step-away solves it” passing and convert free movement into controlled movement.

## 4.19 Orientation and Alignment (Square Before You Attack)
A recurring governance rule is to restore alignment before attempting high-commitment actions. When your hips/shoulders are misaligned, your legs become late and your frames become shallow. The system treats “square up” as a prerequisite, not a preference.

## 4.20 The Hip Line as a Safety Gate
The hip line is treated as the critical boundary: once the passer’s hips cross favorable angles and penetration depth, the exchange becomes recovery-heavy. The system emphasizes early denial and re-centering before hip-line loss becomes structural.

## 4.21 Frames as Structure (Not Pushing)
Frames are defined as load-bearing structures that preserve space and alignment. The point is not to bench-press the passer; it is to keep your skeleton connected to reliable contact points while you rebuild legs and reinsert control.

## 4.22 Breaking Posture vs Breaking Base
Posture breaks can be temporary; base breaks create forced steps and posting reactions. The system prioritizes base compromise because it generates predictable entry windows and reduces the opponent’s ability to strip or disengage.

## 4.23 Step Forcing as the Universal Trigger
Across volumes, step forcing shows up as the universal trigger for entries. If you can make the opponent step (or post), you can predict where weight goes next, which is where the entry becomes “valid.”

## 4.24 Predictable Reactions (If X, Then Y)
The system values grips/handles that produce predictable reactions. Predictability is treated as a form of control: if you can reliably force a posting hand, a retreat, or a specific stance change, you can route into the correct entry family without guesswork.

## 4.25 The “Hands-Down” Proof Condition
A recurring proof condition is that the opponent’s hands must be allocated to base (posting) rather than to stripping and attacking your legs. When hands are down, your rotational and underhooking actions become higher percentage.

## 4.26 Under vs Up: Two Modes of Progression
Progression is organized into two general modes:
- **Under mode:** you go beneath the opponent’s center of mass (elevation/entries) when posture allows.
- **Up mode:** you rise into them or go behind when the opponent’s hips/head posture make under-entries low percentage.
The decision reads select the mode.

## 4.27 Retention-to-Offense Timing (The Transition Window)
The best transition windows occur immediately after denial—when the passer’s chain breaks and they are mid-adjustment. The system emphasizes acting during their “repair step,” not after they have reset their stance.

## 4.28 Threat Stacking (Do Not Threaten One Thing)
Constant threat is not one attack repeated; it is stacked threat where defending one option exposes the next. The system treats this as the method for converting “defensive stability” into “offensive inevitability.”

## 4.29 Hub Logic (Why These Positions Repeat)
Hubs are selected because they:
- keep legs connected even while the opponent turns,
- limit the opponent’s ability to freely rotate out,
- and force predictable escape routes you can pre-empt.
This is why the system repeatedly routes into a small set of stabilizing configurations rather than chasing novelty.

## 4.30 The Reset Problem (Guard is Won by Denying Separation)
A primary opponent objective is separation—creating space to re-initiate the pass chain under better conditions. The system’s anti-exit governance is therefore treated as part of “finishing,” not an optional detail.

## 4.31 Failure is Usually a Chain Break (Not a Single Mistake)
Most failures are framed as allowing two requirements to link (e.g., distance plus angle; grip plus level). The training focus becomes identifying the earliest link you routinely give up and correcting that, rather than collecting more techniques.

## 4.32 The Method Standard (System > Moves)
Every technique is evaluated against the method standard:
1) Does it preserve or improve connection?
2) Does it deny the pass-build chain?
3) Does it force base compromise (step/post)?
4) Does it route into a hub that suppresses exits?
5) Does it prevent reset and lead to a finish?

If it cannot answer “yes” to most of these, it is treated as optional rather than core.

## 4.33 Layering (Defense → Offense → Control)
The system is layered intentionally:
- **Defense:** deny the pass-build chain.
- **Offense:** convert denial into constant threat.
- **Control:** stabilize in hubs that suppress exits.
Skipping layers produces volatility; completing layers produces repeatability.

## 4.34 Base-Break Hierarchy (Small Wins Create Big Wins)
Base compromise is accumulated through small wins: forcing posture changes, forcing steps, forcing posts. These micro-events are treated as the real triggers for entries, rather than arbitrary timing.

## 4.35 The Opponent’s Hands as a Resource
A core objective is to allocate the opponent’s hands to base and survival (posting/defensive frames) rather than stripping grips or controlling your legs. When their hands are busy, your attachment survival rate increases dramatically.

## 4.36 “One Target” Targeting Logic
Leg targeting is framed as a strategic constant because:
- it persists across posture changes,
- it persists across stance changes,
- and it forces predictable defensive patterns.
This reduces the decision tree and increases repeatability.

## 4.37 Entry Windows Are Created, Not Found
The model treats “windows” as manufactured outcomes of: connection + tempo + forced steps. If you are “waiting for an opening,” you are already behind in the system.

## 4.38 The Guard Player’s Default is Re-Attach
When contact breaks, the default response is not to chase with movement. It is to **re-attach with handles** and immediately re-enter the loop (read/connect/deny) before the passer can re-establish distance and grip.

## 4.39 Opponent Retreat is an Attack Trigger
Backsteps, fleeing, and stance resets are not neutral events; they are triggers. The system treats retreat as a moment where weight distribution and limb placement become predictable, enabling follow-ups rather than resets.

## 4.40 Control is Measured by Exit Suppression
Control is evaluated by what the opponent cannot do:
- cannot freely turn away,
- cannot freely backstep,
- cannot freely peel the primary attachment,
- cannot freely create distance.
If exits remain available, the “position” is treated as incomplete.

## 4.41 Stability vs Mobility (Choosing the Right Tool)
Some actions prioritize mobility (recovering, re-centering, re-inserting legs). Others prioritize stability (pinning, hub control). Confusing the two causes failure: chasing stability tools during a mobility problem (or vice versa).

## 4.42 The System Discourages Over-Rotation
A recurring governance theme is to avoid spinning/rotating under an opponent with stable posture and base. Rotations are treated as high-value only when base has already been compromised.

## 4.43 Progressive Denial (Don’t Let Them Rebuild)
After you deny one requirement, the opponent will attempt to rebuild the chain. The system emphasizes staying connected and denying the next build attempt immediately, rather than “resetting to neutral and starting over.”

## 4.44 Training Focus (What to Drill)
The drilling priority is not the longest technique sequences; it is the repeatable loop:
- connection creation under resistance,
- recognizing the active pass-build link,
- denying it and immediately countering,
- entering only on proof conditions,
- stabilizing in a hub,
- and preventing disengagement.

## 4.45 Evaluation Criteria (What Counts as Success)
Success is defined by outcomes inside the loop:
- you forced a post/step,
- you denied a pass-build link,
- you entered on a real window,
- you stabilized long enough to prevent reset,
- you progressed toward a finish.
If those outcomes are absent, treat the exchange as a diagnostic signal rather than “almost worked.”
  
## 4.46 The “Two Jobs” Rule (Stop the Pass + Create the Threat)
At every moment you are doing one of two jobs:
1) interrupt the passer’s requirements chain, or
2) create/maintain threat that prevents the chain from being built cleanly.
If neither is happening, you are drifting toward loss of initiative.

## 4.47 Initiative is a Structural Property
Initiative is not “going first.” It is having connection + alignment such that the opponent must respond to your structure. If your structure is not forcing a response, you do not have initiative.

## 4.48 False Security (Position Without Leverage)
A common failure mode is mistaking contact for control. If your grips/frames do not meaningfully restrict step options or posture, the passer can change variables faster than you can respond.

## 4.49 The Three-Point Stability Test
A position is “stable enough to progress” when you can reliably control:
- one key **posture line** (head/shoulders),
- one key **hip line** (alignment/angle),
- and one key **mobility line** (feet/steps).
Failing any one means you should prioritize re-attachment/re-alignment before advancing.

## 4.50 The “Step Map” (Where Weight Must Go Next)
Many entries are easier when you stop thinking about “moves” and start thinking about where the opponent’s next step must land. The objective is to force a narrow step set, then enter against the forced step.

## 4.51 Denial Priorities (What to Save First)
When overloaded, the triage order is:
1) protect hip line (avoid penetration),
2) restore distance/frames (buy time),
3) rebuild grips/handles (re-establish steering),
4) then recover angles/levels.
This prevents “saving the wrong thing first.”

## 4.52 Early vs Late Defense (Don’t Pay Late Fees)
The cost of denial increases as the chain advances. Denying distance or grip is low cost; denying penetration or pin is high cost. The system is optimized for early recognition and early denial.

## 4.53 Laddering: From Safe to Committed
Progression should “ladder” from low-commitment actions (alignment, frames, grips, step forcing) into higher-commitment actions (rotations, under-entries, inversions). Committing early is treated as a governance violation.

## 4.54 Conversion Rule (When You Get the Reaction, You Must Convert)
If you force a post, a big stance change, or a predictable retreat, you must convert immediately into either:
- entry, or
- improved connection + angle that makes the next reaction inevitable.
Letting the opponent “repair step” without consequence resets the exchange.

## 4.55 Re-Grip Discipline (Win the Second Grip Battle)
Most exchanges are not won on the first grip; they are won on the re-grip after stripping. The system prioritizes re-attachment speed and redundancy over “perfect initial grips.”

## 4.56 Primary Threat vs Secondary Threat
Always maintain one primary threat that shapes behavior (usually leg access), then cycle secondary threats that punish the defense. This keeps your offense coherent and prevents random technique switching.

## 4.57 The “No Free Turn-Away” Rule
If the opponent turns away to disengage or backstep, treat it as an actionable event: follow, occupy space, and re-establish control before distance returns. Allowing clean turn-away resets is treated as a major leak.

## 4.58 The “Compression vs Extension” Dial
Many failures are simply using the wrong dial setting:
- too much extension creates space and strips,
- too much compression kills your own mobility and delays re-insertion.
The skill is adjusting compression/extension to keep connection while preserving your ability to move.

## 4.59 Visibility Rule (See the Hips, Feel the Feet)
When confused, default to:
- visual focus on hips/shoulders for alignment truth,
- tactile focus on feet/weight for step truth.
This improves decision speed under scramble conditions.

## 4.60 The “One Beat Ahead” Standard
You should act one beat ahead of the passer’s chain:
- do not wait for the angle to be fully formed,
- do not wait for penetration to be obvious,
- deny on the first cue and convert immediately.

## 4.61 Control Without Fatigue (Skeletal, Not Muscular)
Sustainable guard depends on skeletal structure (frames, wedges, alignment) rather than maximal muscular pushing/pulling. If a position requires constant maximal effort, it is not a system position.

## 4.62 Common Leak: Over-Chasing the Finish
Chasing finishes before stabilization re-opens exits and produces resets. The doctrine is: stabilize in a hub, suppress the main exit, then finish. If exits are live, prioritize suppression over submission mechanics.

## 4.63 System Consistency (Same Ideas Across Positions)
The same governance applies across all guard families:
- create durable connection,
- deny the pass-build chain,
- force steps/posts,
- enter on proof,
- stabilize in a hub,
- prevent reset.
Different guards are different surfaces; the system is the same engine.

## 4.64 Coach Cues (Operational Language)
Use cues that map to the model:
- “Deny the link” (which requirement is being built?)
- “Force the step” (where must weight go next?)
- “Hands down” (is base compromised?)
- “Stabilize the hub” (are exits suppressed?)
- “No reset” (follow disengagement immediately)

# 5.0 System Overview

## 5.1 Operating Loop (The Default Cycle)
Open guard is executed as a closed loop with explicit conversion points:

**Read → Connect → Deny pass-build → Create base compromise → Enter → Stabilize → Suppress exits → Finish → Recycle**

The loop is designed to be re-run repeatedly without losing structural integrity. The goal is not a single “perfect” exchange; it is repeatable advantage creation.

## 5.2 The Three Reads (Context Selection)
Every exchange begins by selecting the correct action family from three filters:
- **Kneeling:** shoulders vs hips.
- **Standing:** heels vs toes.
- **Head height:** head high favors going under/elevation; head low/hips back shifts preference toward coming up/go-behind.

These reads prevent random technique switching and keep the system coherent under scramble.

## 5.3 Connection Layer (Inputs That Make Everything Work)
Connection is treated as the system’s primary input and must be rebuilt continuously.
- Build **redundant attachments** (hands + legs) so stripping one point does not collapse the structure.
- Prefer **handles** that steer posture and steps, not grips that only “hold.”
- Manage **tempo** through grip timing: you want the opponent reacting to attachment problems, not freely initiating pass-build.

## 5.4 Retention Layer (The Pass-Build Requirements Chain)
Retention is executed as requirements denial, using the passer’s chain:

**Distance → Grip → Angle → Level → Penetration → Pin**

Operational method:
- Identify the link being built *now*.
- Deny that link with the smallest effective correction.
- Immediately re-center and re-attach to prevent rebuild.

The system prefers early denial (distance/grip/angle) because late denial (penetration/pin) is expensive and volatile.

## 5.5 Base Compromise (The Validity Gate for Entries)
Entries are governed by a proof condition: you enter when the opponent’s base is measurably compromised.
Common proof conditions:
- **Posting / hands allocated to base**
- **Forced step / predictable step map**
- **Collapse or over-commitment**
- **Predictable retreat patterns** (backstep/flee) that you can follow and punish

If proof is absent, the system returns you to: connect → deny → force step/post.

## 5.6 Entry Families (How the System Chooses “What to Do”)
Entries are grouped by posture and range to keep selection simple:

**A) Standing distance entries**
- 2-on-1 ankle/leg access
- distance breakers that force steps and expose legs

**B) Kneeling entries**
- single/double/reverse-double style entries
- conversions that consolidate control (cross-catch style controls, hip-pin style controls)

**C) Bridge positions (conversion platforms)**
- X-guard pathways
- RDLR pathways
- headquarters/split-squat responses

**D) Clamp entries**
- DLR entries when alignment and outside control are reliable
- closed guard entries when clamps and posture disruption are reliable

The principle: pick the family that matches the read; do not force a family that contradicts posture and weight.

## 5.7 Stabilization Hubs (Entry Is Access; Hub Is Control)
After entry, the system routes into a small set of hubs because they suppress exits and make progression repeatable.
Hub selection logic:
- Choose the hub that best denies the opponent’s *preferred exit* from that moment (turn-away, backstep, flee, heavy-leg/high-leg clears).
- If exits remain live, treat the position as “access” and prioritize stabilization before finishing.

Typical hub buckets:
- **Inside-control hubs** (strong forward barrier; stable connection)
- **50–50 hubs** (contain turn-away; predictable finishing lanes)
- **Double-control hubs** (tighter multi-leg containment)
- **Cross-control hubs** (improve control during rotation and scrambling)

## 5.8 Anti-Exit Governance (No Reset Is a Requirement)
The opponent’s primary defense is separation. The system treats reset prevention as part of finishing, not a separate topic.
Key governance rules:
- **Follow disengagement immediately** (do not admire the retreat).
- **Occupy space as it opens** (gap management).
- **Re-attach faster than they can re-square** (re-grip discipline).
- Treat backstep/flee as triggers for conversion, not neutral events.

## 5.9 Conversion Rules (Where Exchanges Are Won)
There are four mandatory conversions:
1) **Denial → Threat:** after breaking a pass-build link, present immediate offense.
2) **Reaction → Entry:** after forcing a post/step, enter while the base is compromised.
3) **Entry → Hub:** after gaining access, stabilize before chasing.
4) **Hub → Finish:** suppress the primary exit, then finish.

If any conversion is skipped, the exchange typically resets.

## 5.10 Diagnostics (When the System Fails)
Use the loop to locate the leak:
- **Getting passed early:** denial is late (distance/grip/angle is being conceded).
- **Entries feel “stuffed”:** proof condition is missing (base not compromised) or connection is fragile.
- **You enter but lose legs:** hub selection/stabilization is incomplete (exits still live).
- **You control but cannot finish:** anti-exit is weak (opponent resets distance) or threat stacking is absent.

The fix is almost always upstream: improve connection redundancy, deny earlier in the chain, or force a clearer step/post before entering.

## 5.11 System Inputs and Outputs (What You Control vs What You Create)
**Inputs you can directly control**
- Attachment quality (redundancy, handle strength)
- Alignment (square vs angled, hip line safety)
- Tempo (when the opponent must react)
- Denial timing (early vs late in the chain)

**Outputs you are trying to create**
- Forced step map (narrow step options)
- Posting/hands-down moments
- Predictable retreats (backstep/flee) that are punishable
- Stable hub control with exits suppressed

## 5.12 Priority Stack (What Comes First Under Pressure)
When exchanges speed up, apply a fixed priority stack:
1) **Hip line safety** (avoid penetration)
2) **Distance + frames** (buy time and space)
3) **Connection rebuild** (handles + redundancy)
4) **Angle/level correction** (restore alignment)
5) **Threat re-application** (force reaction)
6) **Entry on proof**
7) **Hub stabilization**
8) **Finish via anti-exit**

If you attempt (6–8) without (1–5), volatility is expected.

## 5.13 Micro-Cycle: The Denial Loop (How to “Stay in Guard”)
Retention is not a single event; it is repeated micro-cycles:
- **Detect** the active pass-build link
- **Deny** with the smallest correction
- **Re-center** hips/shoulders
- **Reinsert** legs/frames
- **Re-attach** grips/handles
- **Convert** immediately into threat

This is the engine that prevents “one denial then collapse.”

## 5.14 Step-Forcing Toolkit (Why Base Compromise Appears)
Base compromise is primarily created by step forcing. Operationally, step forcing comes from:
- steering posture with handles (pull/push to shift weight),
- blocking one foot while directing weight to the other,
- forcing the opponent to clear an obstruction (your frames/legs),
- presenting threat that compels defensive stepping.

If no step is forced, the opponent retains full mobility and entries tend to fail.

## 5.15 “Proof Conditions” Checklist (Entry Readiness)
Treat entries as gated actions. You should see at least one of:
- Opponent’s hands occupied by base (post/brace)
- Weight committed to a single leg (clear heel/toe bias)
- Stance widened or narrowed in a predictable way
- Hips misaligned relative to shoulders (structural tilt)
- A retreat pattern beginning (backstep/flee) that you can follow
- Grip battle decisively won (your handle survives stripping)

If none are present, return to: connect → deny → force step.

## 5.16 Routing Rules (If/Then Decision Shortcuts)
Use fast routing rather than “choosing techniques”:
- **If kneeling + shoulders driving forward:** prioritize frames, re-center, create hands-down, then kneeling entries.
- **If standing + heels loaded:** elevation and under-entries become higher value.
- **If standing + toes loaded / hips back:** come-up/go-behind options increase; avoid low-percentage under-rotations.
- **If head low/hips back:** treat as an “up mode” problem until posture changes.
- **If opponent turns away/backsteps:** follow immediately and convert; do not reset at distance.

## 5.17 Hub Selection Criteria (Choosing the Right “Landing Spot”)
Pick hubs by exit suppression, not preference:
- Opponent trying to **turn away** → choose a hub that contains rotation.
- Opponent trying to **backstep** → choose a hub that controls the far leg and blocks hip rotation.
- Opponent trying to **flee** → choose a hub with forward barrier + re-attachment readiness.
- Opponent clearing **heavy leg/high leg** → choose a hub that maintains multi-point leg connection.

If the hub does not suppress the opponent’s current exit, treat it as transitional, not stable.

## 5.18 Anti-Exit as a Finishing Funnel
Finishing is best understood as a funnel:
**Exit suppression → re-attachment on escape attempt → tighter hub → repeat**
The opponent is expected to attempt resets multiple times. The system wins by making each reset attempt fail faster than the last.

## 5.19 Metrics (How to Know the System is Working)
You can measure system quality with simple metrics:
- Time to establish first durable connection
- Frequency of early denials (distance/grip/angle) vs late denials (penetration/pin)
- Rate of forced steps/posts per minute
- Percentage of entries initiated with proof conditions present
- Time from entry to stable hub (exit suppression achieved)
- Number of successful opponent resets (should trend toward zero)

## 5.20 Common System Leaks (Fast Diagnosis)
- **Leak: fragile connection** → grips peeled, legs late → fix redundancy and re-grip discipline.
- **Leak: late denial** → constantly defending penetration/pin → fix early detection and priority stack.
- **Leak: hope-based entries** → stuffed entries → enforce proof conditions and step forcing.
- **Leak: unstable hubs** → lose legs after entry → improve hub selection and stabilization before chasing.
- **Leak: resets** → opponent repeatedly disengages → strengthen follow/occupy-space rules.

## 5.21 Minimal Operational Checklist (In-Roll Cue Set)
Use this cue set as the “in-roll” checklist:
- “What are they building in the chain?”
- “Can I force a step or a post?”
- “Do I have proof to enter?”
- “Did I stabilize the hub?”
- “Did I suppress the primary exit?”
- “If they try to leave, do I follow or do I reset?”

## 8.3 Standing Passer: Toes-Loaded (Driving In / Forward Pressure)

- Deny penetration attempts early (angle + level).
- Frame and re-center; force hands-down reactions.
- Prefer “up mode” conversions when posture resists under entries.
- Route into hubs that punish forward drive and suppress flee.

## 8.4 Kneeling Passer: Shoulders Leading (Pressure Initiation)
Objective: protect hip line, break their build, enter kneeling family on proof.
- Read shoulders vs hips.
- Deny level + penetration early; re-center before pin forms.
- Force posting/step as they drive.
- Enter: single/double/reverse-double family as proof appears.
- Consolidate via cross-catch/hip-pin structures.

## 8.5 Kneeling Passer: Hips Back (Defensive Kneel / Hard to Pull Under)
Objective: create base compromise first; do not over-rotate.
- Build handles that pull posture forward or force a post.
- Threat stack to create step/post proof.
- Enter kneeling family only after proof; otherwise recycle to threat.

## 8.6 Mobility-Based Passing (Run-Around / Angle Chasing)
Objective: deny angle creation and re-square immediately.
- Treat angle as the active link; deny early.
- Use movement vocabulary to re-center (foot pummels/crossovers).
- Rebuild connection faster than their lateral step.
- Force a step map; enter on proof.

## 8.7 Pressure-Based Passing (Stack / Body Lock / Knee Cut Attempts)
Objective: prevent late-chain completion (penetration → pin).
- Use frames as structure, not pushing.
- Triage: hip line safety → distance/frames → reinsert legs.
- Deny pin formation; re-center and immediately convert to threat.
- Enter once they are forced to post/adjust.

## 8.8 Headquarters / Split-Squat (HQ) Engagement
Objective: treat HQ as a routing point into leg entries.
- Identify split-squat posture and weight allocation.
- Deny angle + penetration; force step adjustments.
- Enter using HQ-specific pathways.
- Stabilize in hub that prevents backstep and turn-away.

## 8.9 Reverse De La Riva (RDLR) Scenarios
Objective: use RDLR as a bridge into stable leg control under passing pressure.
- Maintain connection while denying grip/angle.
- Create proof via forced step or retreat trigger.
- Enter RDLR pathways; consolidate; route to hub.

## 8.10 De La Riva (DLR) Scenarios
Objective: use DLR when outside control and alignment are reliable.
- Maintain outside control; prevent easy disengagement.
- Step-force to create proof.
- Convert to entry; consolidate; suppress flee/backstep.

## 8.11 Closed Guard Entry Scenarios
Objective: clamp when posture and alignment allow reliable closure and control.
- Use connection and posture disruption to close distance.
- Transition to closed guard entries as a governed option (not a default reset).
- Convert to the intended follow-up pathway; prevent separation on opening attempts.

## 8.12 Anti-Exit Scenarios (Reset Prevention as an Application Layer)
Objective: treat exits as triggers and recycle into control.
- Backstep attempt → follow and route to hub that blocks hip rotation.
- Flee attempt → occupy space and re-attach to prevent distance.
- Heavy-leg/high-leg clears → re-establish multi-point connection and re-stabilize.
- Turn-away attempt → contain rotation (50–50 style routing) and progress.

## 8.13 Integration Scenario: “Deny → Threat → Proof → Entry → Hub”
Objective: demonstrate the full system in one repeatable chain.
- Deny the active pass-build link (early if possible).
- Apply constant threat to force a step/post.
- Enter on proof using the appropriate family.
- Consolidate and route to the correct hub.
- Suppress the primary exit; progress to finish; recycle if reset is attempted.

## 8.14 Scenario Routing Template (Use This for Any Look)
For any scenario, run the same routing card:
- Read: kneeling (shoulders/hips) OR standing (heels/toes) + head height.
- Identify active pass-chain link: Distance / Grip / Angle / Level / Penetration / Pin.
- Deny: smallest correction that breaks the link and restores alignment.
- Force proof: step/post/collapse/retreat trigger.
- Enter: posture/range-appropriate family.
- Consolidate: add redundancy immediately.
- Stabilize: choose hub by the exit they are attempting.
- Anti-exit: follow disengagement; deny separation.

## 8.15 Standing: “Long Range / Hands Fighting”
Active problem: distance + grip battle.
- Deny: stop free distance management; re-attach with redundant handles.
- Force proof: create step map via steering grips (stance change / forced step).
- Enter: standing distance family once weight commits.
- Hub: select containment that prevents turn-away.

## 8.16 Standing: “Fast Strip / Re-Grip Exchange”
Active problem: grip as the link (opponent trying to remove your steering).
- Deny: win the second grip battle (re-grip discipline).
- Force proof: make them post/step as they strip (punish the strip moment).
- Enter: standing distance or bridge family depending on posture.
- Hub: stabilize immediately; do not chase finishes during re-grip volatility.

## 8.17 Standing: “Retreat to Reset Distance”
Active problem: distance re-established by retreat (reset attempt).
- Deny: treat retreat as a trigger; follow and re-attach.
- Force proof: occupy space and force a step/post as they retreat.
- Enter: standing distance family on predictable step placement.
- Hub: forward-barrier oriented stabilization to prevent repeat resets.

## 8.18 Standing: “Turn-Away to Disengage”
Active problem: exit attempt (anti-exit layer).
- Deny: no free rotation; follow the turn.
- Force proof: deny the clean pivot; keep multi-point connection.
- Enter: route to containment-friendly hub selection.
- Hub: prioritize turn-away containment over immediate finishing.

## 8.19 Kneeling: “Inside Pressure / Head Low”
Active problem: level + penetration threat (late chain risk).
- Deny: frame structurally; re-center hips/shoulders before pin forms.
- Force proof: make them post or adjust base while pressure is forward.
- Enter: kneeling entry family when posting/step appears.
- Consolidate: immediate consolidation to prevent scramble escape.

## 8.20 Kneeling: “Base Wide / Hips Back”
Active problem: difficulty creating proof (opponent refuses commitment).
- Deny: maintain connection without over-rotation.
- Force proof: threat stack to force step narrowing or a post.
- Enter: kneeling family only after proof; otherwise recycle.

## 8.21 Mobility Passing: “Angle Chase”
Active problem: angle is the link.
- Deny: re-square early; stop the lateral step from linking to level/penetration.
- Force proof: interrupt steps until their base compromises.
- Enter: bridge or standing distance family once the step map narrows.
- Hub: choose stabilization that prevents continued lateral circling.

## 8.22 Mobility Passing: “Backstep as Primary Weapon”
Active problem: retreat/backstep exit attempt.
- Deny: follow immediately; do not reset to long range.
- Force proof: occupy space; force a predictable step after the backstep.
- Enter: route into the family that best punishes hip rotation and retreat.
- Hub: select hub that blocks repeat backsteps.

## 8.23 Pressure Passing: “Stack Compression”
Active problem: pin formation is imminent (late chain).
- Deny: preserve frames as structure; restore distance line first.
- Force proof: as they adjust to maintain pressure, look for post/step.
- Enter: convert immediately when pressure requires them to post.
- Hub: stabilize before attempting finishes; resets are common here.

## 8.24 Pressure Passing: “Body Lock Approach”
Active problem: penetration + pin setup.
- Deny: protect hip line; prevent arms/torso lock completion.
- Force proof: make them widen base or post to keep balance.
- Enter: route to kneeling/bridge family depending on posture.
- Hub: stabilization that denies them returning to chest-to-hip control.

## 8.25 Knee Cut Threat: “Angle + Level Link”
Active problem: angle linking into level (knee line exposure).
- Deny: kill angle early; restore square alignment.
- Force proof: force a corrective step.
- Enter: convert during the repair step.
- Hub: choose stabilization that prevents re-entry to the same lane.

## 8.26 Headquarters / Split Squat: “Split Base Control Problem”
Active problem: penetration lane created by split stance.
- Deny: stop angle completion; keep hip line safe.
- Force proof: create a predictable step (narrow/widen) from the split base.
- Enter: HQ response family on weight commitment.
- Hub: prioritize suppression of backstep and turn-away.

## 8.27 RDLR: “Pressure + Angle Management”
Active problem: grip/angle pressure while staying connected.
- Deny: keep connection through stripping attempts; deny angle completion.
- Force proof: leverage their step attempts to create entry proof.
- Enter: RDLR pathways; consolidate immediately.
- Hub: stabilization that survives rotation and stripping.

## 8.28 DLR: “Outside Control to Entry”
Active problem: maintaining outside control while preventing disengagement.
- Deny: prevent easy peel/step-away reset.
- Force proof: force a step map with outside control intact.
- Enter: DLR pathways on weight commitment.
- Hub: stabilization that blocks flee/backstep behaviors.

## 8.29 Closed Guard: “Clamp as a Governed Option”
Active problem: managing distance when clamps become available.
- Deny: do not chase movement at long range; close distance with control.
- Force proof: posture disruption that prevents immediate stand-out.
- Enter: closed guard entry pathways when clamp is reliable.
- Hub: treat clamp as control; prevent separation on opening attempts.

## 8.30 System Stress Test Scenario (Worst-Case)
Goal: validate the loop under poor conditions (late denial + grip loss).
- Start from a compromised state (late chain: level/penetration pressure).
- Apply priority stack: hip line safety → distance/frames → re-attach.
- Force proof on their repair step.
- Enter and stabilize; anti-exit on first reset attempt.

# 9.0 Drilling Progressions

## 9.1 Progression Architecture (How Drills Are Built)
All drills follow the same scaffold:
- Start state (posture/range and grips)
- Task (what layer you are training)
- Constraint (what is not allowed; prevents “cheating”)
- Success metric (objective outcome)
- Reset rule (when to restart vs continue)

Primary layers drilled in order:
**Connection → Denial → Proof creation → Entry → Consolidation → Hub stabilization → Anti-exit**

## 9.2 Phase 1: Connection Acquisition (Low Resistance)
**Drill 9.2A — Four-Limb Connection Build**
- Start: neutral open guard, no grips.
- Task: establish a stable 4-limb connection (hands + legs) with redundancy.
- Constraint: no entries; no sweeps; only connect and steer.
- Success: opponent is forced to react (stance change / posture adjustment / step).

**Drill 9.2B — 2-on-1 Handle Series**
- Start: neutral open guard; passer has light strip intent.
- Task: establish 2-on-1; maintain through one strip attempt; re-grip.
- Constraint: no entry; objective is survival and steering.
- Success: 2-on-1 re-established within one exchange beat.

## 9.3 Phase 2: Retention Denial (Pass-Chain Recognition)
**Drill 9.3A — Identify the Active Link**
- Start: passer begins with one deliberate build (distance OR grip OR angle).
- Task: call the link out (internally) and deny it.
- Constraint: guard player may only use one denial action per rep (forces precision).
- Success: link broken before it chains forward.

**Drill 9.3B — Chain Denial Ladder**
- Start: passer alternates building links in order: distance → grip → angle → level…
- Task: deny each link early; re-center after every denial.
- Constraint: no counter-offense until after denial is achieved.
- Success: deny 3 consecutive links without conceding penetration.

## 9.4 Phase 3: Proof Creation (Step/ पोस्ट Generation)
**Drill 9.4A — Force the Step Map**
- Start: standing passer, neutral stance.
- Task: force a predictable step (narrow/widen/shift weight) using handles.
- Constraint: no entry until step is forced.
- Success: visible forced step occurs within 10 seconds.

**Drill 9.4B — Hands-Down Trigger**
- Start: standing or kneeling passer, moderate pressure.
- Task: create a posting moment (hands allocated to base).
- Constraint: once hands-down occurs, you must immediately convert (next drill phase).
- Success: hands-down achieved and recognized.

## 9.5 Phase 4: Entry on Proof (Validity-Gated Entries)
**Drill 9.5A — Proof-Gated Entry Reps**
- Start: run Phase 3 until proof appears.
- Task: enter only when proof is present.
- Constraint: if you enter without proof, rep is invalid and restarts.
- Success: clean entry initiated within 2 seconds of proof appearance.

**Drill 9.5B — Posture Split: Standing vs Kneeling Entry Selection**
- Start: partner alternates rounds as standing/kneeling.
- Task: select correct entry family based on posture read.
- Constraint: one entry attempt per rep; must be posture-appropriate.
- Success: correct family selection 8/10 reps.

## 9.6 Phase 5: Consolidation (Entry → Control)
**Drill 9.6A — “Second Connection” Rule**
- Start: successful entry access achieved.
- Task: add a second connection immediately (redundancy) before progressing.
- Constraint: no finish attempts until redundancy is established.
- Success: redundancy achieved without losing access.

**Drill 9.6B — Cross-Catch / Hip-Pin Consolidation (Kneeling Focus)**
- Start: kneeling entry achieved.
- Task: consolidate into the intended control structure.
- Constraint: passer’s only goal is to scramble out.
- Success: maintain control for a 5-second count.

## 9.7 Phase 6: Hub Stabilization (Choose by Exit)
**Drill 9.7A — Hub Selection by Opponent Exit**
- Start: entry achieved; passer chooses one exit (turn-away OR backstep OR flee).
- Task: route to the hub that suppresses that exit.
- Constraint: guard player cannot chase finish; only stabilize.
- Success: exit suppressed for 10 seconds.

**Drill 9.7B — Hub Hold Under Incremental Resistance**
- Start: in hub position.
- Task: hold while resistance escalates every 10 seconds.
- Constraint: no reset to neutral; must solve inside the hub.
- Success: maintain for 30 seconds total without separation.

## 9.8 Phase 7: Anti-Exit (No Reset Drills)
**Drill 9.8A — Backstep Follow**
- Start: opponent initiates backstep disengagement.
- Task: follow immediately and re-attach before distance is regained.
- Constraint: no “restart at long range” allowed.
- Success: re-attachment achieved within 2 seconds.

**Drill 9.8B — Flee Follow**
- Start: opponent attempts to run away/reset stance.
- Task: occupy space and re-attach through the retreat.
- Constraint: if distance fully resets, rep fails.
- Success: maintain contact and control through retreat.

## 9.9 Integration Rounds (Full System)
**Drill 9.9A — Loop Rounds (2-Min Cycles)**
- Start: neutral open guard.
- Task: run the entire loop; recycle as needed.
- Constraint: entries must be proof-gated.
- Success metrics:
  - forced step/post created at least once per minute,
  - at least one proof-gated entry per round,
  - zero clean resets by opponent.

**Drill 9.9B — Scenario Rounds (Standing/Kneeling/HQ/RDLR/DLR)**
- Start: predetermined scenario.
- Task: apply routing card: deny link → force proof → enter → hub → anti-exit.
- Constraint: stay inside the scenario for the whole round.
- Success: maintain system coherence (no random technique switching).

## 9.10 Testing Benchmarks (Objective Targets)
- Connection: establish a durable handle within 5 seconds from neutral.
- Denial: prevent penetration/pin for 60 seconds against progressive passing.
- Proof: force at least 3 clear steps/posts in a 2-minute round.
- Entry: achieve 3 proof-gated entries in 5 minutes.
- Hub: hold a stabilized hub for 20 seconds against full resistance.
- Anti-exit: prevent 5 consecutive reset attempts (backstep/flee) from succeeding.

## 9.11 Resistance Scaling (How to Progress Any Drill)
Use a consistent scaling ladder so drills evolve without becoming random:
- Level 1: compliant (pattern learning)
- Level 2: semi-resistant (single defensive goal, e.g., strip only)
- Level 3: progressive (defender adds one more option every rep)
- Level 4: live (full resistance) with one constraint (proof-gated entries)
- Level 5: adversarial (live + opponent starts in advantage, e.g., late-chain pressure)

## 9.12 Timing Rules (Training the Transition Window)
**Drill 9.12A — “Repair Step” Conversion**
- Start: passer begins to rebuild after a denied link.
- Task: convert during the repair step (threat or entry).
- Constraint: you may only act *during* the repair step window.
- Success: conversion occurs before stance re-stabilizes.

**Drill 9.12B — Two-Second Rule**
- Start: proof condition appears (post/step/collapse).
- Task: initiate entry within 2 seconds.
- Constraint: if >2 seconds, rep is invalid.
- Success: consistent fast conversion on proof.

## 9.13 Chain Integrity Drills (Preventing “Two Links at Once”)
**Drill 9.13A — One-Link Denial Under Chaos**
- Start: passer is allowed to build any one link at a time, but changes rapidly.
- Task: deny the active link with minimal motion.
- Constraint: you may not switch to offense until denial is achieved.
- Success: deny 5 consecutive link attempts.

**Drill 9.13B — Two-Link Punishment**
- Start: passer attempts to connect two links (e.g., distance+angle).
- Task: recognize the pairing and break the earlier link.
- Constraint: no late-chain recovery tactics allowed.
- Success: stop the pair before penetration appears.

## 9.14 Standing Distance Block (2-on-1 Ankle / Distance Breakers)
**Drill 9.14A — Standing Entry Gate**
- Start: standing passer; you begin with a handle.
- Task: force step map then enter (proof-gated).
- Constraint: passer’s goal is to strip and retreat only.
- Success: entry achieved without losing connection.

**Drill 9.14B — Anti-Retreat Loop**
- Start: standing passer repeatedly retreats to reset.
- Task: follow and re-attach; force new proof; enter again.
- Constraint: no long-range reset allowed for either partner.
- Success: 3 consecutive cycles without full reset.

## 9.15 Kneeling Entry Block (Single/Double/Reverse Double)
**Drill 9.15A — Kneeling Read to Entry**
- Start: kneeling passer; shoulders/hips read is obvious.
- Task: select kneeling entry family and execute on proof.
- Constraint: passer’s only defense is to keep base stable (no counter-attacks).
- Success: clean entry selection and initiation.

**Drill 9.15B — Consolidation Hold**
- Start: entry access achieved.
- Task: consolidate to control structure and hold.
- Constraint: passer’s only goal is to disengage (scramble out).
- Success: 10-second control hold.

## 9.16 Bridge Position Block (X-Guard / RDLR / HQ)
**Drill 9.16A — Bridge Conversion Reps**
- Start: you are placed into bridge position with minimal connection.
- Task: rebuild connection → force proof → route to hub.
- Constraint: no finish attempts; only stabilize.
- Success: hub achieved without separation.

**Drill 9.16B — HQ / Split-Squat Reaction Drill**
- Start: opponent enters HQ posture.
- Task: deny angle + penetration; force step; enter on proof.
- Constraint: opponent must attempt backstep as primary escape.
- Success: backstep suppressed and hub stabilized.

## 9.17 DLR / Closed Guard Block (Clamp Governance)
**Drill 9.17A — DLR Entry Under Strip Pressure**
- Start: DLR alignment established; passer attempts to peel and step away.
- Task: maintain outside control; force step map; enter on proof.
- Constraint: no chasing if contact breaks; must re-attach first.
- Success: entry without losing outside control.

**Drill 9.17B — Closed Guard Transition Governance**
- Start: open guard with posture disrupted enough to close.
- Task: close only when clamp is valid; convert to intended follow-up.
- Constraint: if clamp is loose, you must abandon and re-attach in open guard.
- Success: clean closure and controlled progression.

## 9.18 Anti-Exit Micro-Drills (Backstep / Flee / Leg Clears)
**Drill 9.18A — Backstep Re-Entry Ladder**
- Start: opponent attempts backstep 5 times in a row.
- Task: follow, re-attach, and re-stabilize each time.
- Constraint: no finish attempts; only reset prevention.
- Success: 5/5 re-attachments without distance reset.

**Drill 9.18B — Heavy-Leg / High-Leg Clears**
- Start: opponent repeatedly attempts to clear the controlled leg.
- Task: maintain multi-point connection; re-stabilize hub.
- Constraint: you may not switch to a new family; must solve in-hub.
- Success: suppress clears for 20 seconds.

## 9.19 Round Structures (How to Run Live Without Losing the System)
**Format A — Proof-Gated Sparring**
- Rule: any entry attempted without proof is a restart.
- Purpose: eliminates “hope entries,” forces step/post creation.

**Format B — Layered Rounds**
- Round 1: connection only
- Round 2: denial only
- Round 3: proof + entry only
- Round 4: hub + anti-exit only
- Round 5: full integration
Purpose: isolates leaks and prevents “everything at once” chaos.

## 9.20 Coaching Cues (Short Prompts During Drills)
- “Which link are they building?”
- “Force the step.”
- “Proof first.”
- “Second connection.”
- “Stabilize the hub.”
- “No reset.”
- “Convert on the repair step.”

# 10.0 Four-Week Curriculum

## 10.1 Curriculum Design (How the 4 Weeks Build)
The curriculum is layered to match the system:
- Week 1: connection + retention (build the base)
- Week 2: proof creation + entries (convert defense to offense)
- Week 3: consolidation + hubs (make access become control)
- Week 4: anti-exit + integration (finish the loop)

Each week uses the same class template:
- Concept block (10–15 min)
- Technical block (20–30 min)
- Isolation rounds (10–15 min)
- Live rounds with constraints (15–25 min)

## 10.2 Week 1 — Connection and Retention (Knowledge/Denial)
Primary goal: establish durable handles and deny pass-build early.

Key concepts:
- Four-limb connection and redundancy
- Handles (steer, don’t hold)
- Pass-build chain: Distance → Grip → Angle → Level → Penetration → Pin
- Early denial and re-centering

Technical/drill blocks:
- Connection acquisition (four-limb + 2-on-1)
- Re-grip discipline (win second grip battle)
- Chain denial ladder (deny distance/grip/angle)
- Demarcation movement vocabulary (re-center + reinsert legs)

Constraints for live:
- No entries allowed (forces connection and denial quality)
- Scoring: 1 point for early denial; 2 points for denial + re-center without scramble

## 10.3 Week 2 — Proof Creation and Entry Families
Primary goal: manufacture entry windows (step/post) and enter only on proof.

Key concepts:
- Proof conditions: post/hands-down, forced step, collapse, predictable retreat
- Step forcing as the universal trigger
- Standing vs kneeling selection (posture/range families)

Technical/drill blocks:
- Step-map drills (force predictable steps)
- Hands-down trigger drills (create posting)
- Proof-gated entry reps (standing distance entries; kneeling entry family)
- Posture split selection rounds (standing one round, kneeling next)

Constraints for live:
- Any entry without proof = restart
- Scoring: 1 point for forcing proof; 2 points for proof-gated entry initiation

## 10.4 Week 3 — Consolidation and Hub Stabilization
Primary goal: convert entries into stable control and suppress exits.

Key concepts:
- Entry is access; hub is control
- Second connection rule (add redundancy immediately)
- Hub selection by opponent exit (turn-away/backstep/flee/leg clears)

Technical/drill blocks:
- Consolidation reps (cross-catch / hip-pin emphasis for kneeling outcomes)
- Hub routing rounds (partner declares exit intent)
- Hub holds under incremental resistance
- Bridge platform work (X-guard/RDLR/HQ as conversion platforms)

Constraints for live:
- No finishes allowed (forces stabilization)
- Scoring: 1 point for consolidation; 2 points for 10-second stabilized hub

## 10.5 Week 4 — Anti-Exit and Full Integration
Primary goal: prevent reset and run the entire loop under resistance.

Key concepts:
- No-free-reset governance (follow disengagement)
- Backstep/flee as triggers, not neutral events
- Conversion rules: denial→threat, reaction→entry, entry→hub, hub→finish

Technical/drill blocks:
- Anti-exit micro-drills (backstep follow, flee follow, heavy/high leg clears)
- Loop rounds (2-minute cycles, proof-gated entries)
- Scenario rounds (standing, kneeling, HQ, RDLR, DLR/closed guard)
- Stress-test starts (begin from late-chain pressure; recover to loop)

Constraints for live:
- Proof-gated entries remain mandatory
- Reset prevention is scored (opponent reset = point against you)
- Goal metric: at least one full loop per round (deny→proof→entry→hub→anti-exit)

## 10.6 Weekly Benchmark Targets (Simple Performance Metrics)
Week 1:
- establish durable connection within 5 seconds from neutral
- deny distance/grip/angle without conceding penetration for 60 seconds

Week 2:
- force 3 clear steps/posts in a 2-minute round
- initiate entries within 2 seconds of proof appearance

Week 3:
- add second connection after entry reliably
- stabilize a hub for 20 seconds against full resistance

Week 4:
- prevent 5 consecutive reset attempts from succeeding
- execute at least one complete loop per round under live resistance

# 11.0 Failure Patterns

## 11.1 Failure Pattern Map (Where Breakdowns Occur)
Most failures are not “one mistake.” They are a broken layer or a skipped conversion:
- Connection failure
- Denial failure (late recognition)
- Proof failure (hope-based entries)
- Consolidation/hub failure (access without control)
- Anti-exit failure (reset allowed)
- Conversion failure (denial not converted to threat; proof not converted to entry)

## 11.2 Connection Failures
**FP-11.2A — Moving Without Attachment**
- Symptom: you move, opponent steps away, you lose contact and initiative.
- Cause: insufficient handles / no redundancy.
- Fix focus: four-limb connection; re-attach discipline.

**FP-11.2B — Single-Point Grip Collapse**
- Symptom: one grip strip collapses the whole structure.
- Cause: lack of redundancy; slow re-grip.
- Fix focus: “second connection” habit; win the second grip battle.

**FP-11.2C — No Steering (Grips That Only Hold)**
- Symptom: you have grips but cannot force steps or posture reactions.
- Cause: grips not used as handles (no direction).
- Fix focus: gripping with direction; step forcing drills.

## 11.3 Denial Failures (Pass-Chain Timing)
**FP-11.3A — Late Denial (Always Defending Penetration/Pin)**
- Symptom: constant emergency defense; frequent pin formation.
- Cause: distance/grip/angle conceded early.
- Fix focus: early-link recognition; chain denial ladder.

**FP-11.3B — Denying the Wrong Link**
- Symptom: you address grip while angle is being built (or vice versa).
- Cause: poor diagnosis under speed.
- Fix focus: “identify active link” drill; one-denial-per-rep constraint.

**FP-11.3C — Two Links at Once**
- Symptom: opponent links distance+angle or grip+level and you collapse.
- Cause: slow correction; re-centering delayed.
- Fix focus: two-link punishment drill; early triage order.

## 11.4 Proof Failures (Entry Validity)
**FP-11.4A — Hope-Based Entries**
- Symptom: entries get stuffed; you lose connection and concede pass-build.
- Cause: entering without proof (stable base, hands free).
- Fix focus: proof-gated entries; step/post creation drills.

**FP-11.4B — Missing the Transition Window**
- Symptom: proof appears but you hesitate; opponent repair-steps to safety.
- Cause: slow conversion after denial/forced reaction.
- Fix focus: two-second rule; repair-step conversion drills.

**FP-11.4C — Wrong Family Selection**
- Symptom: forcing under-entries vs hips-back posture; poor match to read.
- Cause: selection not governed by posture/range.
- Fix focus: posture split selection rounds; context-law adherence.

## 11.5 Consolidation Failures (Entry → Control)
**FP-11.5A — Access Without Redundancy**
- Symptom: you touch the legs but lose them immediately.
- Cause: no second connection added after entry.
- Fix focus: second connection rule; consolidation hold rounds.

**FP-11.5B — Chasing Finish Before Stabilization**
- Symptom: you attack early; opponent exits and resets.
- Cause: hub not stabilized; exits still live.
- Fix focus: stabilization-first rounds; no-finish constraints.

## 11.6 Hub Failures (Control Layer)
**FP-11.6A — Wrong Hub for the Exit**
- Symptom: you stabilize into a position that allows the opponent’s preferred escape.
- Cause: hub chosen by habit rather than exit suppression.
- Fix focus: “partner declares exit” hub rounds; hub routing criteria.

**FP-11.6B — Hub Drift**
- Symptom: you start stable, then lose structure over time.
- Cause: compression/extension mismanaged; connection decays.
- Fix focus: hub hold under incremental resistance; structure cues.

## 11.7 Anti-Exit Failures (Reset Allowed)
**FP-11.7A — Free Backstep**
- Symptom: opponent backsteps and you reset at long range.
- Cause: failure to follow and occupy space.
- Fix focus: backstep follow micro-drills; no-reset rule.

**FP-11.7B — Free Flee**
- Symptom: opponent runs away and you lose all connection.
- Cause: failure to treat retreat as trigger.
- Fix focus: flee follow drills; re-attachment discipline.

**FP-11.7C — Leg Clear Success (Heavy/High Leg)**
- Symptom: opponent repeatedly clears the controlled leg.
- Cause: insufficient multi-point connection; weak consolidation.
- Fix focus: heavy/high leg clear response rounds; multi-point hub work.

## 11.8 Conversion Failures (Skipped System Steps)
**FP-11.8A — Denial Without Threat**
- Symptom: you defend successfully but remain neutral; opponent rebuilds.
- Cause: no immediate counter-offense after denial.
- Fix focus: denial→threat conversion reps.

**FP-11.8B — Threat Without Proof**
- Symptom: you threaten but never create step/post; exchanges stagnate.
- Cause: threat not forcing reactions (poor steering).
- Fix focus: step-forcing handle drills; predictable reaction training.

**FP-11.8C — Entry Without Hub**
- Symptom: you enter then scramble randomly.
- Cause: no consolidation/hub routing.
- Fix focus: entry→consolidation→hub chains; hub selection by exit.

## 11.9 Quick Diagnosis Table (In-Round)
- Getting passed early → connection weak or denial late (distance/grip/angle).
- Entries stuffed → missing proof or wrong family selection.
- Lose legs after entry → no redundancy or hub not stabilized.
- Opponent resets repeatedly → anti-exit failure (follow/occupy space).
- Control but no progress → threat stacking absent or exit not suppressed.

# 13.0 Terminology

- **Alignment** — the relationship of hips/shoulders to the opponent that determines whether your legs and frames can function on time.
- **Anti-exit governance** — rules and actions that prevent the opponent from resetting distance (backstep, flee, clears) during control/finishing.
- **Base compromise (proof)** — measurable instability (post/hands-down, forced step, collapse, predictable retreat) that validates committing to an entry.
- **Chain (pass-build requirements chain)** — the ordered requirements a passer must assemble: Distance → Grip → Angle → Level → Penetration → Pin.
- **Clamp** — a closed or semi-closed control that restricts posture and distance enough to prevent easy disengagement.
- **Consolidation** — adding redundancy after access so an entry becomes stable control (the “second connection” step).
- **Constant threat** — sustained credible offense that shapes opponent behavior, slows passing tempo, and forces predictable reactions.
- **Context laws (three reads)** — posture/weight filters that guide selection: shoulders/hips (kneeling), heels/toes (standing), head under/over (head height).
- **Control hub (hub)** — a stabilizing control configuration chosen to suppress a specific escape class (turn-away, backstep, flee, clears).
- **Conversion** — the mandatory transitions between layers (denial→threat, reaction→entry, entry→hub, hub→finish).
- **Demarcation** — identifying where guard is being lost (line/zone drift) so the correct recovery tool is selected early.
- **Denial** — breaking the active link in the opponent’s pass-build chain before it connects to the next link.
- **Distance breaker** — a method of preventing “step away solves it” by forcing engagement range where attachments matter.
- **Entry (valid entry)** — an access action initiated only when proof conditions indicate the opponent’s base is compromised.
- **Exit** — the opponent’s method of reset/disengagement (backstep, flee, turn-away, heavy-leg/high-leg clears).
- **Four-limb gripping** — coordinated use of hands and feet/legs to create redundant connection rather than single-point grips.
- **Frame** — a load-bearing structure that preserves space and alignment; not muscular pushing.
- **Handle** — a grip/attachment that steers posture, steps, or alignment (a control interface, not merely a hold).
- **Hands-down** — a base-compromise condition where the opponent must post/brace, reducing their ability to strip and disengage.
- **Hip line** — the critical boundary for penetration and pin risk; protecting it is a primary safety gate.
- **Initiative** — having connection and alignment such that the opponent must respond to your structure, not freely build the pass chain.
- **Knowledge/denial** — the retention model: diagnose the requirement being built and deny it, rather than defending pass names.
- **Layer** — one functional band of the system (connection, denial, proof creation, entry, consolidation, hub, anti-exit).
- **Loop** — the repeatable system cycle: read → connect → deny → force proof → enter → consolidate → hub → anti-exit → finish → recycle.
- **Mode (under vs up)** — two progression modes: go under/elevate when posture allows, or come up/go-behind when posture resists under-entries.
- **Posting** — allocating hands to base to prevent falling; a key proof condition and entry trigger.
- **Proof condition** — see base compromise; the gating signal for committing to entries.
- **Re-attach** — restoring durable connection after grips/legs are cleared, prior to chasing movement.
- **Re-center** — restoring alignment and distance so legs/frames can function before advancing.
- **Repair step** — the opponent’s stabilizing step after a denial; a prime timing window for conversion.
- **Retention** — maintaining guard by denying pass-build requirements and re-centering; treated as the first skill.
- **Routing** — selection by rules (reads + proof) rather than ad hoc technique choice.
- **Second connection rule** — after entry access, immediately add another attachment to create redundancy before progressing.
- **Step map** — the narrowed set of steps the opponent can take due to your handles/blocks; used to predict and time entries.
- **Stabilization** — converting access into control by suppressing exits through hub selection and redundancy.
- **Tempo** — the rate at which grips and positional changes occur; controlling tempo means forcing the opponent to react on your timing.
- **Threat stacking** — maintaining a primary threat while cycling secondary threats that punish the defense and force reactions.
- **Validity gate** — the rule that entries must be proof-gated; no proof means return to connect/deny/force.

- **Entries are proof-gated** (post/step/collapse/retreat triggers).

- Control requires **hub stabilization** (exit suppression).
- Finishing requires **anti-exit governance** (no reset allowed).

## 14.3 Implementation Guidance (What to Emphasize in Training)
- Prioritize **early-link denial** (distance/grip/angle) to avoid expensive late defense.
- Train **re-grip discipline** and redundancy; most exchanges are won on re-attachment.
- Treat retreat/backstep as triggers; do not allow clean distance resets.
- Use constraints (proof-gating, no-finish rounds) to prevent random technique switching.

## 14.4 Common Misinterpretations to Avoid
- “More movement” is not the goal; **better connection** is the goal.
- “Entry attempts” are not progress; **proof-based entries** are progress.
- “Touching legs” is not control; **hub stabilization** is control.
- “Almost finishing” is not success if the opponent can reset; **anti-exit** is part of finishing.
