# VN8 — Open Guard Part 1 — Volume 8 (Operator Notes; Titles-Aligned)

## 8.0 Volume 8

### 8.1 De La Riva Entries (0:00–8:59)
**Purpose**
- Build no-gi De La Riva-style connections that reliably convert “outside range” into **leg engagement** without giving the opponent clean backsteps, ankle captures, or easy disengagement.

**Core configuration idea (no-gi adaptation)**
- In no-gi, you cannot rely on cloth grips to “freeze” the knee/stance.
- The replacement is **redundant leg connection** (two-foot/leg engagement) so the opponent can’t simply step away or peel one point of contact.

**Primary decision rules**
- **Prefer “double connection” before you rotate.**
  - If you rotate around the corner without tight attachment, the opponent can **flee the mat** and you lose the window.
- **Treat the foot like a “knot on a rope.”**
  - Control the foot/ankle line as the “knot” that prevents the leg from sliding free while you climb into deeper entanglement.
- **Knees-down discipline during turning.**
  - When you begin your turning action, keep knees oriented to the mat (not flared upward), so you don’t create an escape lane or lose bite.
- **Protect your feet from counters.**
  - If the opponent can seize/attack your exposed foot, you’ve built a fragile DLR; retract and cover during transitions.

**Operational validation tests**
- If the opponent tries to stand tall or flee and **cannot clear your hooks**, your connection is real.
- If the opponent can backstep/flee immediately, you rotated without sufficient attachment.

**Common failure signatures**
- “I rotate and he just runs away” (insufficient connection before the turn).
- “He grabs my ankle/foot and kills it” (foot exposure + weak cover discipline).
- “I get inside-outside but can’t destabilize” (contact without true control).

### 8.2 De La Riva Entries Part 2 (8:59–15:27)
**Purpose**
- Convert the DLR attachment into **high-control hubs** (notably 50/50 variants) that shut down the opponent’s two primary exits: **fleeing** and **backstepping**.

**Primary decision rules**
- **If they flee the mat, your structure must make it costly.**
  - Build pinching/locking pressure around the leg line so retreat is slow enough that you can finish the transition.
- **If they backstep, your structure must “trap the backstep.”**
  - The goal is not “prevent all movement,” it’s “make movement take long enough that heel exposure arrives first.”

**Control themes emphasized**
- “Knee/foot angle discipline” to deny the opponent clean access to your heels while you attack theirs.
- Preference toward entries that move you from **outside relationship → inside control** (inside control is the end goal even if the entry begins outside).

**Legacy-entry critique (explicit theme)**
- Classic “old-school” entries that force awkward knee-bar style paths are acknowledged but framed as lower percentage against high-level counters.
- Modern emphasis: control + heel exposure pathways that survive elite defense.

**Failure signatures**
- You enter, but opponent clears quickly (tempo too slow; structure not trapping the exit).
- You attack one leg while exposing the other (you win a battle but lose the war).

### 8.3 Closed Guard Entries (15:27–30:36)
**Purpose**
- Solve the core closed-guard leg-entry problem: when the opponent stands, many conventional entries expose you to **back-trap/back-take style consequences** and/or give the opponent the leg-lock initiative.

**Key contrast (old vs new)**
- **Old approach:** chase a single leg line from closed guard → opponent rotates/knee-slides on the other side → you lose back integrity and can get trapped.
- **Preferred approach:** control **both legs** early so the opponent cannot freely knee-slide/backstep into your blind side.

**Primary decision rules**
- **Two-leg control is the safety requirement.**
  - If you only control one leg, assume the other leg becomes their steering wheel for passing/back exposure.
- **Hands-down gate still applies.**
  - The entry is “real” when you can force posting or otherwise break base such that their ability to freely rotate/knee-slide is reduced.
- **Win inside space with knees/feet.**
  - Even if you begin outside, you must migrate to **inside domination** (knees/feet inside) to control momentum rather than letting the opponent dictate it.

**Canonical connector (as presented)**
- Closed guard → transfer to an inside-dominant leg relationship → quick transition into X-guard family → from X-guard, drop to the ankle/shoelace line → lift to destabilize → enter legs from a controlled posture break.

**Why X-guard shows up again**
- X-guard is treated as a **bridge that forces hands to the floor**, giving you the time window to connect to the shoelace/heel line and enter safely.

**Common failure signatures**
- You chase a leg and get rotated into back exposure (single-leg focus + poor inside control).
- You “get to X-guard” but don’t force hands down (upright opponent = no entry window).
- You break grips but don’t rebuild inside structure (they immediately re-initiate).
