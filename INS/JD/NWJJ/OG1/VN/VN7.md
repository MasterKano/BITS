# VN7 — Open Guard Part 1 — Volume 7 (Operator Notes; Entry-to-X Guard and Leg-Entanglement Integration)

## 7.0 Volume 7

### 7.1 Core dilemma: far-leg vs near-leg threats via X-Guard (0:05–0:58)
**Purpose**
- Build an entry that forces a dilemma: opponent must defend the far leg **and** the near leg with equal urgency.

**Core claim**
- If you can create symmetrical threat access (far + near), opponent defense becomes indecisive and slow—your entry rate spikes.

**Primary decision rule**
- Do not “reach forward” for the far leg from neutral distance (it invites the backstep and you lose the exchange immediately).

**Failure signature**
- Leaning/reaching → opponent backsteps → your angle collapses and you’re late.

---

### 7.2 Safe entry principle: tie up legs before you advance (0:50–1:16)
**Purpose**
- Prevent the backstep and create a stable bridge into rumi ashi garami and then X-guard.

**Mechanism (as described)**
- Establish dual leg ties:
  - **hands** on a leg line (leg control #1)
  - **foot** behind opponent’s leg (leg control #2)
- Then pull them into **rumi ashi garami**, and transition smoothly into classic X-guard.

**Primary decision rule**
- Entry must begin with **leg control redundancy** (hands + feet), not hands-only reaching.

---

### 7.3 Classic X-Guard: off-balance first, then route (1:16–2:05)
**Purpose**
- Use classic X-guard’s first job: put the opponent’s **hands to the mat** (posture/base compromise).

**Configuration cues**
- One foot in front of the hip; one foot behind the knee.
- Hooks are “alive”: feet retract so hooks stay sticky.

**Primary decision rule**
- If you don’t get hands down, you don’t “own” the X-guard—keep destabilizing.

**Immediate route**
- Once posture breaks, transition options open (including quick access to inside senkaku).

---

### 7.4 Reverse X-Guard as a faster inside-senkaku pathway (1:42–3:46)
**Purpose**
- Use reverse X-guard to elevate and align hips to enter inside senkaku quickly.

**Opponent’s common counters (the two problems)**
1) **Make the far leg heavy** (so lifting/turning feels impossible).
2) **High-leg** (yank the leg up so you lose close contact and can’t enter).

**Primary decision rule**
- Treat “heavy leg” and “high leg” as predictable counters, not surprises—your job is to maintain or re-create **close contact** and deny clean extraction.

---

### 7.5 Finishing mechanics from the leg window: fill space and lock breaks (approx. 10:42–11:33)
**Purpose**
- Convert the post/triangle reactions into an actual finishing structure rather than a prolonged chase.

**Key mechanic (as described)**
- When they triangle/close their legs, you **fill the space between the ankles** to keep your wedge and prevent them closing the escape window.

**Primary decision rule**
- Space is the enemy: if you leave gaps at ankles/knee line, their defensive triangles become functional.

---

### 7.6 X-guard as the “safe side-on bridge” into legs (11:36–13:29)
**Purpose**
- Frame X-guard as a bridging guard: side-on alignment creates safer leg access than square-on entries.

**Primary decision rule**
- In most X-guard scenarios, the first objective remains: **hands to the mat**, then you choose your leg route.

**Failure signature**
- Attempting to leg-enter without first breaking posture → opponent stays tall, clears legs, or backsteps.

---

### 7.7 Momentum entry: “throwback” to create the X-guard wedge (13:29–17:52)
**Purpose**
- Solve the “too-far-to-touch” problem by using momentum to bring hips into the legs without walking in.

**Mechanism (as described)**
- You throw your body backward to throw their hips forward.
- Establish shin/leg wedges (including shin behind the far hip) to stabilize.

**Primary decision rules**
- Connection > landing aesthetics: your end-state is acceptable as long as you have sticky leg attachment.
- Do not leave your foot exposed where it can be attacked/broken during the transition—retract and cover.

---

### 7.8 Reverse De La Riva to leg-entry: hands-down mandate + knee-across exposure (23:26–24:31)
**Purpose**
- Use Reverse DLR-style control to force hands down, then rotate to expose heel/knee lines.

**Core sequence logic (as described)**
- Build control → demand hands on the floor → pull knee to face across your hip → heel exposure appears.
- Preferred finishes include strong breaks (knee/behind-knee style finishes are emphasized).

**Primary decision rule**
- “Hands down” is the validity test; if they are upright, you are not yet in your finishing window.

---

### 7.9 “Best-of-both-worlds” entanglement: Inside-senkaku + 50/50 compromise (24:32–26:03)
**Purpose**
- Create an entanglement that has:
  - inside-senkaku’s barrier value when opponent comes forward to hand-fight, and
  - 50/50’s control when opponent tries to turn away.

**Configuration idea (as described)**
- One foot behaves like inside senkaku (inside position).
- One foot behaves like 50/50 (outside/containment).
- This “compromise” is presented as producing very strong breaking pressure.

**Primary decision rule**
- If both feet are inside without heel control, turn-outs get easier.
- The compromise structure reduces the turn-out problem while preserving forward barrier.

---

### 7.10 Continued attacks: switching targets and spinning under (26:06–27:57)
**Purpose**
- Attack the “other” leg line off the same Reverse DLR family by small arm-position changes, then spin under to connect.

**Primary decision rule**
- When switching targets (near ↔ far), the key is not speed but preserving the wedge and angle so the spin-under doesn’t become a scramble.

**Failure signature**
- Spinning under without first stabilizing the opponent’s hands/posture → you rotate under a stable base and lose control.

---

### 7.11 Split squat / headquarters: leg omnipresence and finishing routes (47:07–49:38)
**Purpose**
- Demonstrate that even in common passing stances (split squat/headquarters), the legs remain attackable and you can enter breaking positions.

**Core ideas (as described)**
- Establish control that prevents the opponent from immediately clearing (described as holding them off a reverse-double-heel-hook style line).
- Off-balance to hands → thread foot around the corner → enter a criss-cross/backside-capable breaking structure.

**Primary decision rule**
- Off-balance is the gateway: if you don’t force weight onto hands, your threading/rotation attempts are low yield.

---

### 7.12 Backside finishing governance: avoid being flattened; follow backsteps (49:52–54:16)
**Purpose**
- Re-state the backside rule: if your structure doesn’t let your legs “out-push” their hands, they can flatten you and kill breaking alignment.

**Primary decision rules**
- In backside, you must have a configuration that provides **pushing pressure into the back/hip line**; otherwise their hand push wins.
- If opponent backsteps to disengage, **follow**—do not accept the reset.

**Integration note**
- Volume ties back to earlier: hub selection (inside senkaku vs criss-cross vs double-50 style control) depends on whether you’re frontside/backside and what escape they’re using.
