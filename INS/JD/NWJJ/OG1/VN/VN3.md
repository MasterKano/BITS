# VN3 — Open Guard Part 1 — Volume 3 (Framework + Movement Library)

## 3.0 Volume 3
Focus of this volume: guard retention as a **model** (demarcation lines) plus a **movement vocabulary** (the essential body movements that let you re-align and re-guard under passing pressure).

---

### 3.1 3 Critical Elements of Guard Retention — The Theory of Demarcation (0:00–10:52)
**Purpose**
- Make guard retention predictable by teaching **where danger begins** (lines) and what your immediate priorities become when each line is breached.

**Demarcation lines (operational)**
- **Center line:** while the passer is aligned in front, passing is less developed; passing progress accelerates when they work off-center.
- **Toe line:** in seated guard, the passer must **step beyond your toe line** to close distance and begin meaningful passing.
- **Hip line:** the single most critical line; **every guard pass requires reaching/passing your hip line**.
- **Shoulder line:** becomes critical once hip line pressure is present; if they progress up toward shoulders, elbow–knee integrity becomes harder to maintain.

**Primary decision rules**
- If the passer is **in front of the hip line**, you can stay offense-capable and mobile.
- The moment the passer **passes the hip line**, treat it as a **crisis**: offense stops; you enter a defensive framework to re-align them back in front of the hip line.
- Retention improves fastest when you can identify: “Which line did they just win?” and respond with the correct category of movement.

**Validation tests**
- If they have not challenged toe line / hip line, they are not “passing yet” — do not overreact with late-stage frames.
- If they have crossed hip line, your next actions must immediately address re-alignment and structure (not “hold grips”).

**Failure signatures**
- Freaking out early (framing hard while they’re still in front of hip line) → wasted energy, delayed movement.
- Staying offensive when they’ve already crossed hip line → you get pinned before you ever “start defending.”

**Coaching cues**
- “Hip line = alarm bell.”
- Retention is “line management”: keep them in front; if they get past, re-square immediately.

---

### 3.2 Essential Body Movements For Guard Retention (10:52–12:30)
**Purpose**
- Define the “must-have” movement categories that show up repeatedly across all retention scenarios.

**The core idea**
- Retention is not one technique; it is a set of repeatable **body movements** that re-establish:
  - alignment (square hips),
  - inside connection (feet/legs back inside),
  - and structural integrity (especially elbow–knee style connections).

**Primary decision rule**
- When pressure rises, do not invent; select from the essential movement set (pummel, crossover, high leg, inverted spin, scissor-sit, pendulum, shrimp family, scoot, frames).

**Failure signatures**
- Attempting to “hand fight” your way out of late-stage passing without moving your body → you get stuck, then pinned.
- Relying on flexibility alone → collapses against strong angles and compression.

**Coaching cues**
- The pass is beaten by **re-positioning** (hips/legs/frames), not by “holding on.”

---

### 3.3 Foot Pummeling (12:30–17:15)
**Purpose**
- Reclaim inside position and prevent leg compression by using your feet/legs as active “pummeling” tools.

**Definition (operational)**
- “Foot pummeling” = cycling your feet back to **inside lanes** when the passer tries to scoop, staple, or move your legs aside.

**Primary decision rules**
- When the passer starts to **dominate your feet/legs**, your first job is to **pummel back to inside** before they can compress and kill foot utility.
- Do not allow both feet to be gathered/controlled in a way that removes your ability to reinsert frames and hooks.

**Validation tests**
- If you can pummel a foot back inside, you can usually re-establish playable guard alignment without escalating to heavy framing.

**Failure signatures**
- Allowing deep scoops underneath both legs → asymmetry + compression → you lose leg mobility.
- Treating feet as “posts” rather than “hooks” → they get moved aside and stapled.

**Coaching cues**
- “If they’re scooping under, your feet must become active immediately.”
- Your feet are not decorations; they are your primary long-range retention tools.

---

### 3.4 Crossovers (17:15–19:59)
**Purpose**
- Re-align when the passer starts angling to one side while still largely in front of your hip line.

**Definition (operational)**
- “Crossover” = bringing one leg across to re-square and re-connect, denying the passer’s attempt to create clean angle progression.

**Primary decision rules**
- If the passer is gaining angle **but has not yet secured hip-line dominance**, crossover re-alignment is often the highest-efficiency answer (frames can come later).
- Use crossovers to maintain “front-facing engagement” and prevent the passer from living on your flank.

**Validation tests**
- The crossover should end with:
  - your legs re-established in a usable configuration,
  - your hips re-squared,
  - and the passer redirected back into front alignment.

**Failure signatures**
- Trying to frame early (when the threat is still “angle only”) → you slow down and let angle become hip-line penetration.
- Crossing without re-locking connection → you re-square briefly but immediately get angled again.

**Coaching cues**
- “Angle first, hip line later — solve angle early with movement.”

---

### 3.5 High Leg (19:59–22:26)
**Purpose**
- Emergency retention tool when the passer is close and you must re-create barriers and alignment quickly.

**Definition (operational)**
- “High leg” = elevating the leg high to re-establish meaningful inside contact and prevent clean chest/shoulder control development.

**Primary decision rules**
- Use high leg when:
  - the passer is close enough that low leg positioning gets compressed,
  - you need to reinsert a strong inside barrier immediately.
- Treat high leg as a bridge back to re-guard, not as an end-state.

**Failure signatures**
- High leg without structural support (no meaningful inside contact) → you get folded and turned.
- Trying high leg too late (after stable pin pressure) → it becomes a weak push.

**Coaching cues**
- “High leg buys time — spend it to re-square and re-build guard.”

---

### 3.6 The Inverted Spin (22:26–28:14)
**Purpose**
- Solve late-stage angles by rotating your body so your shoulders do not get pinned while you reorient to face the passer again.

**Core problem it addresses**
- When the passer is deep on an angle, you have two broad options:
  - turn into them (protect shoulders, rebuild frames),
  - or turn away/invert to keep shoulders from being pinned while you rotate back to alignment.

**Primary decision rules**
- If facing them directly is collapsing (you are getting flattened), the inverted spin becomes a viable path to keep shoulders unpinned while you rotate.
- The goal is always: **return to a position where feet/legs can re-engage and you can face them again.**

**Failure signatures**
- Inverting without controlling shoulder exposure → you get pinned mid-rotation.
- Spinning without re-establishing connection at the end → you “escape” but remain disconnected and get re-passed.

**Coaching cues**
- Inversion is not acrobatics; it is a shoulder-preservation and re-alignment tool.

---

### 3.7 The Scissor and Sit (28:14–33:44)
**Purpose**
- Recover from hip-line danger by building a frame, creating asymmetry with the legs (scissor), then sitting back into a re-guarded alignment.

**Definition (operational)**
- Frame first to prevent penetration, then use a temporary **scissor leg configuration** to manage distance/angle long enough to re-square.

**Primary decision rules**
- When hip line is threatened:
  1) establish a tight defensive frame structure (keep elbow–knee style integrity),
  2) create asymmetry (scissor) to interrupt direct forward penetration,
  3) sit/re-align back into a playable guard relationship.

**Failure signatures**
- Framing with disconnected elbow–knee relationships → they penetrate through the “gap.”
- Attempting to move legs freely while frames are forming → you sacrifice structure and get passed.

**Coaching cues**
- “Frames protect; scissor stabilizes; sit re-aligns.”

---

### 3.8 The Pendulum (33:44–44:00)
**Purpose**
- Use a low-effort body rotation (shoulder-based) to change alignment and recover guard when the passer is driving around the corner.

**Key concept**
- The pendulum emphasizes that rolling/rotating over the shoulders can be mechanically efficient (not strength-based) when timed correctly.

**Primary decision rules**
- When you need to redirect your hips and legs without fighting head-on, pendulum rotation can reorient your body and re-create inside lanes.
- Pendulum is selected when:
  - direct re-squaring is being blocked,
  - and you can rotate to face them again without giving up shoulder control.

**Failure signatures**
- Rotating without preserving frames → you rotate into pin pressure.
- Treating the pendulum as “movement for movement’s sake” → no re-guard occurs.

**Coaching cues**
- The end condition matters: you must finish with feet/legs available to reconnect, not merely “roll.”

---

### 3.9 Scissor and Roll (44:00–50:51)
**Purpose**
- Combine a scissor structure with a rolling action to reset alignment when the passer’s angle is strong and your frames are under stress.

**Primary decision rules**
- If static scissor is insufficient (they’re crushing through), add the roll to change the geometry and re-establish guard.
- Maintain defensive structure during the roll; the roll is a **delivery mechanism** to regain alignment, not a scramble.

**Failure signatures**
- Hands placed poorly (too low/weak) → you get crushed during the transition.
- Rolling without a clear re-guard target → you end in a worse angle.

**Coaching cues**
- Roll only when it produces a better alignment outcome than staying put.

---

### 3.10 Shrimping (50:51–55:18)
**Purpose**
- Create hip separation and reinsert knees/feet into the line of engagement when the passer is compressing you.

**Definition (operational)**
- Shrimping = hip escape that increases distance and restores your ability to face and frame.

**Primary decision rules**
- Use shrimping when you need space to rebuild:
  - frames,
  - knee position,
  - and the ability to point your barriers back at the opponent.

**Failure signatures**
- Shrimping without restoring frames → you create space but they follow and re-compress.
- Shrimping “sideways” without re-squaring → you give them continued angle.

**Coaching cues**
- Shrimping is not the finish; it is the reset that makes frames/legs usable again.

---

### 3.11 Reverse Shrimping (55:18–57:40)
**Purpose**
- Recover alignment when the passer’s movement demands you escape in the opposite direction (and standard shrimping would worsen your angle).

**Definition (operational)**
- Reverse shrimping = hip escape that retracts/realigns in the direction that preserves your ability to face them and reinsert barriers.

**Primary decision rules**
- If normal shrimping would turn you away or feed angle, reverse shrimp to regain the correct facing relationship.
- The objective is always: “feet/knees back in front of them,” not “move for distance.”

**Failure signatures**
- Choosing the wrong shrimp direction → you run yourself into a worse pinning angle.
- Escaping hips but leaving legs behind → you lose the tools that actually retain guard.

**Coaching cues**
- Escape direction must be chosen based on where the passer is trying to go, not based on habit.

---

### 3.12 Scooting (57:40–1:01:02)
**Purpose**
- A mobility reset: move your base (butt/hips) to re-establish facing and keep your barriers oriented toward the opponent.

**Definition (operational)**
- Scooting = relocating your seated base so you can square up, reframe, and keep the opponent from winning clean angles.

**Primary decision rules**
- If you cannot point feet at them momentarily, scoot to reposition so you can.
- Scooting should re-enable the next layer (pummel/crossover/frame) rather than being a standalone “run away.”

**Embedded rule: orientation requirement**
- You cannot allow situations where neither your **feet** nor your **knees** are oriented toward the opponent.
- If feet cannot point, knees must; if knees cannot, feet must.

**Failure signatures**
- Scooting without re-establishing orientation → you end up side-on and easy to pin.
- Moving your base while leaving your legs unmanaged → passer collects legs and compresses.

**Coaching cues**
- “At least one of feet or knees must be pointing at the opponent at all times.”

---

### 3.13 Framing (1:01:02–1:20:02) *(present in transcript earlier; framing content is bundled into ~49:44–55:34)*
**Purpose**
- Provide the “hard barrier” layer that activates when movement-only retention (pummel/crossover/high-leg/inversion) is no longer sufficient because the passer is **at or past the hip line** and entering pin territory.

**Activation rule (when to frame)**
- **Hip line is the demarcation trigger.**
  - If the passer is still clearly **in front of your hip line**, framing is usually premature (movement solutions are higher efficiency).
  - The moment the passer is **at/past the hip line**, offense stops and you enter a **defensive framework**.

**Distance governs frame selection**
- Choose frames by distance bands (simple hierarchy):
  - **Longest distance:** hands (long frames).
  - **Medium distance:** forearms.
  - **Very close:** back-of-hand / tight skeletal framing (short frames).
- Operational idea: as they close, frames get **shorter and more structural**, connected to your core (not “reach and push”).

**Structural prerequisites (what makes frames work)**
- **Rounded spine (“ball” shape)** to keep hips mobile and prevent flattening.
- **Hips off the mat** as the mobility engine:
  - more hip contact with the mat = slower movement + easier pinning by the passer.
- Frames are not “bench pressing.” They are **wedges** that protect the space you need to move and re-guard.

**Primary decision rules**
- Frame to **stop penetration**, then move to **re-align** (frames protect movement; movement solves the pass).
- If your frame is extended and disconnected from your torso, it will collapse under pressure; shorten and connect.

**Failure signatures**
- Framing too early (while they’re still in front of the hip line) → you stall, lose mobility, and allow angle to become penetration.
- Framing too long as they close → arms fold, shoulders get pinned, and the frame becomes a liability.
- Flat spine + hips glued to the mat → you become “easy to pin,” even with correct hand placement.

**Coaching cues**
- “Hip line decides: no hip line threat = move; hip line threat = frame + move.”
- “Frames are distance-calibrated: long → medium → short as they close.”

### 3.14 Framing High / Framing Low (1:20:02–1:23:03) *(present in transcript; bundled into ~53:25–55:20)*
**Purpose**
- Prevent a common error: chasing high frames when the passer’s posture makes them unreachable, which causes you to overextend and lose structure.

**Definition (operational)**
- **High framing:** frames that address head/upper torso control lines (when reachable).
- **Low framing:** frames that address lower lines (hip/knee-line pathway) when the passer is tall or distance makes high frames unreliable.

**Primary decision rules**
- **If you can reach safely, frame high.**
  - High frames are valid when you can connect without elongating and losing posture.
- **If you cannot reach, do not chase—frame low.**
  - When the passer comes around tall / out of reach, your job is to **block low** rather than “go to them.”
- The correct choice is not aesthetic; it’s structural:
  - if reaching causes extension and loss of connection to your core, you picked the wrong frame height.

**Validation tests**
- If the frame forces you to straighten your spine, flare elbows, or detach from your torso → it is not structurally viable in that moment.
- A good frame (high or low) should preserve:
  - rounded spine,
  - hip mobility,
  - ability to re-square.

**Failure signatures**
- “I keep trying to frame the head/shoulders but I can’t reach” → you overextend, and the passer wins penetration.
- High frame attempt becomes a reaching contest → passer collapses your arms and pins shoulders.

**Coaching cues**
- “If you can’t reach, block.”
- “Don’t go to him—make him run into your frame.”

### 3.15 Framing & The Law of Feet & Hips (1:23:03+) *(present in transcript; delivered as “Law of Feet & Knees” + self-framing, ~55:34–1:00:12)*
**Purpose**
- Provide a simple, non-negotiable orientation law that predicts passing danger, and show how framing supports obeying the law when the passer tries to turn your barriers away.

**Iron law (orientation requirement)**
- You must never allow a situation where **neither** your feet **nor** your knees are pointing at the opponent.
  - If feet are pointing at them: good.
  - If feet get pushed away: knees must take over.
  - If knees get pushed away: you are on the edge of being passed.

**Why this matters**
- When both feet and knees point away, the passer is effectively “turning your shield sideways” and the hip line is about to be accessed cleanly.

**How framing supports the law (self-framing)**
- Use a “frame on yourself” and/or an elbow/hand post on the mat to **limit how far** the passer can push your knees away.
- Frame on the side the passer is coming toward (framing the wrong side provides no mechanical resistance).

**Primary decision rules**
- When the passer forces your feet to point away:
  - immediately transition to **knees pointing at them** (do not accept the loss of both).
- When the passer forces knees to point away:
  - use **self-framing / elbow post** to create resistance and recover knee orientation.
- Always recover to an end condition:
  - **feet back in**, pointed at opponent, ready to play again.

**Validation tests**
- If you can re-point either feet or knees toward the opponent, you are still in the retention fight.
- If you cannot re-point either (both are turned away), you are in immediate pass danger.

**Failure signatures**
- Treating foot displacement as “minor” and waiting → knees follow the feet, and you lose both orientations.
- Posting/framing on the wrong side → no resistance, knees get pushed away, and the corner is taken.
- Trying to solve orientation loss with hands alone (no hip/leg recovery) → you stall and get passed.

**Coaching cues**
- “Lose one, gain the other.”
- “Feet in = guard alive.”
- “Frame on the side they’re passing to; otherwise you’re helping them.”
