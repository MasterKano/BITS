# VN2 — Open Guard Part 1 — Volume 2 (Framework / Model Brief; Titles-Aligned)

## 2.0 Volume 2
Note: the time ranges in the Titles file appear internally inconsistent in places; the structure below follows the chapter order and intent.

### 2.1 The Secret to Successful Guard Play: Dynamic Energy Theory (0:00–5:33)
**Purpose**
- Turn “guard feel” into a readable model: success comes from tracking **where the opponent’s force/momentum is going** and aligning your actions to it.

**Definition (operational)**
- “Energy” here is not mystical; it is the **directional commitment** of the opponent’s posture, weight, and intent (push vs pull; forward vs backward).

**Primary decision rule**
- Your guard actions must **match** the opponent’s directional commitment:
  - When they are driving in one direction, pick mechanics that exploit that direction rather than fighting it head-on.

**Failure signatures**
- “It feels like a brick wall” (you are choosing actions that oppose their committed structure).
- You attempt technique selection without first reading posture/weight direction → random outcomes.

**Coaching cues**
- Start every exchange by answering: “Is he pushing or pulling? Is he coming forward or sitting back?”
- If you can consistently read direction, the correct family of attacks becomes obvious and repeatable.

---

### 2.2 The Law of Shoulders & Hips (Push Pull Dynamic Knees) (5:33–14:11)
**Purpose**
- Provide a simple, visual rule for **kneeling opponents**: the shoulders/hips relationship tells you whether to push or pull.

**Definition**
- Track the opponent’s **shoulders relative to hips**:
  - Shoulders **behind** hips = posture leaning back / weight retreating.
  - Shoulders **in front of** hips = posture leaning forward / weight driving in.

**Primary decision rules**
- If shoulders are **behind** hips → **push** mechanics dominate (you can knock them back and force posts behind).
- If shoulders are **in front of** hips → **pull** mechanics dominate (you can pull them forward and force posts in front).

**Validation tests**
- A correct read produces a measurable break:
  - **hands to the mat behind** (after push) or **hands to the mat in front** (after pull).

**Failure signatures**
- You “pull” when shoulders are behind hips → they feel immovable; your pulling attacks die.
- You “push” when shoulders are in front of hips → you run into their structure; you lose the exchange.

**Coaching cues**
- Don’t stare; use peripheral vision to keep track of shoulders/hips while maintaining your own posture and frames.
- Treat “push vs pull” as a binary selection driven by posture—stop guessing.

---

### 2.3 The Law of Heel & Toes (Push Pull Dynamic Standing) (14:11–21:53)
**Purpose**
- Replace visual reading (harder when someone is standing) with a tactile rule you can feel through grips.

**Definition**
- Standing opponent: read **weight distribution** through your grips:
  - Weight on **heels** = “back-weighted.”
  - Weight on **toes** = “forward-weighted.”

**Primary decision rules**
- Weight on **heels** → **push** works (knockback mechanics are high yield).
- Weight on **toes** → **pull** works (forward commitment can be exploited with pulling mechanics).

**How you “read it”**
- The first grips you establish are not only for control; they are also sensory inputs that tell you where their weight sits.

**Failure signatures**
- You treat standing opponents like kneeling opponents (visual-only reading) → you misread and pick the wrong family.
- You grip without learning anything (no information extracted) → slow adaptation, low conversion.

**Coaching cues**
- Grip with the intent to “read the book,” not just “hold the page.”
- If you cannot tell heels vs toes, your contact is too superficial or too fleeting.

---

### 2.4 The Law of Head Under/Head Over (Under Over Dynamic) (21:53–7:54)
**Purpose**
- Provide a third rule that governs elevation vs go-behind: head height and hip position determine whether “under” or “over” is available.

**Definition**
- Compare head levels and the opponent’s hip behavior:
  - Opponent’s head **high** (center of gravity exposed) → “under” becomes available (elevation/lifts).
  - Opponent’s head **low** with hips **back** (center protected, distance increases) → elevation becomes difficult; “over/go-behind” becomes available.

**Primary decision rules**
- Head **high** → prioritize **getting underneath** and elevating (sweeps/lifts become mechanically easier).
- Head **low** + hips back → stop forcing elevation; shift to **go-over / go-behind** solutions (snap-down style logic).

**Failure signatures**
- You insist on elevation against a low head + hips back → you stall and burn energy.
- You attempt go-behind while their posture is tall/exposed → you miss the easiest elevation window.

**Coaching cues**
- “Under when exposed; over when protected.”
- Treat head/hip posture as the permission structure for your next phase.

---

### 2.5 The First Skill of Guard Play: Retention (7:54–11:37)
**Purpose**
- Establish retention as the first competency because it enables committed offense under resistance.

**Core claim**
- Many athletes “have techniques” but don’t use them because they don’t trust their ability to **keep guard when the attack fails**.

**Primary decision rules**
- Build defense first so offense becomes psychologically and tactically available.
- Retention is not “survive”; it is **survive + re-enter + counter** (defense must convert back into initiative).

**Failure signatures**
- Attack-shy behavior: you won’t extend because you expect to get passed.
- Retention feels like chaos (no model; only reactions).

**Coaching cues**
- Treat retention as a technical system with requirements and counters, not as athletic scrambling.

---

### 2.6 The Knowledge/Denial Model of Guard Retention (11:37–13:53)
**Purpose**
- Make retention teachable: first learn the passer’s requirements, then build denial habits for each requirement.

**Model**
- Step 1: **Knowledge** — identify what the passer must accomplish to pass.
- Step 2: **Denial** — make one or more requirements fail at the moment they are imposed.

**Primary decision rule**
- Retention improves fastest when you defend **requirements**, not “moves.”

**Failure signatures**
- You defend the wrong thing (reaction is late or misaligned) because you don’t know what requirement is currently being built.

**Coaching cues**
- In every exchange, ask: “Which requirement is he trying to satisfy right now?”
- Deny early; late denial becomes scramble-only.

---

### 2.7 The Six Elements of Guard Passing — Distance (13:53–16:10)
**Purpose**
- Define the first requirement: the passer must **close distance** enough to meaningfully connect and begin building the pass.

**Definition**
- “Distance” is the ability to get past your long-range barriers (feet/shins/frames) into working range.

**Primary denial rules**
- Use your longest tools first (feet/shins/frames) to keep them at a range where they cannot connect chest/knee lines.
- If distance closes, immediately build the next layer (frames + inside positioning) to prevent cascade into later elements.

**Failure signatures**
- They “walk in for free” → your long-range barriers are not active or not re-established after grip breaks.

**Coaching cues**
- Distance denial is not one action; it is a continuous re-creation of barriers as they step and angle.

---

### 2.8 Grip (16:10–18:09)
**Purpose**
- Define the second requirement: once distance is closed, the passer must establish **meaningful grips/controls** to stabilize progress.

**Definition**
- “Grip” is any control that restricts your ability to re-frame/re-guard (not merely a touch).

**Primary denial rules**
- Break grips early before they connect to posture/balance.
- If a grip is established, immediately change the geometry (frames, angles, inside position) so the grip loses utility.

**Failure signatures**
- You accept grips as inevitable and only react once they start passing → too late.
- You hand-fight without rebuilding frames/inside position → grip breaks don’t matter because they reconnect instantly.

**Coaching cues**
- Grip denial must be paired with re-building structure; otherwise you only delay the inevitable.

---

### 2.9 Angle (18:09–43:46:00)
**Purpose**
- Define the third requirement: the passer must create **angle** so your defensive lines no longer face them square.

**Definition**
- Angle is the passer moving off your center line so they can bypass symmetrical frames and attack edges.

**Primary denial rules**
- Re-center: whenever they build angle, your job is to **re-establish the center line** (square up).
- Use legs/feet and frames to “steer” them back into front-facing engagement.

**Failure signatures**
- You chase them with your arms only → they keep circling and your frames shorten until they collapse.
- You allow angle without re-centering → you end up defending late-stage pin attempts.

**Coaching cues**
- Angle is where passing starts to feel “easy” for them; treat it as an alarm bell.
- If you can’t re-center, you must escalate to stronger frame structures and hip movement.

---

### 2.10 Level (43:46:00–45:57:00)
**Purpose**
- Define the fourth requirement: the passer must win **level** (height/plane) to clear frames and approach pinning range.

**Definition**
- Level change is the passer dropping their level to enter through/under your defensive frame lines and approach chest-to-chest or knee-on-torso contact.

**Primary denial rules**
- Maintain frame integrity as they lower level: frames shorten as they come closer; you must adjust without collapsing.
- Use elbow–knee connectivity as a structural blocker to prevent them occupying your torso space.

**Failure signatures**
- They “drop in” and your frames disconnect → they arrive at pin range.
- You keep long frames while they close → your arms fold and you get compressed.

**Coaching cues**
- As distance collapses, your frames must become shorter and more structural (connected to your core).

---

### 2.11 Penetration (45:57:00–48:37:00)
**Purpose**
- Define the fifth requirement: the passer must **penetrate** your defensive shell—breaking key connections that prevent entry.

**Definition (as used here)**
- Penetration is the passer successfully defeating the guard’s internal structure (notably elbow–knee style connectivity) to enter torso control.

**Primary denial rules**
- Preserve the integrity of your core connections (elbow–knee style links) so their level change runs into a “wall.”
- If penetration begins, immediately re-build the connection or switch to a new frame structure before pin consolidates.

**Failure signatures**
- Your elbow–knee connectivity breaks and you accept it → they pass directly into pinning mechanics.
- You focus on pushing them away after penetration instead of rebuilding structure → you extend and get pinned.

**Coaching cues**
- Think of penetration as “they got inside the frame.” Your response is “rebuild the frame,” not “bench press.”

---

### 2.12 Pin (48:37:00+)
**Purpose**
- Define the final requirement: a pass is not complete until the passer **pins** with stabilizing contact for time.

**Definition (pin criteria, operational)**
- Pin requires:
  - At least one **shoulder** controlled to the mat (shoulders off-mat = recovery still possible),
  - meaningful **contact** (chest-to-chest or knee-on-torso style contact),
  - and **stability over time** (control held long enough to count as consolidation).

**Primary denial rules**
- If they cannot pin, the pass is not finished—treat “almost passed” as a scramble opportunity.
- Keep shoulders mobile and re-insert frames/legs to recover guard before the pin stabilizes.

**Failure signatures**
- You accept “close enough” as a pass and stop moving → they stabilize and the pass becomes real.
- You recover late (after they settle weight and establish stable contact) → the re-guard window closes.

**Coaching cues**
- Your best recovery chances are before the pin stabilizes—fight hardest in the transition phase.
- If your shoulders are not controlled and you can frame, you are still in the retention fight.
