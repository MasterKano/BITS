# VN5 — Open Guard Part 1, Vol 5 (Notes)

## 5.1 Constant Threat Theory (Behavior is Determined by Belief) (0:00–4:57)
- **Problem statement:** If an opponent believes you’re not dangerous, they attack relentlessly and you end up stuck in a defensive cycle (you defend; they initiate).
- **Core claim:** **Opponent behavior is governed by belief** about your threat level. Change the belief → change their engagement speed, risk tolerance, and passing commitment.
- **Desired effect:** Create **respect** (caution/hesitation) so you can:
  - slow their entries,
  - “steal momentum,”
  - shift to proactive guard offense,
  - control tempo (and therefore outcomes).
- **Design requirement for the threat:** It must be **constant**—not something the opponent can switch off via simple posture/station changes.

## 5.2 The One Target Your Opponent Can Never Hide From You (4:57–14:03)
- **Why “legs” are selected as the constant threat:**
  - **Upper-body submissions** (triangles/arm locks) are highly posture-dependent; standing/posturing creates separation and breaks your control.
  - **Snaps/collar-ties in no-gi** can be high-risk because extended separation can invite flying entries/counters.
  - **Many sweeps** are posture-conditional (work well vs knees but not standing, or vice versa).
- **Legs are posture-proof:** Regardless of whether they’re standing, kneeling, one-knee-up, head high/low—**their legs remain available** in some form.
- **Strategic outcome:** A strong leg-attack threat forces the opponent to defend with skill (not posture alone), producing:
  - hesitation to close distance,
  - reduced passing aggression,
  - a tempo advantage that feeds the rest of your guard system.

## 5.3 Direct Attacks on the Standing Legs (2-on-1 ankle entries) (14:03–15:51)
- **General goal:** When opponents play “safe at distance,” you still need **reachable, direct harassment** that forces reactions.
- **Key tool:** **2-on-1 on the ankle/leg** (often built from 2-on-1 at the wrist → upgraded to elbow/leg control as you connect).
- **Ankle-pick principle (directional):**
  - If the opponent’s **toes point toward you**, pulling in the direction of the toes can drop them.
  - To avoid falling, they must **turn the toes away**—that turn is the predictable reaction you capitalize on.
- **Timing rule:** **Don’t chase—intersect.**
  - When the foot turns/withdraws, your hands should already be positioned to “catch the turn,” not follow it late.
- **Front-leg vs back-leg access:**
  - Build the ability to attack **either leg** (front or back), regardless of stance width (square, staggered, or very tight stagger).
  - When they step to recover, the “moving” foot often becomes **momentarily unweighted**, making it easier to catch than a planted, weighted foot.
- **Finish logic after the catch:**
  - Pull **straight** or **across** depending on what you see.
  - If pulling across, you typically need a **climbing leg** (outside leg that elevates your hips) + a **catch leg** that comes over to load/turn them and create the knockdown or entry.

## 5.4 Partial Inversions From Distance (15:51–50:44)
- **Context:** Opponent stays just close enough to avoid stalling calls, but far enough to avoid meaningful engagement.
- **Method:** **Partial inversion** (up on the points of the shoulders) to project hips into the legs and establish attachment.
  - Expect variable outcomes: sometimes they fall **backward**, sometimes they push and fall **forward**.
  - Your finishing orientation may be **double-seated facing**, or you may land in **backside-type** positions.
  - **Priority:** Where you end up is secondary; **connection to the legs** is primary.
- **Primary entry sequence (conceptual):**
  - From distance, **snatch heel** / initiate leg capture.
  - Use shoulder-supported hip projection to close the gap without “walking in.”
  - Establish a control that prevents the foot from freely slipping/clearing as they react.
- **Common problems to anticipate (and why they matter):**
  - Opponent “posts/weights” away so the target leg feels heavy when you try to lift/turn.
  - Opponent **high-legs** (yanks the leg upward) to deny follow-up access.
  - Answer is generally: improve **coverage** and attachment (control the line of the foot/ankle and keep your hips connected so they can’t simply retract).
- **Operational takeaway:** Partial inversion is your **distance breaker**—a way to turn “outside range” into immediate leg engagement, forcing posture and stance reactions that re-open your offense.

## 5.5 Shin-to-Shin Entry (50:44–end)
- **Purpose:** A clean, repeatable connector from open distance into leg engagement that can yield:
  - off-balances to hands,
  - transitions to ashi entries,
  - or backside-style outcomes depending on their pressure response.
- **Core mechanics (high level):**
  - Build shin-to-shin contact as a wedge/rail to lift and steer the opponent’s base.
  - Use the lift to force posting reactions and open the path to deeper leg entanglement.
  - Emphasize taking them **over the correct line** (often “over the shoulder” conceptually) rather than drifting sideways and losing the connection window.
- **Decision point:** If they resist by driving in, you often get forward-fall/posting outcomes; if they retreat or turn, you often get angle/backside-type outcomes.
- **Non-negotiable:** Maintain a **tight, sticky connection** through the entry—shin contact is not the finish; it is the vehicle that gets you into your next locked configuration.
