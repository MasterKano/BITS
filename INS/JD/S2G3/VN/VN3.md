## 3.1 Special Study: Uki Waza — Arm Drag and Hip Variation (0:00–11:40) 

- Objective
  - Convert a “failed angle” arm drag (can’t reach the back/leg) into a forward-directed sacrifice throw that lands directly into a chest-to-chest pin.

- Key concepts
  - This is a *competition-robust* answer when an arm drag does not yield back exposure: instead of forcing angle, you turn the drag into **post denial + sacrifice**. :contentReference[oaicite:7]{index=7}
  - You are engineering a scoring landing: the entire finish is built around ending **chest over chest** so the opponent cannot spin out or turn in cleanly. :contentReference[oaicite:8]{index=8}
  - Foot blocking improves cleanliness, but the deeper engine is that the opponent cannot post because the arm drag grip controls the posting arm. :contentReference[oaicite:9]{index=9}

- Gates
  - Must secure **(1) arm-drag control + (2) far-side lat control** before committing the drop. :contentReference[oaicite:10]{index=10}
  - Must land on your **side / elbow** so your chest stays higher than theirs; do not collapse flat or you lose control. :contentReference[oaicite:11]{index=11}
  - Finish requires chest position: “over their chest,” not beside it.

- Positional landmarks
  - Arm drag grip is active (you control their arm so it cannot post). :contentReference[oaicite:12]{index=12}
  - Second hand reaches over the top to grip **lat / far-side body** (wrap around them, not just collar-tie style contact). :contentReference[oaicite:13]{index=13}
  - Outside step creates a drive leg; second foot blocks opponent’s foot (ideal case). :contentReference[oaicite:14]{index=14}

- Mechanics & details (Primary line)
  - Arm drag entry → opponent circles out / squares (you can’t access the back/leg). :contentReference[oaicite:15]{index=15}
  - Reach over and secure **lat grip** while keeping the arm drag grip. :contentReference[oaicite:16]{index=16}
  - **Outside step** (creates drive leg) → then place the second foot to **block their foot** → drop your bodyweight and slide across into a pin. :contentReference[oaicite:17]{index=17}
  - Landing rule: you come up on elbow/side so your chest stays high and heavy; keep chest centered over them. :contentReference[oaicite:18]{index=18}

- Branches (defender action → your response)
  - Opponent yanks away / tries to square → lat grip + drag control keeps them attached; you commit the sacrifice. :contentReference[oaicite:19]{index=19}
  - Opponent tries to spin away → arm wrap (lat control) blocks the spin-out line. :contentReference[oaicite:20]{index=20}
  - Opponent tries to turn into you → chest-over-chest + crossface pressure emerges naturally as they turn into your frame. :contentReference[oaicite:21]{index=21}
  - You miss the foot block (imperfect reality) → still often works because the arm drag denies posting and your momentum carries the fall. :contentReference[oaicite:22]{index=22}

- Coaching cues
  - “Lat + drag, then sacrifice.”
  - “Outside step = drive leg.”
  - “Chest over chest; come up on your elbow.”

- Common errors (Failure-first diagnosis)
  - Collapsing flat / falling away from their chest → they spin out; you lose the pin. :contentReference[oaicite:23]{index=23}
  - No foot block and no momentum → they simply step past you; you get nothing.
  - Not wrapping deep enough on the lat → your upper-body clamp is too shallow to stop rotation.

- Drills (explicit or implied)
  - Arm drag → partner circles out immediately (deny back take) → you must transition to lat grip + outside step + sacrifice.
  - Constraint drill: “pin only counts if chest-to-chest within 2 seconds of landing.”

- System tags
  - #ukiwaza #arm-drag #lat-grip #sacrifice-throw #post-denial #chest-to-chest-pin

- Links
  - Directly supports Volume 1 doctrine: “score comes from aftermath; finish chest-to-chest.”
  - Feeds later “older athlete” adaptations: same sacrifice logic, less turning.

## 3.2 Special Note on Arm Drag and Hip Variation (11:40–14:28) 

- Objective
  - Remove dependency on perfect foot-block precision: learn a no-contact version that still produces a pin because the opponent cannot post with the trapped arm.

- Key concepts
  - Foot blocking is “nice,” not mandatory; the true mechanism is **post denial** from the arm drag grip. :contentReference[oaicite:25]{index=25}
  - You can use a stepping pattern like a conventional lateral drop: step in and drop without touching the foot, because their arm is trapped and they cannot base. :contentReference[oaicite:26]{index=26}
  - This matters in competition chaos: you can still commit decisively even when foot position is uncertain. :contentReference[oaicite:27]{index=27}

- Gates
  - Must keep the opponent’s posting arm controlled by the drag (if the arm is free, missing the foot becomes a much bigger problem).
  - Must land chest-to-chest so turning in or turning away are both blocked (your chest and their trapped arm close both escape doors). :contentReference[oaicite:28]{index=28}

- Positional landmarks
  - Your stepping foot can be “inside on the floor” with **no contact** to their foot; the throw still works when the arm is denied. :contentReference[oaicite:29]{index=29}
  - End position: tight chest-to-chest pin; opponent’s arm position prevents them turning away cleanly. :contentReference[oaicite:30]{index=30}

- Mechanics & details (Primary line)
  - Maintain drag + lat control → step in → sacrifice down into pin without needing to catch the foot. :contentReference[oaicite:31]{index=31}

- Branches (defender action → your response)
  - Opponent turns into you → your chest pressure and alignment make it difficult for them to build posture. :contentReference[oaicite:32]{index=32}
  - Opponent turns away → their trapped arm blocks the turn-out line. :contentReference[oaicite:33]{index=33}

- Coaching cues
  - “Block is optional; post denial is not.”
  - “Step in, drop in—pin is the goal.”

- Common errors (Failure-first diagnosis)
  - Letting the drag loosen as you step → opponent posts and steps away.
  - Half-commitment because you “missed the foot” → you create the exact window for them to regain base.

- Drills
  - Alternating reps: (A) block-foot version, (B) no-foot-contact version—same finish requirement (chest-to-chest).
  - Chaos drill: partner moves feet unpredictably; you must commit to no-contact entry when unsure.

- System tags
  - #ukiwaza #no-foot-block #post-denial #competition-robust

- Links
  - Connects to “older athlete” conservative approach: you can get reliable results without needing precise foot traps.

## 3.3 2 on 1 Opponent Headlocks (14:28–18:27) 

- Objective
  - Punish a common (often naïve) reaction: opponent reaches over your head for a front headlock/guillotine while you hold a 2-on-1.

- Key concepts
  - The 2-on-1 changes the risk profile: when the opponent reaches over your head, their ability to defend falls because their arm becomes structurally trapped and cannot post well. :contentReference[oaicite:35]{index=35}
  - This is a deliberate bait: you lower your head to invite the headlock attempt, then convert it into a sacrifice throw that ends in a pin. :contentReference[oaicite:36]{index=36}
  - It is presented as a high-value option against guillotine-focused opponents: “their guillotine becomes your pin” when you know the response pattern. :contentReference[oaicite:37]{index=37}

- Gates
  - Must have a real 2-on-1 (two hands controlling one arm; shoulder behind shoulder line).
  - The moment the headlock comes on is the trigger—do not wait and let them start walking you down first.

- Positional landmarks
  - 2-on-1 lock is tight (their arm across your chest / controlled at wrist/hand line). :contentReference[oaicite:38]{index=38}
  - Opponent reaches over top for head control (front headlock look). :contentReference[oaicite:39]{index=39}
  - You step out wide to create the sacrifice lane.

- Mechanics & details (Primary line)
  - Win 2-on-1 → deliberately place head “in pawn position” (low) to tempt the headlock. :contentReference[oaicite:40]{index=40}
  - As headlock is applied → step outside / wide → sacrifice straight down → block foot (ideal) → land chest over chest into a pin. :contentReference[oaicite:41]{index=41}

- Branches (defender action → your response)
  - Opponent believes headlock will let them sprawl/walk you down → you drop first; their headlock becomes their commitment to your throw. :contentReference[oaicite:42]{index=42}
  - Opponent tries to tighten guillotine mechanics → 2-on-1 limits their arm function and posting, making the fall hard to stop.

- Coaching cues
  - “2-on-1 first, then bait.”
  - “Headlock attempt = go time.”
  - “Drop and pin; don’t wrestle in place.”

- Common errors (Failure-first diagnosis)
  - Baiting without a real 2-on-1 → you give them a true front headlock without denying posts.
  - Waiting too long after the headlock is applied → they start walking you down and you lose the sacrifice window.

- Drills
  - 2-on-1 acquisition → partner must reach over head on cue → you execute immediate ukewaza entry.
  - Constraint: partner is only allowed guillotine-style headlock as defense; your task is to make it fail reliably.

- System tags
  - #2on1 #anti-headlock #ukiwaza #guillotine-punish #post-denial

- Links
  - Connects to cow catcher/front-headlock hub: this is another “head control moment → takedown” conversion, but driven by the opponent’s mistake.

## 3.4 Front Headlock Uki Waza (18:27–22:32) 

- Objective
  - Use an outside front headlock to hit Uki Waza into a pin, with optional immediate mount/guillotine finishing.

- Key concepts
  - The front headlock version prioritizes *angle creation* first because the opponent’s free hand can base; you must remove that base by stepping off-line and turning them. :contentReference[oaicite:44]{index=44}
  - This variation does **not** have the underhook advantage on landing; therefore, you must be ready to transition to a go-behind if they recover to knees. :contentReference[oaicite:45]{index=45}
  - A specific grip choice is emphasized: a simple “5-finger grip” is used because the system is thinking ahead to guillotine mechanics after the landing. :contentReference[oaicite:46]{index=46}

- Gates
  - Must create angle (“come out to the side”) before the block/sacrifice.
  - Must accept that if landing is not controlled, you transition immediately to back exposure rather than stalling on your knees. :contentReference[oaicite:47]{index=47}

- Positional landmarks
  - Outside front headlock (you are positioned to the outside of the head/arm line). :contentReference[oaicite:48]{index=48}
  - Grip: 5-finger lock (not the usual palm-to-palm/wrist-to-wrist front headlock lock). :contentReference[oaicite:49]{index=49}
  - Side-on angle: you sit/sink so you’re nearly side-on and bring the head “around the corner.” :contentReference[oaicite:50]{index=50}

- Mechanics & details (Primary line)
  - Snapdown / head control → lock outside front headlock with 5-finger grip. :contentReference[oaicite:51]{index=51}
  - Step off to the side to create angle; sit/sink side-on while bringing their head with you (cornering the posture). :contentReference[oaicite:52]{index=52}
  - Extend leg/foot across as a block; sacrifice and take them over; land on top. :contentReference[oaicite:53]{index=53}
  - Post-landing options:
    - come up to knees into a basic pin,
    - jump over to mount (if you want immediate scoring height),
    - finish guillotine if the grip is tight enough (“feet come in tight, elbow retracts, hips forward”). :contentReference[oaicite:54]{index=54}

- Branches (defender action → your response)
  - Opponent bases / tries to come back up to knees quickly → you must be alert to go behind and score the back (no underhook safety net here). :contentReference[oaicite:55]{index=55}
  - Landing is slightly uncontrolled → immediate priority becomes: up off knees, chase the back line, don’t stall. :contentReference[oaicite:56]{index=56}

- Coaching cues
  - “Angle first, then block.”
  - “If it’s not clean—go behind immediately.”
  - “Feet tight, elbow retract, hips forward” (guillotine finish mechanics after the fall). :contentReference[oaicite:57]{index=57}

- Common errors (Failure-first diagnosis)
  - Trying to sacrifice without creating the angle → opponent posts and steps, and you end underneath.
  - Getting “stuck on knees” after a messy landing → you let them recover rather than taking back exposure.

- Drills
  - Front headlock → angle step → block and sacrifice (slow technical reps).
  - Constraint: if partner reaches knees within 2 seconds, you must immediately go behind (automatic transition rule).

- System tags
  - #front-headlock #ukiwaza #angle-first #mount-conversion #go-behind-contingency

- Links
  - Direct connection to front headlock top-control systems (if landing is not fully stable, the back-take becomes the scoring continuation).

## 3.5 Overhook Uki Waza — The Lateral Drop (22:32–34:11) 

- Objective
  - Build the “classic” lateral drop mechanics: overhook + high underhook/lat grip, correct side-fall, and a chest-down finish that defeats roll-through attempts.

- Key concepts
  - Danaher’s finish criterion is explicit: it’s not done until you are **chest/belly facing the mat**; anything short of that is a scramble invitation. :contentReference[oaicite:59]{index=59}
  - The throw is an expression of “give way to force”: forward pressure (especially from an underhook) is fuel, but your mechanics must still work without naïve pressure. :contentReference[oaicite:60]{index=60}
  - One simple meta-rule is elevated to “summary of 10,000 details”: **look where you want them to land**—your body will self-organize around that directionality. :contentReference[oaicite:61]{index=61}

- Gates
  - Overhook must be **elbow-deep** (not shallow); underhook must be **wrist-deep** and high by the shoulder line (lat/shoulder control), not low on the ribs. :contentReference[oaicite:62]{index=62}
  - Must fall to the **opposite side facing away** (body turns ~180° during the fall); falling onto your back or onto the side facing them is failure. :contentReference[oaicite:63]{index=63}
  - Must prevent opponent hand-lock/body-lock:
    - monitor their free hand if possible,
    - or thumb inside their elbow to disrupt locking. :contentReference[oaicite:64]{index=64}

- Positional landmarks
  - Upper-body grips
    - Overhook: elbow deep.
    - Underhook/lat grip: wrist deep, high, turning their shoulders. :contentReference[oaicite:65]{index=65}
  - Lower-body alignment
    - Penetration foot steps just inside; points toward their near heel so you become side-on (avoid stepping in square). :contentReference[oaicite:66]{index=66}
  - Head position
    - Start with head higher / correctly placed so you can rotate; incorrect head placement obstructs the 180° turn and increases roll-through risk. :contentReference[oaicite:67]{index=67}

- Mechanics & details (Primary line)
  - Acquire overhook (common because opponent hunts underhooks) → establish head position and control their free hand. :contentReference[oaicite:68]{index=68}
  - Step your whole body across; insert underhook/lat grip as you step. :contentReference[oaicite:69]{index=69}
  - Sacrifice down and across:
    - drive leg = the stepping leg that pushes off the mat,
    - uki waza leg = the blocking/tripping leg that prevents their step forward. :contentReference[oaicite:70]{index=70}
  - Finish only when chest is down; if you end sideways or supine, you have not finished. :contentReference[oaicite:71]{index=71}

- Branches (defender action → your response)
  - Opponent pushes hard into the underhook (common; underhook is a pushing grip) → this is “perfect time” to hit; you give way and redirect into the fall. :contentReference[oaicite:72]{index=72}
  - Opponent tries to lock hands/body lock → monitor hand or thumb inside elbow; do not allow the lock before you step. :contentReference[oaicite:73]{index=73}
  - Opponent attempts roll-through → correct head-side + chest-down finish makes roll-through difficult; wrong head-side exposes reversal. :contentReference[oaicite:74]{index=74}

- Coaching cues
  - “Elbow-deep overhook; wrist-deep underhook—high.”
  - “Be decisive: don’t step and stop.”
  - “Look where you want him to land.”
  - “It’s not over until chest and belly face the mat.” :contentReference[oaicite:75]{index=75}

- Common errors (Failure-first diagnosis)
  - Falling to your back → works only on poor opponents; creates roll-through risk. :contentReference[oaicite:76]{index=76}
  - Falling to the side facing them → “disaster” (you give them the roll-through/top). :contentReference[oaicite:77]{index=77}
  - Underhook too low / shallow → you cannot turn shoulders; opponent resists and posts.
  - Not blocking their step forward → they step over and crush you.
  - Relaxing before chest-down completion → opponent stands or reverses.

- Drills
  - Slow-motion “180° fall” drill: start from over-under; execute fall so body turns and finishes chest-down every rep.
  - Hand-lock disruption drill: partner tries to lock hands; you must monitor/insert thumb and then step.
  - “Look cue” drill: reps where the only instruction is “pick landing spot with eyes,” then execute.

- System tags
  - #ukiwaza #lateral-drop #overhook #over-under #directionality #chest-down-finish #anti-roll-through

- Links
  - Reappears later as a core sacrifice module (Volume 6 double overhooks uki waza, Volume 8 older-athlete lateral drop variants).

## 3.6 My Favorite Setup — Overhook Uki Waza (34:11–37:03) 

- Objective
  - Solve the main practical problem of the overhook lateral drop: opponent keeps elbow tight so you can’t access the underhook/lat grip needed for the throw.

- Key concepts
  - If the opponent’s elbow is tight and wrist fighting is heavy, you often cannot “win the underhook” directly; you must **force their hand to the mat** to open the lane. :contentReference[oaicite:79]{index=79}
  - Forcing the hand down creates a dilemma:
    - if they stay there, you front headlock / go behind,
    - therefore they must rise,
    - and the rise opens the underhook window for uki waza. :contentReference[oaicite:80]{index=80}

- Gates
  - Must be able to force the hand down reliably from the overhook tie (stabbing step + pump).
  - Must take the underhook immediately as the opponent rises (window is brief).

- Positional landmarks
  - Starting tie: opponent has underhook; you have classic overhook. :contentReference[oaicite:81]{index=81}
  - Stabbing step: you “stab the ground and pump” to force their hand down to the mat. :contentReference[oaicite:82]{index=82}
  - As they rise: underhook lane opens; you insert and have space to step under their weight.

- Mechanics & details (Primary line)
  - From overhook: hit a stabbing step and pump to force their hand to the mat. :contentReference[oaicite:83]{index=83}
  - As they begin to rise (to avoid front headlock danger): shoot your hand into the opening to capture the underhook/lat grip. :contentReference[oaicite:84]{index=84}
  - Step inside and execute uki waza lateral drop mechanics (you now have the space you were missing). :contentReference[oaicite:85]{index=85}

- Branches (defender action → your response)
  - Opponent stays with hand on mat → front headlock is available; go behind becomes immediate threat (forces them to rise). :contentReference[oaicite:86]{index=86}
  - Opponent rises quickly → that rise is your underhook window; take it and drop.

- Coaching cues
  - “Hand to the mat = underhook lane opens.”
  - “Force down, catch on the rise.”
  - “If you can put the hand down, the underhook becomes easy.” :contentReference[oaicite:87]{index=87}

- Common errors (Failure-first diagnosis)
  - Forcing hand down but hesitating on the underhook → window closes; you’re back to elbow-tight defense.
  - Trying to lateral drop without creating space first → you fight their best defense (tight elbow).

- Drills
  - Overhook rounds where your only goal is to force the hand to the mat and immediately take underhook (no throw allowed).
  - Add throw after proficiency: hand down → underhook insert → step and drop (full sequence).

- System tags
  - #overhook #hand-to-mat #stabbing-step #entry-creation #ukiwaza-setup #front-headlock-dilemma

- Links
  - Directly connects to “front headlock/go-behind threat” systems: the opponent’s fear of front headlock is what makes the setup function.

## 3.7 2 on 1 Uki Waza — Strongest Lateral Drop (37:03+) 

- Objective
  - Use 2-on-1 control to create a lateral drop that is harder to resist and safer to “miss,” because the opponent’s posting arm is trapped in front of your chest.

- Key concepts
  - Conventional lateral drop (over-under) is strong but carries sacrifice risk if you mis-execute; the 2-on-1 variation reduces that risk because even a “bad” attempt still leaves you with arm control and recovery options. :contentReference[oaicite:89]{index=89}
  - The 2-on-1 changes two things:
    - opponent cannot post well (arm trapped in front),
    - your entry angle becomes more “across the body” (more perpendicular to their feet). :contentReference[oaicite:90]{index=90}

- Gates
  - Must secure a true 2-on-1 where their arm is across your chest (not behind your back like classic underhook). :contentReference[oaicite:91]{index=91}
  - Must commit the fall at the correct angle: head wants to travel toward the opponent’s far-foot line (perpendicular orientation). :contentReference[oaicite:92]{index=92}

- Positional landmarks
  - 2-on-1 locked; you can drag them forward to prompt stepping.
  - Your fall angle is visibly more “across”: head closer to their far foot; your body more perpendicular to their stance line. :contentReference[oaicite:93]{index=93}

- Mechanics & details (Primary line)
  - Secure 2-on-1 → drag them forward so they step → step whole body in and “catch” → commit down in front of their feet into the pin. :contentReference[oaicite:94]{index=94}
  - Maintain the universal rule: look where you want them to land; that cue remains constant. :contentReference[oaicite:95]{index=95}

- Branches (defender action → your response)
  - If you “screw it up” → recovery is easier because their arm is already trapped in front; nothing immediately catastrophic happens compared to a failed over-under sacrifice. :contentReference[oaicite:96]{index=96}
  - Opponent tries to base/post → arm trap reduces their posting ability; you still land in a pin-friendly relationship.

- Coaching cues
  - “Arm across the chest = no post.”
  - “Fall further across; head toward far foot.”
  - “Look where you want him to land.”

- Common errors (Failure-first diagnosis)
  - Using a weak 2-on-1 that doesn’t trap the arm across your chest → you lose the main advantage of the variation.
  - Falling at conventional over-under angle (not across/perpendicular) → you give them a better resistance line.

- Drills
  - 2-on-1 only rounds: your only takedown allowed is 2-on-1 uki waza; partner’s defense goal is “post and step out.”
  - Miss-recovery drill: intentionally under-commit 20% and practice immediate recovery to top/pin while maintaining 2-on-1.

- System tags
  - #2on1 #ukiwaza #lateral-drop #safer-sacrifice #perpendicular-angle #post-denial

- Links
  - Connects to the series-wide 2-on-1 tie hierarchy and to “older athlete” emphasis on sacrifice throws that do not require long turning or one-legged balance.

## 3.X Explainers (high-density, readable)

### A) Universal Checklist (run this mentally on any Uki Waza attempt)
- Do I have **post denial** (arm trapped / hand forced down)?
- Do I have **step denial** (blocking leg preventing the forward step)?
- Is my **directionality** aligned with the opponent’s head (and am I finishing chest-down)?
- If landing is not clean, do I have an immediate **contingency** (go-behind / pin rebuild)?

### B) “Look where you want them to land” — what it functionally solves
- It automatically biases you into:
  - correct head-side placement (reduces roll-through),
  - correct 180° turn during the fall (away-facing side),
  - correct leg behavior (one drives, one blocks),
  - correct chest-down finish (instead of sideways/supine).

### C) Uki Waza Entry Tree (ASCII)

~~~
ENTRY HANDLE →
├─ Arm drag (can't get angle/back) →
│ lat grip + outside step → (optional foot block) → chest-to-chest pin
│
├─ Opponent reaches over head vs your 2-on-1 →
│ step wide → sacrifice → foot block → pin
│
├─ Outside front headlock →
│ create angle → sit side-on → block across → land top
│ └─ if scramble/they knee-up → go-behind
│
└─ Overhook / over-under →
elbow-deep overhook + high underhook/lat grip →
decisive step → one leg drives / one blocks →
finish chest-down
├─ Setup if elbow tight: stabbing step → hand to mat → underhook lane opens
└─ Stronger/safer variant: replace underhook with 2-on-1 trap

~~~

N03 — ASCII Execution Gates (Mechanics + Diagnosis)
Top-to-bottom pipeline. If a gate fails, fix it before committing to the sacrifice.

GATE 0 — Correct module selection
- Arm drag stalled? → 3.1 / 3.2
- 2-on-1 and they headlock? → 3.3
- Outside front headlock? → 3.4
- Overhook/over-under lane? → 3.5 (or 3.6 to create it)
- 2-on-1 safest lateral? → 3.7

GATE 1 — Post denial is REAL (non-negotiable)
- Is the posting arm trapped/controlled?
  - Arm drag: the dragged arm must still be denied as a post.
  - 2-on-1: arm must be across your chest and controlled.
  - Overhook: overhook must be elbow-deep enough to compromise posting.
IF NO → you are “sacrificing into a post” (high reversal/scramble risk).
FIX → tighten trap / re-grip / reset.

GATE 2 — You have deep steering / attachment (lat wrap or head control)
IF NO → they rotate out and you land beside them.
FIX → deepen lat clamp, keep chest close, keep head/shoulder relationship.

GATE 3 — Angle + directionality is correct
- Are you driving toward no-base space (not into their posts)?
- Are you turning with the throw (not falling straight back)?
IF NO → they step/post and you lose.
FIX → step off-line first; “pick landing spot” with eyes; rotate.

GATE 4 — Step denial exists (block/catch the step)
- Foot catch/block is ideal.
- No-foot-contact is acceptable ONLY if Gate 1 (post denial) is strong.
IF NO → they simply step past and crush you.
FIX → adjust block leg OR revert to no-contact only when post denial is secured.

GATE 5 — Sacrifice mechanics are safe (side fall + correct turn)
- Fall to side (not flat back).
- Turn so you can finish chest-down / chest-to-chest.
IF NO → roll-through risk spikes.
FIX → drill the “turn + elbow/side landing” until automatic.

GATE 6 — Finish criterion is satisfied (you are not done yet)
- Finish only when your chest is over them AND you are pin-ready (chest-down orientation).
IF NOT FINISHED → treat it as “unfinished work,” not as a completed takedown.

CONTINGENCY RULE (mandatory)
- If landing is not clean and they knee-up quickly → immediate go-behind.
  Do not stall on your knees trying to “hold something that isn’t there.”
