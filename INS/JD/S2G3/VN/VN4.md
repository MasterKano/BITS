## Volume 4 Doctrine Summary (Operational)
- This volume is a “late-defense” toolkit: when a shot is already deep (single/high single/double/high crotch) or you were already taken down, you can still reverse the situation by (1) denying posts, (2) applying correct directionality, and (3) insisting on a chest-up finishing posture so the scramble resolves in your favor.
- Core repeated laws:
  - Post denial: remove the hand/foot that can base out (often by wrist capture + directional throw).
  - Directionality: throw toward where they cannot base (often “toward the direction their toes point” or “perpendicular to the ankle-line”).
  - Finish posture: do not admire the throw—finish with your chest/head higher so the pin is immediate.

### 4.1 Sumi Gaeshi — Whizzer + Wrist vs Single Leg (0:00–10:40)
- Objective
  - Convert a single leg (knee-down single or high single) into a decisive sumi gaeshi that reliably ends in top position/pin.

- Gates
  - Correct wrist: grip the wrist of the **outside arm** (the arm that can post). Wrong wrist = they post and you get nothing.
  - Correct direction: do **not** throw straight back into their post; throw “around the corner” toward the inside leg / direction their toes point.
  - Entry depth: your hop must pass the “ankle-line” (imaginary line joining their ankles) or they can drop hips and stop the throw.

- Controls (positional landmarks)
  - Shallow whizzer locked + wrist grip (“two-on-one” on the posting arm).
  - Shoelaces posted to their hamstring/thigh (do not let the foot float).
  - Ear-to-ear head connection (head close = strong; head far = weak).
  - Hop angle: quarter-turn hop so your entry aims toward their inside/post foot.

- Primary line
  - Find balance as the leg is lifted → secure the correct wrist (outside arm) → lock shallow whizzer tight.
  - Shoelaces to hamstring + ear-to-ear head position.
  - Hop “around the corner” (quarter turn) toward the inside leg, passing the ankle-line.
  - Finish with chest higher than their chest; hand to belly/stomach to prevent them turning up; come up into underhook + side pin.

- Branches (defender action → your response)
  - They keep death-grip on your leg during flight → you are often pulled onto mount/very strong top; accept and stabilize.
  - They let go mid-throw → you must win the race: sit up immediately (chest higher), post hand on belly, underhook and pin.

- Failure-first diagnosis
  - Common errors
    - Grabbing the inside wrist (they post with outside hand) → always take outside wrist.
    - Hopping straight back instead of around the corner → they base with foot and stop you.
    - Not passing ankle-line → they drop hips and kill amplitude/finish.
    - Finishing flat beside them → scramble loss; always chest-up finish.
  - Coaching cues
    - “Outside wrist + tight whizzer.”
    - “Hop toward the inside leg—past the ankle line.”
    - “Ear-to-ear, shoelaces to hamstring.”
    - “Chest higher than chest.”

- Drills (explicit/implied)
  - 10 reps/side: high single → outside wrist + whizzer → shoelaces post → corner hop → chest-up finish.
  - Reaction drill: partner releases leg mid-throw; your task is immediate sit-up + belly post + underhook pin.

- System tags

- Links

  - Feeds directly into “yoko sumi” principles later (same directionality obsession).

### 4.2 Sumi Gaeshi — Lat + Wrist vs Single Leg (10:40–15:07)
- Objective
  - Use an alternate upper-body anchor (lat grip) to increase head/posture control while countering the single leg.

- Gates
  - Still must control the correct wrist (outside arm).
  - Lat grip is condition-dependent: can slip when sweaty; choose whizzer if purchase is unreliable.

- Controls (positional landmarks)
  - Wrist grip on outside arm + lat muscle grip on far side.
  - Shoulder pressure on top of their head (improves head/posture suppression).
  - Same hop mechanics as 4.1 (past ankle-line, toward inside leg).

- Primary line
  - Balance → correct wrist → replace whizzer with lat grip.
  - Hop inside past ankle-line; finish chest over chest + belly post.
  - Choose lat grip early/dry conditions for stronger rotation; revert to whizzer when slippery.

- Branches
  - If head starts slipping due to sweat → abandon lat grip preference; return to whizzer-and-wrist (more robust retention).

- Failure-first diagnosis
  - Common errors
    - Forcing lat grip when it’s slipping → you lose attachment and fall into a scramble.
  - Coaching cues
    - “Lat when dry; whizzer when slippery.”
    - “Same hop, same finish—only the grip changes.”

- Drills
  - Conditioned rounds: 2 minutes lat+wrist only; 2 minutes whizzer+wrist only; compare reliability.

- System tags
  - #sumi-gaeshi #lat-grip #single-leg-counter #slippage-management

- Links
  - Directly complements 4.1 as the “same throw, different anchor” decision.

### 4.3 Sumi Gaeshi — Chest Wrap vs Double Leg (15:07–21:05)
- Objective
  - Survive and reverse a deep double leg when you feel you are “about to go down” (late defense).

- Gates
  - This is second-line defense: if you can down-block/sprawl/go-behind safely, do that first.
  - Same-side rule: if their head is on your right, throw over your right shoulder (never across your body).

- Controls (positional landmarks)
  - Chest wrap lock around torso (pinch elbows back to prevent them pulling out).
  - Step penetration: do not sit from far; step up deeply (ideally past their knee line).
  - Sumi hook: foot inside as deep as possible; knee ideally outside (but accept “knee on belly-button line” if speed forces it).

- Primary line
  - Recognize “hips captured, going down” → wrap chest immediately.
  - Step the foot on the head-side inside their legs; step the second foot up toward their feet.
  - Sit and take them over the same-side shoulder (head-side = throw-side).
  - Prefer rotation to come up on top; if not, hip-heist up into sprawl/go-behind when they rise.

- Branches
  - Best-case: you rotate up on top → pin.
  - Worst-case: you don’t rotate up; they come up → you follow into immediate go-behind (still a scoring reversal).

- Failure-first diagnosis
  - Common errors
    - Throwing across your body → you gift them side control.
    - Feet too far back when you sit → no depth, no leverage.
  - Coaching cues
    - “Head-side = throw-side.”
    - “Step deep before you sit.”
    - “Try to rotate on top; if not, hip-heist to go-behind.”

- Drills
  - Late-defense drill: partner hits fast double and “locks hips”; you must chest-wrap and throw same-side.
  - Recovery drill: partner denies rotation-on-top; you hip-heist to go-behind immediately.

- System tags
  - #double-leg-counter #late-defense #chest-wrap #same-side-rule

- Links
  - Connects to the “dilemma mindset” repeated later: if you can’t finish throw → convert to go-behind.

### 4.4 Sumi Gaeshi — Butterfly vs Double Leg (21:05–25:17)
- Objective
  - Apply the familiar butterfly sumi gaeshi (ground sweep mechanics) as a last-ditch counter to a deep double leg.

- Gates
  - Grips must be fast and simple: tricep + lat (no need to clasp hands).
  - Foot must be inside their knee line; you “fold” with their drive, not against it.
  - Finish “until knee touches mat” (complete the fold/turn; don’t stop early).

- Controls (positional landmarks)
  - Tricep control (often over back of head with tricep) + far lat control.
  - Foot inside their knee; body folds into butterfly-sumi alignment.
  - Direction: you sit/fold in the direction they are already driving.

- Primary line
  - Deep double is in; accept you’re late → grip tricep + lat immediately.
  - Put foot inside knee; sit and fold into butterfly sumi.
  - Hook elevates as you turn them; continue until your knee touches mat.
  - Come up into top pin.

- Branches
  - They sprawl hips back (weight hangs back) → abandon hoist; throw legs back, snap them down, go-behind.
  - They drive into you to finish → hoist/turn is easier; commit and come up on top.

- Failure-first diagnosis
  - Common errors
    - Trying to “fight the drive” with upright resistance → you get finished.
    - Foot not inside knee → you can’t load/turn them.
  - Coaching cues
    - “Fold where he drives.”
    - “Tricep + lat—fast grips.”
    - “Finish until knee touches.”

- Drills
  - Alternating reps: partner drives hard vs explained sprawl-back defense; you choose hoist vs go-behind.

- System tags
  - #butterfly-sumi #double-leg-counter #dilemma #finish-to-knee-touch

- Links
  - Bridges “guard sweep mechanics” into standing defense: same engine, different entry.

### 4.5 Sumi Gaeshi / Yoko Sumi Counter vs High Crotch (25:17–29:27)
- Objective
  - Counter the high crotch (head outside single) with a yoko sumi style pin-ending throw; optionally convert head-inside single to head-outside to access the same counter.

- Gates
  - Head control first (chin strap or hand-through/back-of-hand-on-chest).
  - Knee-out/foot-in structure is mandatory (foot inside, knee outside).
  - Second-foot step is what turns the position into the pinning counter (don’t stay static).

- Controls (positional landmarks)
  - Head control + lat control (second hand on lat).
  - Foot inside their leg; knee outside.
  - Second foot steps up; you turn them away into a half-nelson/underhook pin alignment.

- Primary line
  - Catch head (chin strap or hand-through) + lat.
  - Set foot inside/knee outside; drop to squat alignment.
  - Step second foot up and turn them into the pin (half-nelson + underhook structure appears as they try to turn).

- Branches
  - Versus head-inside single: pass their head to outside first, then lock the same mechanics and finish.
  - If they threaten to switch to double: thumb posting/frames can help, but the counter remains available if you keep arm position.

- Failure-first diagnosis
  - Common errors
    - Knee centered (not outside) → lever fails.
    - No second-foot step → you stall and they recover.
  - Coaching cues
    - “Foot in, knee out.”
    - “Step the second foot—then pin.”

- Drills
  - Conversion reps: head-inside single → pass head outside → yoko sumi finish.

- System tags
  - #high-crotch-counter #yoko-sumi #head-pass #pin-finish

- Links
  - Connects to VN02 cow-catcher finishes conceptually (head/shoulder control resolves into pin).

### 4.6 Yoko Sumi Gaeshi on the Mat (29:27–34:12)
- Objective
  - After being taken down (double/single driving you down), reverse with yoko sumi mechanics to end in top pin.

- Gates
  - Knee must be outside; foot inside (center-knee kills the move).
  - Recline to shoulder to load their weight; do not try to lift from upright.
  - Lift path is “out and around,” not straight sideways.

- Controls (positional landmarks)
  - Upper grip: either over-back grip or inside head control (both acceptable).
  - Knee outside, foot inside as sumi hook.
  - Shoulder recline + push off mat to elevate/rotate them.

- Primary line
  - As they drive you down, set knee-out/foot-in.
  - Heist/adjust to slide in underneath.
  - Recline to shoulder; elevate with hook; lift out-and-around.
  - Come up into conventional pins.

- Branches
  - They sprawl hips back → snap legs back and go straight behind (dilemma: hang back = go-behind easier; drive in = hoist easier).

- Failure-first diagnosis
  - Common errors
    - Knee in the middle → lever fails.
    - Staying upright while trying to lift → you cannot load them.
  - Coaching cues
    - “Knee outside, foot inside.”
    - “Shoulder down to load—then lift out and around.”
    - “Drive-in = hoist; hang-back = go-behind.”

- Drills
  - Start-from-takedown drill: partner finishes double; you immediately insert knee-out/foot-in and reverse.
  - Dilemma drill: partner alternates drive-in vs hips-back sprawl; you choose hoist vs go-behind.

- System tags
  - #yoko-sumi #mat-reversal #knee-out-foot-in #dilemma

- Links
  - Directly supports “never accept being taken down as the end of the exchange” doctrine.

### 4.7 2-on-1 Yoko Sumi Gaeshi (34:12–39:03)
- Objective
  - Use a 2-on-1 structure (wrist→elbow climb + lat) to create the angle and foot placement needed for yoko sumi with reliable directionality.

- Gates
  - Convert wrist control to elbow control (stronger steering).
  - Split their feet before entry.
  - Directionality: throw perpendicular to the line joining their ankles; never “back toward yourself.”

- Controls (positional landmarks)
  - Elbow control (hand climbs up arm) + far lat grip.
  - Step sequence: step in one, then step in two (second step sets direction).
  - End state: chest higher than chest, pin.

- Primary line
  - Establish 2-on-1 → climb to elbow → grip far lat.
  - Separate their feet; step in one; step in two; throw over shoulder with right foot.
  - Finish chest-up; crossface/arm control prevents their turn; settle into pin.

- Branches
  - Sometimes first step places foot inside thigh before sitting—acceptable variation if it preserves directionality.

- Failure-first diagnosis
  - Common errors
    - Sitting naively without the correct angle → they base on near foot and you fail.
    - Throwing “back toward yourself” → runs into the post.
  - Coaching cues
    - “Perpendicular to the ankle-line.”
    - “Step one sets penetration; step two sets direction.”

- Drills
  - Footwork-only reps: 2-on-1 grips → step 1/2 → freeze and check ankle-line perpendicularity.

- System tags
  - #2on1 #yoko-sumi #directionality #footwork-1-2

- Links
  - Conceptually pairs with VN06 “no angle, no throw,” but here encoded as ankle-line perpendicular rule.

### 4.8 Arm Drag Sumi Gaeshi (39:03–44:09)
- Objective
  - Use the arm drag reaction (opponent steps back to deny back take) as the trigger for yoko sumi entry.

- Gates
  - Arm drag must actually force the step-back; that step-back is the “opening.”
  - Entry still requires deep steps past the ankle-line.
  - Head direction rule: head should fall toward the **far** foot, not the near foot.

- Controls (positional landmarks)
  - Arm drag with hand/wrist inside; over-the-top lat grip.
  - Step count: one, two, and (often) three as you fully enter and turn.
  - Directionality: throw perpendicular to ankle-line; head to far foot = success.

- Primary line
  - Hit arm drag; expect step-back.
  - Grip lat; step in deep (first step to ankle-line, second step past it).
  - Fall across their body with head toward far foot; add sumi hook; finish into pin.

- Branches
  - If their leg stays forward (naive) → back take is available; but the “educated” step-back is what feeds the yoko sumi.

- Failure-first diagnosis
  - Common errors
    - Head falls toward near foot → they never go over.
    - Not stepping past ankle-line → base survives.
  - Coaching cues
    - “Head to far foot.”
    - “Step to the line; then past the line.”
    - “Fall across the body, not into the base.”

- Drills
  - Reaction drill: partner must step back on drag; you must immediately enter yoko sumi without resetting.

- System tags
  - #arm-drag #yoko-sumi #reaction-entry #head-to-far-foot

- Links
  - Integrates seamlessly with arm drag/back-take systems: if they deny the back, they grant the throw.

### 4.9 Ude Gaeshi — Mechanics (44:09–50:28)
- Objective
  - Execute a no-gi ude gaeshi using a figure-four “standing kimura” that forces either (a) an arm break if they stay, or (b) a forward roll that becomes the takedown.

- Gates
  - Grip specification is non-negotiable:
    - Underclasp palm **up** on wrist + **thumb** engaged.
    - Figure-four lock with the second hand (no-thumb is fine there).
    - Keep their elbow as close to **90°** as possible (works straight, but worse).
  - Body position:
    - Maintain an angle (2-on-1 naturally provides it).
    - Ear to shoulder / forehead at neck (head close or the connection weakens).
  - Hand placement battle:
    - Their fist must be outside the thigh (ideally “back pocket”), not inside/in front.

- Controls (positional landmarks)
  - Figure-four (kimura-like) on the arm from a 2-on-1.
  - Their fist outside the thigh; your head tight to shoulder/neck.
  - Your body faces the direction their toes point; you fall in front of them with a drive leg.

- Primary line
  - Lock figure-four with correct underclasp + thumb; keep elbow ~90°.
  - Win the fist position: outside thigh → back pocket.
  - Step your foot outside their foot to align.
  - Commit body to the floor in front of them (sacrifice + squat), push off drive leg, and send them through in the direction you are facing.
  - Finish on your side (never flat on back) and come up head/chest higher for the pin.

- Branches
  - If they try to square up → head tight + angle maintenance preserves the setup.
  - If their fist stays inside thigh → the throw’s torque is compromised; you must fix fist position before committing.

- Failure-first diagnosis
  - Common errors
    - No underclasp / palm-down 2-on-1 style grip → inadequate mechanics for this throw.
    - No thumb on wrist → weaker control.
    - Head far from shoulder → connection breaks.
  - Coaching cues
    - “Palm up + thumb.”
    - “Keep it 90°.”
    - “Fist outside thigh (back pocket).”
    - “Fall facing the same way his toes point.”

- Drills
  - Static positional: 2-on-1 → lock figure-four → win fist outside thigh (no throw yet).
  - Commitment drill: from correct grips, practice “fall to side + push off drive leg” without rushing.

- System tags
  - #ude-gaeshi #figure-four #standing-kimura #dilemma-throw #commitment

- Links
  - Connects to kimura control mechanics, but applied as a standing-to-ground “roll-or-break” dilemma.

### 4.10 Ude Gaeshi — Application (50:28–54:38)
- Objective
  - Apply the mechanics with the correct level of commitment so the throw works against live resistance.

- Gates
  - This is not a “timid” technique: you must commit your body to the floor; going to knees slowly fails.
  - Step foot outside their foot for alignment before the fall.
  - Finish posture: do not end lying down; end with forehead/head on chest line so scramble is blocked.

- Controls (positional landmarks)
  - Right foot outside their right foot (example alignment).
  - Fall facing same direction as opponent (not facing them).
  - Forehead ideally on stomach/chest during finish to prevent turn-up.

- Primary line
  - Lock figure-four; fist outside thigh; ear to shoulder.
  - Step outside foot → commit fall (side finish).
  - Head blocks their scramble; arm prevents turning away; hand can post across belly to settle pin.

- Branches
  - As confidence increases, you can roll all the way through (higher velocity), but only after the base mechanics are stable.

- Failure-first diagnosis
  - Common errors
    - Dropping to knees (“timidity”) → opponent walks into you; you lose base.
    - Landing flat on back → they out-scramble and come up on top.
  - Coaching cues
    - “No knees. Commit to the mat.”
    - “Side finish, head higher.”

- Drills
  - Progressive commitment: start with controlled drop mechanics; increase speed only after consistent chest-up finishes.

- System tags
  - #ude-gaeshi #commitment #finish-chest-up #anti-scramble

- Links
  - Reinforces the volume-wide “finish posture wins the aftermath” principle.

### 4.11 Ude Gaeshi — Setups (54:38–59:16)
- Objective
  - Create reliable pathways into the specific 2-on-1 required for ude gaeshi (palm-up + thumb + figure-four).

- Gates
  - Entry must result in palm-up wrist orientation; if initial grip is palm-down, convert before committing.
  - You are primarily setting up the 2-on-1; the throw is “downstream” once the structure is correct.

- Controls (positional landmarks)
  - Setup A: “two hands on one wrist” → shoulder-to-shoulder → classic 2-on-1 → convert to figure-four.
  - Setup B: opponent has inside ties → you go to elbow/wrist → thread through → 2-on-1 → figure-four.
  - Setup C: collar tie/wrist exchanges → cross-wrist to 2-on-1 → rotate grip to palm-up → figure-four.
  - Setup D: leg attack defense (single on knee) → whizzer + weight over → break grip → return to 2-on-1 → figure-four.

- Primary line
  - Acquire 2-on-1 by common hand-fighting routes.
  - Convert grip to palm-up; lock figure-four.
  - Immediately pursue fist-outside-thigh condition; then execute throw.

- Branches
  - If opponent grabs/peels your 2-on-1 → use that engagement to re-thread and re-lock; insist on palm-up conversion.

- Failure-first diagnosis
  - Common errors
    - Entering figure-four with wrong wrist orientation → weaker throw and higher escape risk.
  - Coaching cues
    - “Get the 2-on-1 first; fix palm-up second; lock figure-four third.”

- Drills
  - Setup chains: each rep begins from a different tie (wrist, inside ties, collar) and must end in correct figure-four before any throw attempt.

- System tags
  - #ude-gaeshi #entries #2on1-to-figure4 #handfighting

- Links
  - Ties back to volume’s earlier 2-on-1 yoko sumi work: 2-on-1 is a recurring “throw gateway.”

### 4.12 Ude Gaeshi — Mistakes to Avoid (59:16+)
- Objective
  - Eliminate the predictable failure modes that turn ude gaeshi into a scramble-loss instead of a takedown-win.

- Gates
  - Hand placement: do not force hand between legs; put fist outside thigh / behind back (back pocket).
  - Arm angle: 90° is preferred; straight arm can work but is slower and easier to resist.
  - Commitment: no slow-motion knee drops; step through and rotate decisively.
  - Finish: do not lie down at the end; come up forehead to chest and collect the pin.

- Controls (positional landmarks)
  - “Correct” finish posture: forehead on chest line, chest higher, arm prevents turn-away.

- Primary line (error → correction)
  - Error: force fist between legs → Correction: fist outside thigh/back pocket for real shoulder torque.
  - Error: allow arm to straighten → Correction: fight for 90°; if it straightens badly, convert to arm drag/back take (dilemma).
  - Error: timidity/knees → Correction: commit body to mat; rotate through; push off one foot.
  - Error: celebrate/lie down → Correction: immediately sit up into chest-up finish; walk them down to pins.

- Failure-first diagnosis
  - Coaching cues
    - “Outside thigh, not between legs.”
    - “90° if possible; straight-arm = switch to drag/back.”
    - “Commit, then collect the pin.”
