# DN01 — Doctrine Notes (Concepts governing the entire series)

## DN01.1 Introduction (0:00–4:30)  [oai_citation:1‡Titles.txt](file-service://file-AiDgUWXoCWC5V4jFHjKJBi)
- Goal
  - Establish what this series is optimizing for in no-gi Jiu-Jitsu: takedowns that naturally lead into scoring-quality top control.

- Key concepts
  - The series treats upper body takedowns as a complement to leg attacks, not a replacement; the value is having two “axes” of attack so opponents who over-defend one become vulnerable to the other.  [oai_citation:2‡John Danaher Standing2Ground Upper Body Takedowns 1.txt](file-service://file-6AN9aXdvAh5UCznGtNTqZj)
  - Danaher frames the system around throws with minimal athletic requirements and includes explicit adaptations for older/less athletic athletes as part of the same program.  [oai_citation:3‡John Danaher Standing2Ground Upper Body Takedowns 1.txt](file-service://file-6AN9aXdvAh5UCznGtNTqZj)
  - The “why” matters: if you do not understand the scoring and transition problem being solved, you will learn techniques as isolated moves and fail to apply them consistently.

- Positional landmarks
  - The metric of success is not amplitude; it is the ability to land with torso connection and stabilize (pin-ready orientation).

- Mechanics & details
  - Treat Volume 1 as the operating manual: it defines the scoring problem, the risk/benefit profile, and the training methods that make later volumes work.

- Coaching cues
  - “Learn the problem first; then the techniques make sense.”
  - “Control-first takedowns, not takedowns-then-scramble.”

- Common errors
  - Learning later-volume throws without first learning the tie/stance habits that make them safe and repeatable.

- Links

## DN01.2 Upper Body Takedown Concepts — The Central Problem of Takedowns in Jiu Jitsu (4:30–14:52) 
- Goal
  - Identify why many leg-tackle takedowns fail to convert to scores: the takedown happens before the required torso control is secured.

- Key concepts
  - In Jiu-Jitsu scoring terms, the opponent frequently turns to knees/turtle to prevent a clean takedown score; controlling only the legs makes the scoring criteria hard to satisfy against trained opponents.  [oai_citation:4‡John Danaher Standing2Ground Upper Body Takedowns 1.txt](file-service://file-6AN9aXdvAh5UCznGtNTqZj)
  - Leg tackles often create a difficult sequence: you finish on legs (chest-to-thigh), then must climb to torso control inside a short window while the opponent is actively scrambling.
  - Upper body takedowns “reverse the order of operations”: you secure upper body contact/control first, then complete the takedown, so you land closer to (or already within) the control needed to score.  [oai_citation:5‡John Danaher Standing2Ground Upper Body Takedowns 1.txt](file-service://file-6AN9aXdvAh5UCznGtNTqZj)

- Positional landmarks
  - Typical leg-tackle endpoint (problem state): arms locked on legs while opponent rotates to knees.
  - Desired scoring endpoint: chest-to-chest or chest-to-back contact with the ability to keep them grounded through the count.  [oai_citation:6‡John Danaher Standing2Ground Upper Body Takedowns 1.txt](file-service://file-6AN9aXdvAh5UCznGtNTqZj)

- Mechanics & details
  - Diagnose takedown success as a two-stage process:
    1) Put opponent on the floor.
    2) Prevent the “erase-the-score” recovery (knee turn/turtle/stand-up) long enough to stabilize.
  - Build takedown selection around landing geometry: choose entries and finishes that naturally keep you connected to the torso.

- Coaching cues
  - “Down is not the finish—control is.”
  - “Expect the knee-turn; engineer against it.”

- Common errors
  - Confusing “I got him down” with “I can hold him down.”
  - Chasing legs after the fall instead of upgrading to torso control immediately.

- Links
  - VN02 (Cow Catcher): direct answer to the knee-turn/turtle recovery.
  - VN03 (Uki Waza): lateral drop/sacrifice solutions that land in control when structured correctly.

## DN01.3 Advantages of Upper Body Takedowns (14:52–19:32) 
- Goal
  - Understand why upper body takedowns are strategically valuable in no-gi Jiu-Jitsu relative to leg tackles.

- Key concepts
  - Upper body takedowns often finish already in chest-to-chest contact, reducing the “climb” from legs to torso and increasing scoring conversion.  [oai_citation:7‡John Danaher Standing2Ground Upper Body Takedowns 1.txt](file-service://file-6AN9aXdvAh5UCznGtNTqZj)
  - They typically reduce exposure to common leg-entry risks (neck/arm exposure during deep level changes) and reduce the likelihood of being flattened under a heavy sprawl when an attempt fails. 
  - Tactical switching matters: many athletes defend one axis (legs or upper body) better than the other; being able to switch increases takedown probability.  [oai_citation:8‡John Danaher Standing2Ground Upper Body Takedowns 1.txt](file-service://file-6AN9aXdvAh5UCznGtNTqZj)

- Positional landmarks
  - You should feel “torso-first connection” before the opponent hits the mat.

- Mechanics & details
  - Choose finishes that keep your posture and head position high enough to avoid guillotine-style risk while staying connected to the torso.  [oai_citation:9‡John Danaher Standing2Ground Upper Body Takedowns 1.txt](file-service://file-6AN9aXdvAh5UCznGtNTqZj)
  - Treat “no need to climb” as an explicit design goal: if you are landing disconnected, you are not capturing the primary advantage.

- Coaching cues
  - “Reverse the order: control first, takedown second.”
  - “If you land connected, the score becomes routine.”

- Common errors
  - Using upper-body ties but finishing in a way that breaks connection, recreating the scramble problem you were trying to avoid.

- Links
  - VN05–VN06: clinch-based throws designed to land in pins.
  - VN08: conservative variants that preserve the same advantage under lower athleticism.

## DN01.4 Disadvantages of Upper Body Takedowns (19:32–25:44) 
- Goal
  - Understand the risk profile so you can train the stabilizers that keep you safe and on top.

- Key concepts
  - Upper body takedowns have an “all-or-nothing” quality: when they work, they often work decisively; when they fail, you can be countered quickly because both athletes are in close contact with throwing potential.  [oai_citation:10‡John Danaher Standing2Ground Upper Body Takedowns 1.txt](file-service://file-6AN9aXdvAh5UCznGtNTqZj)
  - Close contact favors the athlete who initiates first and manages off-balancing and direction better; hesitation creates counter windows.  [oai_citation:11‡John Danaher Standing2Ground Upper Body Takedowns 1.txt](file-service://file-6AN9aXdvAh5UCznGtNTqZj)
  - Momentum management is critical (roll-through risk). The system’s implied answer is disciplined angles, posture, and finish orientation rather than “bigger throws.”  [oai_citation:12‡John Danaher Standing2Ground Upper Body Takedowns 1.txt](file-service://file-6AN9aXdvAh5UCznGtNTqZj)

- Positional landmarks
  - Unsafe: square clinch with no angle; hips rising above opponent’s hips during rotation; head drifting away; landing extended rather than connected.

- Mechanics & details
  - Treat every throw as “throw + pin.” Your landing must be engineered to keep you on top (chest-down/chest-connected), not just to make them fall.

- Coaching cues
  - “Angle before action.”
  - “Throw and pin, not throw and hope.”

- Common errors
  - Throwing from symmetrical ties without first creating angle/off-balance.
  - Relaxing at the moment of success and allowing roll-through.

- Links
  - VN03: structured lateral drops that prioritize landing geometry.
  - VN05–VN06: knee-drop and alignment safety themes.

## DN01.5 Minimum Athletic Requirements to Perform the Moves in This Video (25:44–31:16) 
- Goal
  - Define what “minimum athleticism” actually means in practice and what to train when you do not want to rely on speed or explosiveness.

- Key concepts
  - The system is built to be learned by most people; the core requirements are stance integrity, basic rotational ability, and the ability to maintain posture and connection through movement—not elite speed.  [oai_citation:13‡John Danaher Standing2Ground Upper Body Takedowns 1.txt](file-service://file-6AN9aXdvAh5UCznGtNTqZj)
  - Many takedowns in the series are framed as mechanically progressive (hustle-style) rather than maximal amplitude lifts.
  - Athleticism is treated as a variable; technique and alignment should be able to substitute for raw speed.

- Positional landmarks
  - You should be able to:
    - keep knees bent and base under you,
    - rotate without folding at the waist,
    - maintain head/hand connection while stepping.

- Mechanics & details
  - If you “need speed,” diagnose what is missing:
    - insufficient angle,
    - insufficient off-balance,
    - poor hip placement,
    - or broken connection.

- Coaching cues
  - “Make the position do the work.”
  - “Correct angles reduce athletic demand.”

- Common errors
  - Selecting the highest-amplitude versions too early instead of using conservative entries and finishes.

- Links
  - VN08: explicit adaptations for older/less athletic athletes.

# TN01 — Training Notes (Methods to build the attributes for later volumes)

## TN01.1 Entering Into Upper Body Grips: The 3 Main Methods — Legs as a Gateway to the Upper Body (31:16–33:39) 
- Goal
  - Build reliable entry pathways into upper-body ties without treating the clinch as “random.”

- Key concepts
  - Method 1: use your leg attacks to force reactions, then climb to upper-body control as they defend.
  - Method 2: use their leg attacks as a predictable opportunity to connect to head/shoulders/waist and turn defense into offense.
  - Method 3: direct hand-fighting to win inside position and arrive at preferred ties.
  - The unifying theme is that legs often function as the forcing mechanism, while upper-body control becomes the scoring mechanism.  [oai_citation:14‡John Danaher Standing2Ground Upper Body Takedowns 1.txt](file-service://file-6AN9aXdvAh5UCznGtNTqZj)

- Positional landmarks
  - You are “in the system” when you can connect to the torso (underhooks/overhooks/body lock/2-on-1/head ties) without being forced to disengage.

- Mechanics & details
  - Train the transitions explicitly (not just the endpoints):
    - leg entry → defensive reaction → upper-body tie acquisition.
  - Build a habit of upgrading from “contact” to “control” (head position + elbow position + hip distance).

- Coaching cues
  - “Use legs to open doors; finish with upper body control.”
  - “Win the tie, then choose the throw.”

- Common errors
  - Viewing a defended shot as failure rather than as the gateway to the clinch.
  - Staying too long on the legs after the defense presents upper-body handles.

- Links
  - VN02–VN04: direct demonstrations of this gateway principle as counters and conversions.

## TN01.2 Defending a Shot (33:39–35:22) 
- Goal
  - Defend singles/doubles in a way that immediately converts to upper-body control rather than “sprawl and reset.”

- Key concepts
  - Shot defense is an offensive opportunity: the opponent’s commitment presents head/shoulder lines that can be captured into front headlock/chin strap/underhook/tight waist pathways.
  - The conversion must be fast; if you only stop the shot and pause, the opponent resets to neutral.

- Positional landmarks
  - Head/shoulder connection is established immediately after the shot is stalled.
  - Hips are safe (not extended), and hands are moving “up the body” rather than lingering on the legs.

- Mechanics & details
  - Stop the penetration, then immediately attach to:
    - head control (chin strap/front headlock), and/or
    - underhook/tight waist, depending on the angle of the shot.
  - Use the attachment to create either:
    - a go-behind/back exposure, or
    - a direct upper-body takedown entry.

- Coaching cues
  - “Defend the shot by winning the next grip.”
  - “Stop the legs, then climb upstairs.”

- Common errors
  - Sprawl and disengage (wasting the advantage the opponent just gave you).
  - Leaving head position uncontrolled so the opponent re-attacks or stands up cleanly.

- Links
  - VN02: shot-stoppage entries to Cow Catcher.
  - VN04: shot counters into Sumi Gaeshi / Ude Gaeshi.

## TN01.3 Upper Body Grip Fighting (35:22–40:56) 
- Goal
  - Develop a repeatable tie-up hierarchy and understand which variables (head/elbows/hips) determine who can progress to throws.

- Key concepts
  - Grip fighting is positional play at standing range. You are trying to:
    - win inside position (underhooks/inside ties),
    - trap arms (overhooks/2-on-1),
    - and use head position to deny the opponent’s ability to square and insert hips.
  - Head position is a steering and denial tool; if head position is neutral, ties tend to be temporary.

- Positional landmarks
  - Underhook: inside arm is deep; head is positioned to prevent them turning freely.
  - Overhook: clamp at/above elbow line so they cannot post/pummel easily.
  - 2-on-1: isolated arm creates angles and limits their ability to connect torso-to-torso.

- Mechanics & details
  - Build tie acquisition as a sequence:
    - contact → inside/outside win → head position → elbow closure → hip distance management → angle creation.
  - Drill transitions between ties (because opponents will force changes):
    - underhook ↔ overhook ↔ 2-on-1 ↔ head control.

- Coaching cues
  - “Inside plus head wins.”
  - “Elbows close; angles open.”

- Common errors
  - Winning an arm position but leaving head position neutral.
  - Reaching with arms while posture collapses, giving up underhooks.

- Links
  - VN05–VN07: specific throwing suites that begin from these tie hierarchies.
  - VN06: double overhooks/underhooks as “late-stage” results of good grip fighting.

## TN01.4 The First Great Training Method: Pummelling (40:56–50:49) 
- Goal
  - Use structured pummelling to build inside-position acquisition, recovery, and realistic clinch comfort.

- Key concepts
  - Pummelling is only valuable when it is constrained enough to resemble reality: head movement, elbow closure, and posture discipline must be trained, not assumed.
  - Adding “micro-scoring” converts pummelling from warm-up to a skill lab; it teaches what matters (inside position, head control, off-balance, back exposure).

- Positional landmarks
  - Knees bent, stance stable, head active.
  - Elbows close enough to deny easy entries, but mobile enough to thread and recover.

- Mechanics & details
  - Phase progression:
    1) Head movement first (learn to win space before threading arms).
    2) Add arm threading with posture intact (no reaching/leaning).
    3) Add competitive pummelling with constraints and scoring.
  - Examples of scoring layers (use as teaching constraints):
    - point for double underhooks achieved,
    - point for front headlock capture,
    - point for forced hand-to-mat off-balance,
    - point for “go-behind angle” achieved.

- Coaching cues
  - “Move the head to move the hands.”
  - “Close elbows to close doors.”
  - “Win inside, then stabilize.”

- Common errors
  - Treating pummelling as arm-waving without head or elbow discipline.
  - Allowing posture to rise and base to narrow (easy to off-balance and throw).

- Links
  - VN06: directly feeds double overhooks/underhooks acquisition.
  - VN05/VN07: directly feeds overhook/underhook throwing platforms.

## TN01.5 The Second Great Training Method: Hip Jousting (50:49+) 
- Goal
  - Develop “hip denial” and hip-entry awareness so you can prevent opponents from inserting hips for throws and create your own throwing windows.

- Key concepts
  - Hip insertion is the engine of many throws. If the opponent can insert hips freely, you will be thrown; if you can consistently deny hip insertion, their throwing success drops sharply.
  - Hip checking functions like an upper-body analog to the sprawl: it denies the opponent’s preferred entry lane rather than retreating and giving space.

- Positional landmarks
  - The decisive variable is “empty space” between hips:
    - empty lane = opponent can enter hips,
    - filled lane = opponent must re-route or change attacks.
  - Head and chest remain connected; posture remains intact while hips manage distance.

- Mechanics & details
  - As opponent attempts hip entry:
    1) rotate/turn hips to make contact and eliminate the insertion lane,
    2) re-square so they cannot turn the corner,
    3) walk across hips to create angle for your own entries.
  - Train hip jousting as a timing drill (recognize entry attempt early), not a strength drill.

- Coaching cues
  - “No empty hip lane.”
  - “Hip check, then angle.”
  - “Deny first; throw second.”

- Common errors
  - Retreating hips straight back and leaving a clean insertion lane.
  - Over-leaning shoulders behind hips so far you become driveable.
  - Treating hip denial as purely defensive rather than as the first beat of a counter-entry.

- Links
  - VN05–VN08: hip entry/denial is a hidden prerequisite for most throws.
  - VN08: conservative footwork and safer hip management for older athletes.
