DENSE EXPLAINER NOTES (How to read and teach Volume 6)
================================================================================

1) Volume 6 is about **locks, not throws**
- Unlike VN05 (overhook/over-under), Volume 6 starts from **both arms controlled**.
- Overhooks lock *their arms* (deny posting).
- Underhooks/body lock control *their torso and hips*.
- Teaching implication:
  - If the lock is loose, *none* of the throws will work reliably.

2) Double Overhooks = anti–double-underhook system
- 6.1 reframes “double overhooks” as functional **if angle and level are denied**.
- Core rule:
  - No angle (don’t let them get behind).
  - No level (don’t let them sink to hips).
- Teaching implication:
  - Teach squaring + hips back before teaching any throws.

3) Trip-first logic on the overhook side
- Overhooks deny posts → trips become very high percentage.
- Progression:
  - 6.2 Ouchi (inside, forward weight)
  - 6.3 Step-around Kosoto (outside, alignment)
  - 6.4 Calf-to-calf Kosoto (escape prevention)
- Teaching implication:
  - Teach inside trip first, outside trip second, calf-lock third.

4) Uki Waza as the “pressure release valve” (6.5)
- When opponent drives hard into the overhooks:
  - You *don’t* resist.
  - You disappear underneath them laterally.
- Teaching implication:
  - Uki Waza is pressure-triggered, not chosen at will.

5) Double Underhooks / Body Lock = strongest control platform
- 6.6 establishes body lock as:
  - best late-match grip,
  - best slippery-condition grip,
  - best platform for turning shoulders behind hips.
- Teaching implication:
  - Spend disproportionate time on body-lock maintenance.

6) Step-around vs calf-to-calf on body lock
- Same logic as overhooks, but higher control:
  - 6.7 Step-around Kosoto = alignment model.
  - 6.8 Calf-to-calf Kosoto = escape prevention.
- Teaching implication:
  - Students should *recognise* when step-around stops working and upgrade.

7) Back Bend Takedown = “low athleticism win” (6.9)
- Minimal hopping.
- Minimal turning.
- Step → arch → knee down.
- Teaching implication:
  - This is the default for older / fatigued / conservative athletes.

8) Ken Ken Uchi Mata = power option, not baseline (6.10)
- Requires hopping and balance.
- Body lock reduces scramble risk.
- Teaching implication:
  - Teach only after students can hold body lock under resistance.

9) High-Lock Series = grip migration, not new throws (6.11)
- High-lock is an *upgrade*:
  - better shoulder control,
  - better steering,
  - easier rotation.
- Feeds into:
  - Uki Goshi (half hip),
  - Tai Otoshi (block),
  - Sasae (timed step).
- Teaching implication:
  - Teach “climb the lock” before teaching these throws.

10) Minimum viable teaching order (Volume 6)
- Phase 1: Lock understanding (6.1 / 6.6)
- Phase 2: Trips (6.2 / 6.7)
- Phase 3: Escape prevention (6.4 / 6.8)
- Phase 4: Conservative finish (6.9)
- Phase 5: Athletic options (6.5 / 6.10)
- Phase 6: High-lock upgrades (6.11)

11) Diagnostic cues (what to say on the mat)
- “Square first.”
- “Hips back before throws.”
- “Make him carry you.”
- “Trip the base, don’t throw the body.”
- “Climb the lock.”

12) Mastery checklist
- Can maintain double overhooks without being turned or lifted.
- Can deny angle and level consistently.
- Can execute inside trip and outside trip from lock.
- Can recognise when to upgrade to calf-to-calf.
- Can choose back bend vs ken-ken based on fatigue and balance.
- Can climb to high-lock without losing control.

## Volume 6 Doctrine Summary (Readable / Explanatory)

- Volume intent  
  Volume 6 is about turning **double-control clinches** into takedowns that are mechanically inevitable. When both of the opponent’s arms (or their torso) are controlled, your job is less “throwing” and more “removing their ability to stay standing.”

- The organizing idea  
  The opponent’s takedown success in double-underhooks comes from two advantages: **angle** (getting behind you) and **level** (getting under your hips/lumbar). If you deny both, the position becomes neutral, and your overhooks become a platform to attack because their arms are trapped and their posts disappear.

- What students usually miss  
  People fail this volume by trying to “do the throw” before the **lock, alignment, and weight-loading** are in place. The throws here are simple on purpose; the real skill is making the opponent unable to resist them.

- Teaching priority  
  Teach **platform first**, then one trip, then one upgrade. Students should learn to diagnose: “Did I lose angle?” “Did I lose level?” “Did I fail to trap posts?” before they change techniques.

# DOUBLE OVERHOOK SYSTEM

---

## 6.1 Double Overhooks — Understanding the Position (0:00–5:07)

### Classification
- Platform / control chapter (foundation)

### Primary Decision Rule
- If you deny **angle** and **level**, the opponent’s double underhooks stop being dominant.

### Goal
- Neutralise the opponent’s double underhooks and convert to a stable “no-post” platform where trips become high-percentage.

### Key concepts (explanatory)
- Double underhooks are only truly dangerous when the opponent either turns the corner behind your hips (angle) or sinks down to your lumbar/hips (level). When you square up and push your hips back, you remove both engines of their lift and rotation.
- Double overhooks are effective because they change the match from “who can lift” to “who can post.” By trapping both of their arms, you remove the opponent’s ability to post hands on the fall, which makes even basic trips disproportionately effective.
- The position is best taught as a fatigue position. Your goal is to make the opponent **carry your weight** while you keep angle and level denied, so their legs fatigue and their stance narrows.

### Positional landmarks
- Hips back; shoulders stacked over hips (no fold).
- Elbows dropped to opponent’s elbows/hips.
- Hands joined in front of opponent’s chest/ribs (after clamp).
- Head neutral or slightly offset to intended throw side.
- Feet active; you are circling to stay square.

### Mechanics & details (stepwise)
1. Square up as soon as they seek to move behind you.
2. Push hips back and slightly down to deny lift.
3. Slide elbows down until they meet opponent’s elbows/hip line.
4. Clamp elbows inward to trap both arms.
5. Join hands only after the clamp is secure.
6. Walk and circle so the opponent carries your weight.
7. When balance degrades, transition immediately into a trip.

### Coaching cues
- “No angle, no level.”
- “Clamp first, lock second.”
- “Make him hold you.”

### Common errors
- Staying tall → opponent lifts.
- Joining hands before clamping elbows → arms slip free.
- Staying planted → opponent gets angle behind you.

### First follow-up
- From stable lock: Ouchi (6.2) when weight is forward; Kosoto (6.3) when they stay upright.

- SYS: Clinch denial principles (angle/level gates)

## 6.2 Ouchi Gari from Double Overhooks (5:07–10:00)

### Classification
- Primary teaching throw (baseline finish)

### Primary Decision Rule
- If arms are trapped and their weight comes forward, inside trip is the safest and most consistent takedown.

### Goal
- Use trapped arms (no posts) to put the opponent straight down and land into immediate top control.

### Key concepts (explanatory)
- In this volume, the overhooks are not “throwing grips.” They are a **post-denial system**. Once the arms are trapped, the legs only need to create a small off-balance for the opponent to fall.
- Ouchi works well here because it collapses the base directly underneath them. You are not trying to sweep a foot fast; you are trying to make the opponent’s stance structurally disappear at the moment their upper body is already controlled.
- Head direction matters because it is the simplest steering wheel. Turning your head and chest tells the opponent where to fall and prevents the common “sideways slump” that creates scrambles.

### Positional landmarks
- Double overhooks locked; elbows tight.
- Hips back until you commit to the step.
- Reaping leg steps between their feet.
- Head and chest turn toward the fall direction.

### Mechanics & details (stepwise)
1. Maintain double overhook lock; keep elbows tight.
2. Load their weight onto the target leg (make them carry you; feel forward pressure).
3. Step reaping leg deep between their feet.
4. Hook behind heel/calf with inside of the foot.
5. Pull upper body forward and slightly across the reaping line.
6. Turn head and chest to the side you want them to land.
7. Follow down chest-to-chest or into dominant half guard top.

### Coaching cues
- “Hips back, then step.”
- “Hook first, head turns.”
- “Straight down.”

### Common errors
- Reaping before weight is loaded → no collapse.
- Leaning backward during finish → you fall instead of them.
- Leaving space in overhooks → they free an arm to post.

### First follow-up
- 3-second pin settle; then begin pressure passing.

### Links
- SYS: Ouchi / inside-trip control logic
- VN05: Inside-trip comparisons from overhook/over-under

## 6.3 Step-Around Kosoto Gake from Double Overhooks (10:00–14:53)

### Classification
- Primary teaching throw (alignment-dependent)

### Primary Decision Rule
- If forward trips stall and the opponent stays upright, take them straight back using step-around alignment.

### Goal
- Use safe step-around footwork to trip the opponent backward under trapped arms, finishing in a pin.

### Key concepts (explanatory)
- Kosoto succeeds here because the opponent cannot post hands as they fall, so the backward topple becomes clean and controlled. The risk is not the opponent’s defense; the risk is **your alignment** if you skip steps.
- The “safety step” is the centerpiece. It aligns both partners’ knees so you can sit back without twisting yourself or them, and it makes the finish feel like sitting into a chair rather than falling sideways.
- Think of this as “alignment creates inevitability.” The more correct your alignment, the less force you need.

### Positional landmarks
- Arms still trapped; elbows tight.
- First step around opponent’s near side.
- Second safety step aligns knees.
- Hips sit back, not sideways.

### Mechanics & details (stepwise)
1. Maintain lock; make opponent carry weight.
2. Step around near side.
3. Take the safety step until knees align (both knees same direction).
4. Sit back like into a chair.
5. Pull opponent’s upper body over the reap as they topple.
6. Follow down with chest connection into pin.

### Coaching cues
- “One, two, sit.”
- “Knees same way.”
- “Sit, don’t fall.”

### Common errors
- Skipping the safety step → twisting and instability.
- Sitting sideways → scramble risk.
- Reaping shallow → they step out.

### First follow-up
- Immediate chest-to-chest pin; block hips from turning.

### Links
- VN05: Step-around kosoto family
- SYS: Alignment / safety-step protocol

## 6.4 Calf-to-Calf Kosoto Gake from Double Overhooks (14:53–20:55)

### Classification
- Situational upgrade (escape-prevention)

### Primary Decision Rule
- If the opponent repeatedly steps out of kosoto, upgrade to calf-to-calf to lock the leg.

### Goal
- Immobilise the opponent’s leg so they cannot step out, then topple them backward under trapped arms.

### Key concepts (explanatory)
- Calf-to-calf is best understood as “removing the escape lever.” If the opponent’s leg is free, they can step away at the last second; if your calf is glued to theirs, the escape disappears.
- The important target is not the heel—it’s the **knee line**. Catching only the heel often lets the opponent retract or pivot out; controlling behind the knee line prevents that.
- This is an “upgrade,” not a separate technique. You use the same kosoto logic, but you increase leg control when the opponent’s defense is stepping.

### Positional landmarks
- Deep step behind opponent’s leg line.
- Calf glued to calf (no daylight).
- Slight knee bend to trap knee line.
- Upper-body lock remains tight throughout.

### Mechanics & details (stepwise)
1. Step deeper behind opponent than normal kosoto.
2. Glue calf-to-calf; flex knee slightly to capture behind knee line.
3. Maintain arm trap (no release during entry).
4. Pull upper body back while sitting into finish.
5. Follow down chest-to-chest; prevent turn-in scramble.

### Coaching cues
- “Glue the calf.”
- “Trap the knee, not the foot.”
- “Don’t open the arms.”

### Common errors
- Catching only the heel → step-out escape.
- Straightening the knee → calf contact slips.
- Loosening overhooks mid-entry → opponent posts.

### First follow-up
- Pin settle; isolate near-side arm immediately (they can’t post, so you can dominate shoulders).

### Links
- VN05: Calf-to-calf kosoto variants
- SYS: Escape-prevention upgrades (leg immobilisation)

## 6.5 Uki Waza — Lateral Drop from Double Overhooks (20:55–24:21)

### Classification
- Situational throw (pressure-triggered release valve)

### Primary Decision Rule
- If the opponent is driving hard into your double overhooks, do not resist straight back—drop laterally and wheel them.

### Goal
- Convert the opponent’s forward pressure into a lateral drop that lands you beside or above them with immediate control.

### Key concepts
- Uki Waza here is not a discretionary throw; it is a reaction to pressure. When the opponent supplies forward force, you remove their base sideways rather than fighting that force.
- The side-fall angle is critical. Falling to your side preserves chest contact and control; falling flat or backward tends to create scrambles.
- Double overhooks reduce posting ability, which allows smaller movements to have large effects.

### Positional landmarks
- Double overhooks tight; elbows clamped.
- Hips close to opponent’s hips.
- Head stable and connected.
- Clear lateral fall angle established.

### Mechanics & details
1. Maintain double overhooks and invite forward pressure.
2. Step in close on the throwing side.
3. Sit/fall to your side, not straight back.
4. Rotate shoulders to drive opponent’s near shoulder to the mat.
5. Maintain at least one overhook through the fall.
6. Land beside them with chest pressure.

### Coaching cues
- “Side, not back.”
- “Let him push.”
- “Stay glued.”

### Common errors
- Falling flat → scramble.
- Dropping too far away → dragging instead of throwing.
- Releasing overhooks mid-fall.

### First follow-up
- Chest connection → pin settle; if they turn to knees, connect front headlock.

### Links
- VN03: Uki Waza mechanics
- SYS: Pressure-conversion throws

## 6.6 Double Underhooks — Understanding the Position (24:21–33:16)

### Classification
- Platform / control chapter (dominant clinch)

### Primary Decision Rule
- If your lock controls the torso and your head stays tight, you can reliably move the opponent’s shoulders behind their hips.

### Goal
- Establish a body-lock platform that works under fatigue, sweat, and resistance.

### Key concepts
- Body lock succeeds because it controls the torso rather than the arms; this makes it reliable late in rounds.
- Off-balancing comes from moving shoulders behind heels, not from lifting.
- Space is the enemy: any gap between head, chest, or hips reduces steering power.

### Positional landmarks
- Hands locked around torso.
- Head tight to chest or shoulder.
- Hips in; spine straight.
- Short, active steps.

### Mechanics & details
1. Secure lock and clamp tight.
2. Curl opponent inward.
3. Walk shoulders behind heels.
4. Maintain head contact throughout.
5. Select finish based on base and resistance.

### Coaching cues
- “Clamp and curl.”
- “Head tight.”
- “Move shoulders, not feet.”

### Common errors
- Standing tall.
- Letting hips drift away.
- Head floating.

### First follow-up
- Trips (6.7/6.8), back bend (6.9), or ken-ken (6.10).

### Links
- VN05: Over/under to lock
- VN08: Conservative body-lock finishes

## 6.7 Step-Around Kosoto from Double Underhooks (33:16–39:33)

### Classification
- Primary teaching throw

### Primary Decision Rule
- If weight is loaded onto one leg under body lock, trip outside.

### Goal
- Take the opponent backward while maintaining full torso control.

### Key concepts
- The lock pins shoulders during the fall, reducing scramble risk.
- Weight-loading is mandatory; reaping an unweighted leg fails.

### Positional landmarks
- Lock intact.
- Head tight.
- Step behind heel line.

### Mechanics & details
1. Clamp lock and load weight.
2. Step behind heel line.
3. Sit and rotate shoulders.
4. Maintain lock through fall.

### Coaching cues
- “Load, then reap.”
- “Stay locked.”

### Common errors
- Reaping too early.
- Opening hands during fall.

### First follow-up
- Pin settle.

### Links
- SYS: Body-lock kosoto
- VN05: Kosoto alignment logic

### Common errors

## 6.9 Back Bend Takedown from Double Underhooks (19:19–22:32)

### Classification
- Primary conservative option

### Primary Decision Rule
- If resistance is high and athletic output is low, step deep and back bend.

### Goal
- Tip opponent backward with minimal rotation.

### Key concepts
- Step depth creates the fall; the arch finishes it.
- Knee-down stabilises the landing.

### Positional landmarks
- Deep step between legs.
- Chest up.
- Knee ready to drop.

### Mechanics & details
1. Clamp lock.
2. Step deep.
3. Arch back while dropping knee.
4. Pull hips past heels.

### Coaching cues
- “Step deep.”
- “Bend, don’t fold.”

### Common errors
- Shallow step.
- Dropping straight down.

### First follow-up
- Maintain lock → settle top.

## 6.10 Ken Ken Uchi Mata from Double Underhooks (22:32–53:08)

### Classification
- Athletic option

### Primary Decision Rule
- If you can hop without losing the lock, ken-ken progressively removes base.

### Goal
- Finish uchi mata while preventing spin-outs.

### Key concepts
- Progressive hops outperform single explosive lifts.
- Lock integrity prevents scrambles.

### Positional landmarks
- Lock tight.
- Lifting leg deep.
- Finish leg between opponent’s legs.

### Mechanics & details
1. Insert lifting leg.
2. Hop and steer.
3. Finish with leg between legs.

### Coaching cues
- “Small hops.”
- “Stay locked.”

### Common errors
- One big lift attempt.
- Loose lock.

### First follow-up
- Top control or front headlock if they turn.

## 6.11 High-Lock Series — Uki Goshi / Tai Otoshi / Sasae (53:08–1:05:44)

### Classification
- Grip upgrade

### Primary Decision Rule
- If low lock stalls rotation, climb the lock.

### Goal
- Increase steering and rotation leverage.

### Key concepts
- High lock controls shoulders.
- Same platform, improved steering.

### Positional landmarks
- Lock climbed to ribs/armpits.
- Elbows tight.
- Head connected.

### Mechanics & details
1. Climb lock.
2. Re-tighten.
3. Apply uki goshi, tai otoshi, or sasae.
4. Maintain control through fall.

### Coaching cues
- “Climb, then throw.”
- “Tighten first.”

### Common errors
- Leaving space while climbing.
- Throwing before re-tightening.

### First follow-up
- Pin settle.
