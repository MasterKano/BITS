- A pin is not “pressure” in the abstract. It is **BODY WEIGHT applied through WEDGES**.
- Therefore escape is not “push.” Escape is:
  (1) **MOVE / REDIRECT WEIGHT** (kuzushi/off-balance) AND
  (2) **WORK INSIDE THE WEDGES** (inside position) until you can exit.

# 2.0 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

## 2.1 System Thesis (What Pins Are)
This system treats a pin as a mechanical reality:

**A pin is body weight applied through wedges.**

“Pressure” matters only insofar as it is the felt effect of:
- **body weight** (mass + alignment + gravity), and
- **wedges** (structural blocks that prevent your hips/shoulders from turning, inserting knees, or reintroducing legs).

Therefore the escape problem is never “push the person off.”
It is: **move/redirect weight** and **work inside wedges** until you can exit.

## 2.2 Escape End-States (The Three Modes)
Every escape resolves into one of three end-states:

1) **Escape to guard** (primary, highest-frequency jiu-jitsu outcome),
2) **Escape by reversal** (bottom → top), or
3) **Escape to neutral / stand-up** (break contact).

This framing prevents indecision: you are always building toward a defined finish.

## 2.3 Minimal vs Positive Escapes (Why “Just Surviving” Isn’t Enough)
The system distinguishes:

- **Minimal escapes**: you reduce suffering and avoid losing, but remain pinned or stalled.
- **Positive escapes**: you exit into positions that immediately support initiative:
  - structured guard (with inside control),
  - stand-up entries (single-leg / base under you),
  - or top control (reversal).

The instructional emphasis is on **positive escapes** because they produce repeatable, compounding advantage rather than temporary relief.

## 2.4 Universal Protocol (The Document’s Operating System)
Across mount, side, knee-on-belly, north-south, back control, and turtle, the same protocol applies:

1) **SURVIVE**  
   Defend immediate submissions and stop positional upgrades (back/mount/high mount, tighter crossface, hook insertion).

2) **DIAGNOSE**  
   Identify: pin type + wedge points + opponent base + head/arm control configuration.  
   (Wrong diagnosis produces the wrong escape.)

3) **FRAME**  
   Build the correct frames for the distance:  
   - extended hand frames when space exists,  
   - forearm/elbow frames when pressure is tight.

4) **MOVE**  
   Use essential movements to change the opponent’s weight and open lanes:
   - asymmetric bridging (kuzushi),
   - shrimping (hip relocation),
   - back heisting (reorientation / stand-up geometry).

5) **INSERT**  
   Work inside, limb-by-limb. Under tight pins, the first insertion is typically **elbow/forearm**.

6) **CONNECT**  
   Stabilize space with the **elbow–knee connection** so you cannot be re-flattened and the knee lane remains open.

7) **LEGS IN**  
   Reintroduce legs to reclaim guard or to build the base required for reversal/stand-up.

8) **EXIT**  
   Finish to guard (default), reversal, or neutral.

This protocol is the controlling structure for the entire report.

## 2.5 The “Engine Room” (Why Escapes Work)
Escapes are powered by a small set of repeatable movement engines:

- **Bridge (often asymmetric)** to displace weight and compromise base,
- **Shrimp / hip escape** to relocate hips away from wedges and create knee-entry lanes,
- **Back heist** to change orientation and create stand-up/single-leg pathways,
with situational supports (shoulder roll, spins) when alignment permits.

These movements are not “techniques”; they are the mechanics that make frames and inside position possible.

## 2.6 Structural Priorities (What You Are Trying to Win)
The system repeatedly prioritizes:

- **Space before force** (frames first; then movement),
- **Inside before exit** (majority-inside control is the real milestone),
- **Elbow position battles** (especially on transitions and back-control/turtle),
- **Knee line access** (knee-entry angle and insertion point decide success),
- **Frame adaptation** (opponent transitions demand immediate re-framing).

A recurring success marker is: **once you establish elbow–knee connection, the escape becomes a controlled sequence rather than a scramble.**

## 2.7 How the 8 Volumes Fit Together (Roadmap)
- **Vol. 1**: principles, modes of escape, and essential body movements (system foundations).
- **Vol. 2–3**: mount escape families (elbow escape spine; UPA as reversal and/or setup; key variations like ankle-trap entries).
- **Vol. 4–6**: side-pin escape spine (elbow/knee + knee-escape families) and high-percentage alternates; insertion points and knee-entry mechanics.
- **Vol. 7**: adaptations when the opponent changes base (sit-out/reverse sit-out), plus position-specific scenarios (knee-on-belly; north-south by type).
- **Vol. 8**: back control and turtle escape families, emphasizing the positional/submission dual battle and the sliding-elbow series.

## 2.8 Failure Patterns (Most Common Reasons Escapes Fail)
Most failure collapses into a small set of recurring errors:

- **Force without weight movement** (trying to push a stable pin),
- **Framing outside wedge points** (frames get collapsed or bypassed),
- **Stale frames on transitions** (opponent rotates base; defender keeps old frames),
- **Wrong lane / wrong type** (especially in north-south; misidentifying the configuration),
- **Premature side-switching** before securing the critical control point (e.g., losing knee/leg capture in mount escapes),
- **Losing the submission battle while addressing the position** (notably back control).

## 2.9 Summary Statement (One Sentence)
**Protect → Diagnose → Frame → Move → Get Inside → Connect → Legs In → Exit.**

Every technique in Volumes 1–8 is a position-specific implementation of that sequence.

# 4.0 CORE CONCEPTS

## 4.1 Pin Theory: Wedges + Body Weight (The Root Model)
**Pin = body weight applied through wedges.**  
- **Body weight** is the load: mass + alignment + gravity (and how it is distributed through base and connection).
- **Wedges** are the structures that prevent your body from rotating, inserting knees, or reintroducing legs.

**Implication:** Escapes fail when you try to “push weight” while the wedge structure remains intact.  
**Winning condition:** you either (a) remove/neutralize key wedges, (b) shift the load so the wedge cannot be maintained, or (c) do both in sequence.

## 4.2 The Three Escape Modes (Exit Objectives)
Every escape is a build toward one of three end-states:
1) **Escape to guard** (default; highest percentage in sport jiu-jitsu),
2) **Escape by reversal** (bottom → top), or
3) **Escape to neutral / stand-up** (break contact, create separation).

**Rule:** choose the end-state early; the same “space” can be spent on different exits.

## 4.3 Minimal vs Positive Escapes (Quality Standard)
- **Minimal escape:** reduces suffering but does not reliably improve position.
- **Positive escape:** exits into a position that immediately supports initiative:
  - structured guard,  
  - stand-up entries (single-leg/base),  
  - or top control (reversal).

**Rule:** prioritize positive escapes unless the situation requires a temporary minimal escape to prevent loss.

## 4.4 Universal Protocol (Order of Operations)
Across all pins, performance depends on sequencing:
1) **SURVIVE:** defend submissions and prevent upgrades.
2) **DIAGNOSE:** identify pin type + wedge points + base + head/arm control.
3) **FRAME:** build load-bearing structures to stop chest-to-chest collapse.
4) **MOVE:** bridge/shrimp/heist to shift weight and open a lane.
5) **INSERT:** work inside limb-by-limb.
6) **CONNECT:** stabilize space (elbow–knee connection).
7) **LEGS IN:** reintroduce legs to re-guard / build base.
8) **EXIT:** guard / reversal / neutral.

**Rule:** if you are stuck, you are usually skipping a step or doing steps out of order.

## 4.5 Frame Theory (Space as a Managed Resource)

### 4.5.1 What a Frame Is (Functional Definition)
A **frame** is a load-bearing structure (typically skeletal alignment) that:
- supports opponent weight without collapse,
- preserves distance, and
- creates/maintains an **escape lane**.

A frame is not “pushing.” It is **structural resistance** that makes movement possible.

### 4.5.2 Distance-Based Frame Selection
- **Far distance / early contact:** hand/extended frames can work.
- **Near distance / point-blank pin:** **forearm/elbow frames** are superior (short lever, strong alignment).

**Rule:** as pressure increases, frames shorten (hands → forearms → elbows).

### 4.5.3 Frame Lifecycle (Build → Maintain → Upgrade → Re-frame)
- **Build:** stop chest-to-chest / stop head control from flattening you.
- **Maintain:** keep the lane open while you move hips/shoulders.
- **Upgrade:** convert space into insertion (knee line, elbow position, hip line).
- **Re-frame:** when opponent changes base/angle, your old frame becomes stale.

**Failure mode:** **stale frames**—opponent rotates base; defender keeps the same frame; frame becomes bypassed or collapsed.

### 4.5.4 The Three Core Frames (Bottom Side Control Reference)
While details vary, the recurring structural needs are:
1) **Head/Crossface management frame** (deny head control that flattens you),
2) **Hip-line frame** (block hip wedge / prevent mount progression),
3) **Inside-position frame** (create a lane for elbow/knee insertion).

## 4.6 Inside Position Doctrine (The Real Milestone)
**Inside position** means your limbs occupy the space between the opponent’s controlling structures and your torso/hips.

- “Outside” limbs get pinned, collapsed, or isolated.
- “Inside” limbs create wedges of your own and open the path for legs to return.

**Majority inside** is the practical threshold: once you have it, the escape becomes procedural.

**Rule:** the first insertion under tight pressure is typically **elbow/forearm inside**—hands often lose the battle at zero distance.

## 4.7 Elbow Position Battles (A System-Wide Theme)
Across transitions (side base changes, back control, turtle), the recurring fight is:
- **keep elbows from being separated from the body**, and
- **win the inside line with the elbow/forearm** (rather than reaching with hands).

**Rule:** if the opponent wins elbow separation, they can (a) tighten wedges, (b) isolate limbs, (c) access submissions, and (d) upgrade positions.

## 4.8 Elbow–Knee Connection (Space Stabilizer)
The **elbow–knee connection** is a structural lock that:
- preserves distance,
- prevents re-flattening,
- protects the hip line, and
- supports knee insertion and guard recovery.

**Rule:** treat elbow–knee connection as a checkpoint:  
- before it = scrambling for space,  
- after it = controlled escape sequence.

## 4.9 Knee Line Access (Entry Angle + Insertion Points)
“Knee insertion” is not binary; it is governed by:
- **insertion point** (where the knee tries to enter relative to wedges), and
- **entry angle** (alignment of knee/hip relative to opponent’s hip wedge and base).

**Rule:** if the knee keeps getting blocked, fix angle and insertion point before adding effort.

## 4.10 Kuzushi (Moving the Weight) as a Necessary Partner to Framing
Off-balancing is the act of making the opponent’s weight **structurally inconvenient**:
- base is compromised,
- posts are forced,
- alignment is disrupted,
- wedge maintenance becomes expensive.

**Rule:** when frames “hold but nothing changes,” you need kuzushi; when kuzushi “moves but collapses,” you need better frames.

## 4.11 Essential Movement Engines (What You Reuse Everywhere)
- **Bridge (asymmetric):** displace weight; compromise base; create rotation.
- **Shrimp (power/sliding/reverse):** relocate hips; create knee lanes; restore distance.
- **Back heist:** elevate hips; change orientation; access stand-up/single-leg lanes.
- **Shoulder roll / spin (situational):** used when alignment + space provide a safe lane.

**Rule:** techniques are applications; these are the engines.

## 4.12 Transitions: The “Change = Re-frame” Rule
Most pins are not static—top player transitions base, angle, and wedge points.

**Primary rule:** any meaningful change by the top player triggers:
- immediate re-framing,
- immediate re-diagnosis of wedge points,
- and re-entry into the universal protocol.

**Failure mode:** continuing an escape that matched the old pin against the new pin.

## 4.13 Common Failure Patterns (Concept-Level)
- **Force without structure:** pushing without frames.
- **Structure without movement:** framing but never moving weight/hips.
- **Outside fighting:** trying to win from outside the wedges.
- **Wrong type solution:** misidentifying the pin configuration (especially north-south).
- **Premature transitions:** switching sides or chasing legs before critical inside control is secured.
- **Ignoring the submission battle:** attempting positional escape while losing elbow/neck control.

## 4.14 Performance Standards (How to Judge Quality)
A “good” escape attempt shows:
- early choice of exit mode,
- frames that match distance,
- movement that shifts weight,
- progressive inside insertion (not reaching),
- elbow–knee connection achieved,
- legs reintroduced without conceding upgrades,
- and an exit into a positive position.

# 5.0 SYSTEM OVERVIEW

## 5.1 How to Use This System (Operator Method)
1) **Identify the pin family** (mount / side pin / knee-on-belly / north-south / back / turtle).
2) Run the **universal protocol**: Survive → Diagnose → Frame → Move → Insert → Connect → Legs In → Exit.
3) Execute the **primary escape spine** for that pin family.
4) If blocked, switch to a **branch** that matches the new configuration (not your preference).
5) Confirm you hit the **checkpoints** (inside position, elbow–knee connection, knee-line access, legs reintroduced) before attempting the finish.

**Operational rule:** if you stall, your error is usually (a) wrong diagnosis, (b) stale frames after a transition, or (c) trying to exit before majority inside is achieved.

---

## 5.2 Mount Family (Mount Escapes)

### 5.2.1 Problem Model
Mount pins by aligning body weight over your hips and limiting your ability to rotate your hips and knees inside. The top player’s success is amplified by head/arm control and climbing to higher mounts.

### 5.2.2 Priorities
- Protect neck/arms; deny high mount upgrades.
- Build short, strong frames (elbow/forearm) to stop chest-to-chest collapse.
- Create knee-entry lanes (knee line access) through hip movement.

### 5.2.3 Primary Escape Spine
**Elbow escape to guard** is the default mount solution:
- Frame to prevent mount climb,
- create space with movement,
- insert knee line,
- stabilize with elbow–knee connection,
- reintroduce legs to recover guard.

### 5.2.4 Common Branches
- **UPA (bridge-and-roll) reversal** when trapping and kuzushi are available (or as a setup to force posts).
- **Ankle-trap entries** when the mount is in motion (knee drive / step-over / transitional mount).

### 5.2.5 Success Checkpoints
- You deny high mount and prevent back exposure during turns.
- You secure the critical control point (knee/leg capture) before switching sides.
- You achieve elbow–knee connection prior to attempting the final guard recovery.

---

## 5.3 Side Pin Family (Bottom Side Control / Side Pins)

### 5.3.1 Problem Model
Side pins typically succeed through a combination of:
- head control (crossface axis) that flattens you,
- hip wedge and base that block hip rotation,
- chest pressure that collapses frames and prevents knee entry.

### 5.3.2 Priorities
- Win the head/crossface battle early (stop flattening).
- Maintain hip-line defense (prevent mount transition and hip domination).
- Create the lane for knee insertion (knee-line access).

### 5.3.3 Primary Escape Spine
The side-pin “spine” is **elbow/knee escape → knee-escape progression → guard**:
- Establish correct frames,
- move hips away from wedges (shrimp/reverse shrimp),
- insert the knee line,
- connect elbow to knee,
- recover guard (often half/butterfly/full depending on reaction).

### 5.3.4 Common Branches (High-Percentage Options)
- **Single-leg pathway finishes**: convert the knee-escape to stand-up/single-leg mechanics (cutback, back takes, half/butterfly/full guard endpoints).
- **High-leg escape** when crossface is beaten and the high-leg lane is accessible.
- **Ankle-trap reversal** when ankle access is granted and the opponent’s base can be tilted.

### 5.3.5 Success Checkpoints
- Frames match distance (short frames under heavy pressure).
- Knee insertion occurs at a correct **insertion point** and **entry angle**.
- Once elbow–knee connection is achieved, the escape becomes controlled rather than scrambly.

---

## 5.4 Side Pin Base-Change Layer (Sit-Out / Reverse Sit-Out Variants)

### 5.4.1 Problem Model
When the top player changes base (sit-out / reverse sit-out), the wedge map changes:
- new angles attack your elbow position,
- old frames often become stale,
- pin tightness is rebuilt through rotation rather than static pressure.

### 5.4.2 Priorities
- Treat **elbow position** as the primary battle.
- Re-frame immediately when the base changes.
- Use reverse shrimping to recreate distance when the top follows your movement.

### 5.4.3 Escape Spine
- Maintain or recover inside elbow position,
- rebuild frames for the new angle,
- re-enter the standard knee-line insertion pathway,
- exit to guard (or stand-up if the base change exposes legs).

### 5.4.4 Success Checkpoints
- You never accept elbow separation as “temporary.”
- You detect base change early and re-frame immediately.

---

## 5.5 Knee-on-Belly Family

### 5.5.1 Problem Model
Knee-on-belly compresses and controls through a mobile posting structure: pressure is sharp, and transitions to mount/side are common. The top player is typically light enough to move quickly but heavy enough to deny hip mobility.

### 5.5.2 Priorities
- Protect against mount transition and crossface re-entry.
- Frame to control distance and prevent chest-to-chest reconnection.
- Create hip movement lanes and reclaim knee line.

### 5.5.3 Escape Spine
- Frame and angle hips away from the knee line,
- insert your knee and recover guard,
- or stand up if the top player’s base becomes elongated.

### 5.5.4 Common Branch
- **Ankle-trap option** when ankle access is presented.

### 5.5.5 Success Checkpoints
- You exit without conceding mount.
- You convert space into a knee-line insertion quickly (do not “rest in pain”).

---

## 5.6 North-South Family (Type-Specific)

### 5.6.1 Problem Model
North-south pins differ meaningfully by arm configuration and wedge map. Treating them as one position causes wrong solutions.

### 5.6.2 Priorities
- Correctly identify the **north-south type** (configuration ID first).
- Recognize transition moments as primary escape windows.
- Maintain frames that protect the neck/shoulder line while you turn.

### 5.6.3 Escape Spine
- If in transition: escape during the movement (transition escape).
- If stabilized: apply the correct solution for the identified type (double-over / double-under / over-under).

### 5.6.4 Success Checkpoints
- You do not apply a “generic” north-south escape.
- You preserve inside frames and do not concede arm isolation.

---

## 5.7 Back Control Family (Rear Mount)

### 5.7.1 Problem Model
Back control is a dual battlefield:
- **Positional**: hooks + chest-to-back prevent turning and exiting.
- **Submissions**: seatbelt and strangle threats attack the neck while you attempt to move.

### 5.7.2 Priorities
- Win the neck battle first by controlling elbow/wrist positions on the strangle side.
- Choose fall direction and isolate the bottom hook.
- Establish head position to enable the turn.

### 5.7.3 Escape Spine
**Sliding-elbow escape** is the central method:
- select fall direction (brace leg advantage),
- remove/defeat the bottom hook,
- get head to the floor,
- slide elbow to the mat between bodies,
- turn to face and recover guard/top as timing allows.

### 5.7.4 Success Checkpoints
- You do not chase hooks while losing the submission battle.
- Your elbow reaches the mat between bodies (the critical demarcation).
- You turn to face without conceding a remounted back control.

---

## 5.8 Turtle Family

### 5.8.1 Problem Model
Turtle is not “safe” by default; it is a transitional shell. The primary risk is conceding back control (hooks/seatbelt) while waiting.

### 5.8.2 Priorities
- Prevent seatbelt dominance and hook insertion.
- Maintain elbow position and deny access to your neck and hips.
- Re-face or re-guard using the safest available lane.

### 5.8.3 Escape Spine
- Stabilize against back takes,
- use re-facing mechanics and/or roll-based lanes (when safe),
- return to guard or a neutral facing position.

### 5.8.4 Success Checkpoints
- You re-face without granting hooks.
- You treat turtle as an active escape phase, not a resting position.


# 6.0 TECHNIQUE LIBRARY

## 6.1 Universal Tools (Movement Engines + Structural Targets)

### 6.1.1 Bridge (Asymmetric) — “Move Weight / Break Base”
**Purpose:** Displace opponent’s center of gravity; force posts; create rotation/lanes.  
**When:** Opponent’s weight is centered and stable; you need kuzushi before insertion.  
**Core Steps:**
1) Plant feet close enough to load hips (do not bridge from long legs).
2) Choose a side: load one shoulder/hip (asymmetric), not a straight-up bridge.
3) Bridge “through” the opponent, not straight up; aim to tilt their base.
4) Convert the tilt into: (a) trap/roll, (b) space for frames, or (c) knee insertion.
**Key Details:** Head/look direction reinforces the turn; bridge angle beats posts.  
**Counters & Answers:** If they post wide, change angle; if they float, transition to shrimp/knee entry.  
**Failure Modes:** Bridging straight up; bridging with no trap/control; bridging from extended legs.  
**Finishes/Exits:** Reversal, or forced post that enables knee/elbow insertion.  
**Drill:** 10 reps each side: asymmetric bridge → reset → bridge with angle change.

### 6.1.2 Shrimp (Power / Sliding / Reverse) — “Relocate Hips / Create Knee Lanes”
**Purpose:** Move hips away from wedges; create space to insert knee line.  
**When:** You have any frame structure and a lane exists (or can be created).  
**Core Steps (Power):** Frame → turn to side → hips travel away → insert knee line.  
**Core Steps (Sliding):** Tight frames → micro-hip travel → keep connection → knee slides in.  
**Core Steps (Reverse):** Opponent follows your shrimp → reverse shrimp to recreate distance → re-frame.  
**Key Details:** Shrimp is hip travel, not shoulder scoot; timing with frame pressure is decisive.  
**Failure Modes:** Shrimping flat; shrimping without frames; inserting knee with wrong angle.  
**Finishes:** Elbow–knee connection → guard.  
**Drill:** Frame-to-shrimp ladder: 3 power, 3 sliding, 3 reverse each side.

### 6.1.3 Back Heist — “Reorientation / Stand-Up Geometry”
**Purpose:** Get hips under you; create stand-up / single-leg pathways from bottom.  
**When:** You’ve made space and can get an elbow/knee post to elevate hips.  
**Core Steps:** Frame → post → elevate hips → turn to face/angle → build base.  
**Key Details:** Hips must rise; head stays protected; elbows stay tight.  
**Failure Modes:** Trying to heist while flattened; exposing back/neck.  
**Finishes:** Single-leg, stand-up, or guard entry.  
**Drill:** Side-pin scenario: frame → shrimp → heist to base (no takedown finish).

### 6.1.4 Elbow–Knee Connection (Structural Checkpoint)
**Purpose:** Lock distance; prevent re-flattening; stabilize knee insertion into guard.  
**When:** After first meaningful knee-line insertion.  
**Core Steps:** Elbow line stays inside → knee meets elbow → compress space out.  
**Failure Modes:** Reaching hands; leaving elbow floating; knee outside hip wedge.  
**Finishes:** Half/butterfly/full guard pathways.

## 6.2 Mount Escapes (Volumes 2–3 spine)

### 6.2.1 UPA / Bridge-and-Roll (Mounted) — Primary Reversal
**Source:** Vol 2 — “Bridging Escape From Mounted Position (UPA)” (0:00–30:01)  
**Purpose:** Reverse mount when you can trap a side and remove posts.  
**When (cues):** Opponent’s arm/leg can be trapped; their base is committed; you can bridge off-line.  
**Core Steps:**
1) **Survive:** Protect neck/arms; keep elbows tight.
2) **Trap:** Trap same-side arm (deny post) + trap same-side leg/foot (stop stepping out).
3) **Angle:** Turn slightly to load one shoulder; do not bridge straight up.
4) **Bridge + Roll:** Drive hips through; look/turn to direct the roll.
5) **Stabilize Top:** Clear entanglements; settle into top position.
**Key Details:** The roll succeeds from angle + post denial; not from “power.”  
**Common Counters & Answers:**
- **Wide post appears:** change bridge angle; re-trap or switch to elbow escape.
- **Grapevines:** clear vines first (free legs) then resume.
**Failure Modes:** No trap; bridging straight up; turning wrong direction; pausing mid-bridge.  
**Finishes/Exits:** Top control; or forced post creating elbow-escape lane.  
**Drill:** 2-minute rounds: partner mounts with progressive resistance; you only seek trap+angle.

### 6.2.2 Elbow Escape (Mount) — Core Guard Recovery
**Source:** Vol 2 — “Elbow Escape From Mounted Position” (30:01–1:21:35)  
**Purpose:** Recover guard from mount via knee-line insertion and elbow–knee connection.  
**When:** Opponent stabilizes mount; reversal is unavailable or lower percentage.  
**Core Steps:**
1) **Survive:** Elbows tight; prevent high mount; protect neck/arms.
2) **Frame:** Forearm/elbow frames inside; stop chest-to-chest flattening.
3) **Move:** Shrimp to create a knee lane (often small, repeated).
4) **Insert Knee:** Bring knee inside at correct insertion point; do not flare.
5) **Connect:** Elbow–knee connection; deny re-flattening.
6) **Recover Guard:** Bring second leg in; choose half/full/butterfly as available.
**Key Details:** Progress is measured by knee-line access, not by “space feeling big.”  
**Common Counters & Answers:**
- **Opponent follows hips:** reverse shrimp; re-frame; reinsert knee.
- **High mount climb:** frame to block climb; re-center hips; resume knee insertion.
**Failure Modes:** Shrimping flat; reaching with hands; knee outside; switching sides early.  
**Finishes:** Half guard → full guard; or open guard entry.  
**Drill:** 3-stage: (1) frame only, (2) frame+shrimp, (3) full escape.

### 6.2.3 Mount Elbow Escape — Ankle-Trap Method (Transitional Mount)
**Source:** Vol 3 — “Ankle Trap Method” (0:00–15:37)  
**Purpose:** Exploit mount transitions/step-overs by trapping ankle to create reliable knee capture and escape.  
**When:** Opponent is moving knees/feet; you can access/secure the ankle.  
**Core Steps:**
1) Identify transitional moment (knee drive / step-over / shifting base).
2) Trap ankle/foot to prevent the leg from freeing.
3) Create space with movement (bridge/shrimp) to bring knee line inside.
4) Secure knee/leg control before changing direction.
5) Finish elbow escape to guard.
**Key Details:** This is timing-driven; the ankle trap is the control point.  
**Counters & Answers:** If they retract foot early, fall back to knee-centered elbow escape.  
**Failure Modes:** Late ankle capture; losing the ankle while switching sides; overcommitting arms.  
**Finishes:** Guard recovery.  
**Drill:** Partner does controlled mount transitions; you hunt ankle capture then reset.

### 6.2.4 Foot-Fighting + Long/Short Position (Mount Escape Meta)
**Source:** Vol 3 — “Foot Fighting…” (22:50–35:20)  
**Purpose:** Manage mount leg configuration to create escape lanes and prevent high mount.  
**When:** Opponent’s feet/legs are actively controlling your hips or blocking shrimp lines.  
**Core Concepts Applied (technique package):**
- Identify whether you are in a “long” vs “short” leg relationship.
- Use foot positioning to: (a) reduce hip wedge, (b) open knee insertion angle, (c) avoid back exposure.  
**Failure Modes:** Ignoring legs while only addressing upper body; turning without leg control.  
**Drill:** 1-minute sets: only foot/hip adjustments (no full escape) → then full elbow escape.

### 6.2.5 Dealing with High Mount + Back-Exposure Risk (Mount Escape Safety)
**Source:** Vol 3 — “Dealing With High Mounts” (35:20–41:21); “Back Exposure…” (41:21–46:54)  
**Purpose:** Keep escapes safe while opponent climbs and threatens back takes.  
**When:** Opponent climbs to high mount or your turning exposes back.  
**Ruleset:**
- Do not turn away without controlling the critical points (knee/leg capture and elbow position).
- Build frames that block climb; re-center hips; choose escape direction that preserves facing.  
**Failure Modes:** Turning too far; chasing leg without elbow control; accepting high mount pressure.

### 6.3.1 Elbow Escapes from Side Position — Introduction + Core Pattern
**Source:** Vol 3 — “Elbow Escapes From Side Position…” (46:54–1:30:59)

# 8.0 APPLICATION SCENARIOS

## 8.1 Universal Scenarios (All Pins)

### 8.1.1 You are flattened (crossface/shoulder pressure wins)
**Cues:** head forced away; shoulders pinned; hips cannot rotate; shrimp feels useless.  
**Objective:** restore the ability to turn (get off flat).  
**Primary response:** re-enter FRAME step: shorten frames (forearm/elbow), rebuild head-control denial first, then turn to side.  
**If blocked:** reverse shrimp to recreate distance; re-frame; retry turn.  
**Fail alarms:** continuing to shrimp while flat; reaching with hands; accepting head control.  
**Preferred exits:** re-established frames → knee line → elbow–knee → guard.

### 8.1.2 Opponent changes base/angle mid-escape (stale frame event)
**Cues:** hip rotation; chest angle changes; your elbow line starts being pulled away; pressure “moves” rather than increases.  
**Objective:** stop stale frames; re-diagnose wedges.  
**Primary response:** stop finishing attempts; re-frame immediately for the new wedge map; re-run diagnose → frame → move.  
**If blocked:** reverse shrimp (distance recovery) then reinsert knee line.  
**Fail alarms:** persisting with the old frame; trying to “power through” the new base.  
**Preferred exits:** guard via knee line; stand-up if legs exposed by base change.

### 8.1.3 You created space but nothing changes (frames hold, no progress)
**Cues:** frames are strong; opponent still stable; no knee lane appears.  
**Objective:** convert static space into movement and insertion.  
**Primary response:** add kuzushi: asymmetric bridge or hip bump to force posts; then insert inside.  
**If blocked:** change angle (not intensity) and repeat: frame → move weight → insert.  
**Fail alarms:** resting in discomfort; staying square; extending arms.  
**Preferred exits:** insertion → elbow–knee → legs in.

### 8.1.4 You are reaching for the finish and keep losing position
**Cues:** knee almost in, but you get re-flattened; elbow floats away; opponent re-crossfaces.  
**Objective:** lock the checkpoint before finishing.  
**Primary response:** prioritize elbow–knee connection (connect) before bringing second leg or turning further.  
**If blocked:** re-win inside elbow; adjust knee insertion point/angle; reconnect.  
**Fail alarms:** finishing before majority inside; switching sides too early.  
**Preferred exits:** structured half/butterfly/full guard.

## 8.2 Mount Scenarios

### 8.2.1 Opponent climbs to high mount as you start escaping
**Cues:** hips slide up; knees pinch higher; your elbows feel separated; head pressure increases.  
**Objective:** stop climb; recover working space.  
**Primary response:** frame to block climb and re-center hips; reinsert short frames; resume elbow escape spine.  
**If blocked:** use asymmetric bridge to disrupt base and force hands to post; then re-frame.  
**Fail alarms:** continuing to shrimp while opponent climbs; turning away and giving back exposure.  
**Preferred exits:** elbow escape to guard; UPA if trap becomes available.

### 8.2.2 Opponent posts wide to kill UPA
**Cues:** trapped-side hand posts; base becomes wide; roll direction feels “dead.”  
**Objective:** convert failed reversal attempt into guard recovery.  
**Primary response:** abandon roll; use the space created by their post to start elbow escape (knee line).  
**If blocked:** change bridge angle to force post again; then insert knee line.  
**Fail alarms:** repeated straight-up bridges; trying to roll a posted base.  
**Preferred exits:** elbow escape; secondary reversal only if post is removed.

### 8.2.3 Opponent grapevines your legs
**Cues:** their feet hook/extend your legs; hips feel stapled; shrimp path is muted.  
**Objective:** free legs to restore hip mobility.  
**Primary response:** clear/peel grapevines using leg mechanics before committing to elbow escape or UPA.  
**If blocked:** bridge to lighten their leg pressure, then clear again.  
**Fail alarms:** attempting full elbow escape while legs are immobilized.  
**Preferred exits:** elbow escape to guard; UPA if traps align after clearing.

### 8.2.4 Opponent is transitioning mount (knee drive / step-over)
**Cues:** feet/knees moving; you feel momentary lightness; ankle becomes accessible.  
**Objective:** exploit transition window.  
**Primary response:** ankle-trap method → secure control point → knee capture → elbow escape.  
**If blocked:** fall back to knee-centered elbow escape (stable mount plan).  
**Fail alarms:** missing the timing window; chasing ankle late.  
**Preferred exits:** guard recovery with knee control secured.

## 8.3 Side Pin / Side Control Scenarios

### 8.3.1 Crossface is crushing; you can’t turn
**Cues:** head forced away; shoulder pressure pins; your near-side arm is isolated.  
**Objective:** re-win head-control denial and shoulder freedom.  
**Primary response:** shorten frames; prioritize crossface denial first; turn to side before shrimping.  
**If blocked:** reverse shrimp to recreate space, then rebuild frames and turn.  
**Fail alarms:** shrimping flat; pushing with hands; letting elbow drift away from body.  
**Preferred exits:** elbow escape spine → knee line → guard.

### 8.3.2 You get the knee in but it keeps getting stapled back out
**Cues:** knee enters briefly; opponent re-staples; you repeat without progress.  
**Objective:** fix seam selection: insertion point + angle.  
**Primary response:** stop grinding; adjust knee entry angle and insertion point; reconnect elbow–knee quickly.  
**If blocked:** reverse shrimp to reset distance; reattempt with corrected angle.  
**Fail alarms:** repeating the same failed insertion.  
**Preferred exits:** half guard or butterfly (first stable endpoint) → then upgrade.

### 8.3.3 Opponent follows your hip escape perfectly
**Cues:** every shrimp is matched; distance never grows; frames start collapsing.  
**Objective:** create net distance via timing change.  
**Primary response:** reverse shrimp after they follow; re-frame; then power shrimp on the next beat.  
**If blocked:** add kuzushi (asymmetric bridge) to force a post, then insert knee line.  
**Fail alarms:** linear shrimping without timing change.  
**Preferred exits:** guard recovery; stand-up if their base elongates.

### 8.3.4 Opponent sits out (base change) during your escape
**Cues:** hip rotation; chest turns; your elbow is attacked/dragged; old frames feel “wrong.”  
**Objective:** immediate re-frame; win elbow position.  
**Primary response:** elbow battle first → re-frame for new angle → resume knee line pathway.  
**If blocked:** reverse shrimp to recreate space; reinsert knee line.  
**Fail alarms:** stale frames; accepting elbow separation.  
**Preferred exits:** guard; stand-up if their legs are exposed by angle.

### 8.3.5 High-leg lane appears (crossface beaten, space high)
**Cues:** head control neutralized; your upper body can turn; lane for high leg exists.  
**Objective:** take the high-percentage alternate rather than forcing the main seam.  
**Primary response:** high-leg escape.  
**If blocked:** return to elbow/knee escape spine with improved head position.  
**Fail alarms:** attempting high-leg while still flattened.  
**Preferred exits:** guard with immediate offensive posture.

### 8.3.6 Ankle becomes accessible from side pin
**Cues:** ankle exposed; base can be tilted; upper-body wedges are manageable.  
**Objective:** convert to reversal if safe; otherwise use it to create insertion space.  
**Primary response:** ankle-trap reversal attempt with tight control.  
**If blocked:** use the reaction to reinsert knee line and recover guard.  
**Fail alarms:** chasing ankle while losing crossface/arm safety.  
**Preferred exits:** reversal to top; fallback guard.

---

## 8.4 Knee-on-Belly Scenarios

### 8.4.1 Opponent threatens to transition to mount as you defend
**Cues:** knee slides; hips square; hands post for movement.  
**Objective:** prevent mount and exit quickly.  
**Primary response:** frame + hip angle to create knee line; recover guard immediately.  
**If blocked:** build base to stand when their base elongates.  
**Fail alarms:** staying square under knee pressure; delaying the knee line.  
**Preferred exits:** guard or stand-up.

### 8.4.2 Ankle access is presented on KOB
**Cues:** their ankle/foot is reachable without extending arms.  
**Objective:** exploit ankle-trap option safely.  
**Primary response:** ankle-trap sequence to tilt/reverse.  
**If blocked:** abandon and re-guard (do not force).  
**Fail alarms:** reaching long; exposing arms/neck.  
**Preferred exits:** reversal or guard.

---

## 8.5 North-South Scenarios

### 8.5.1 You are caught in north-south during a transition
**Cues:** opponent is moving; grips not fully set; wedges feel incomplete.  
**Objective:** escape while configuration is weak.  
**Primary response:** transition escape: frame early, turn/reorient, insert knee/hip line, recover guard/face.  
**If blocked:** re-diagnose type; shift to type-specific plan.  
**Fail alarms:** waiting until stabilized.  
**Preferred exits:** guard or facing neutral.

### 8.5.2 You can’t tell which north-south type you’re in
**Cues:** confusion; repeated failed attempts; arms feel trapped unpredictably.  
**Objective:** pause execution; classify configuration.  
**Primary response:** identify: transition vs double-over vs double-under vs over-under; then commit.  
**If blocked:** treat elbow line safety first; rebuild inside frames; reattempt.  
**Fail alarms:** generic escape attempts; arm isolation.  
**Preferred exits:** type-correct escape to guard/face.

---

## 8.6 Back Control Scenarios

### 8.6.1 You start escaping hooks but strangle threat spikes
**Cues:** seatbelt tightens; choking hand advances; neck danger increases.  
**Objective:** re-win the submission battle immediately.  
**Primary response:** stop hook work; address strangle-side elbow/wrist control; then resume sliding-elbow sequence.  
**If blocked:** choose fall direction that reduces choking leverage; reset.  
**Fail alarms:** “hook fixation” while losing neck.  
**Preferred exits:** sliding elbow → face → guard/top.

### 8.6.2 Bottom hook keeps re-inserting as you try to turn
**Cues:** you clear it, it returns; you can’t reach demarcation.  
**Objective:** make hook defeat durable.  
**Primary response:** prioritize bottom hook defeat as the hinge step; only then progress to head-to-floor and elbow demarcation.  
**If blocked:** adjust fall direction and hip angle to improve leverage; reattempt.  
**Fail alarms:** turning without hook control; rushing the face turn.  
**Preferred exits:** elbow demarcation → face.

### 8.6.3 You reach “elbow to mat between bodies” but stall
**Cues:** elbow touches but you don’t complete face turn; opponent clamps.  
**Objective:** convert checkpoint into turn completion.  
**Primary response:** bring head to floor and use it as a post; turn hips and shoulders together to face.  
**If blocked:** re-secure elbow position (don’t lose demarcation) and retry with better base.  
**Fail alarms:** letting elbow float back out; pausing.  
**Preferred exits:** face → guard or scramble top.

---

## 8.7 Turtle Scenarios

### 8.7.1 Opponent is hunting seatbelt immediately
**Cues:** arm threads; chest-to-back connection forms; neck line threatened.  
**Objective:** deny dominance before it locks.  
**Primary response:** elbows tight; block inside line; turn to face when lane appears.  
**If blocked:** build base and reframe; do not accept hooks.  
**Fail alarms:** staying static; letting seatbelt settle.  
**Preferred exits:** re-face to guard/neutral.

### 8.7.2 Opponent begins inserting hooks
**Cues:** leg threading; hip connection deepens; you feel your turn ability shrinking.  
**Objective:** stop back capture now.  
**Primary response:** block hook entry and re-face immediately; prioritize hook denial over rolling.  
**If blocked:** reset elbow line, widen base, then re-face.  
**Fail alarms:** rolling while hooks are present; accepting one hook as “okay.”  
**Preferred exits:** re-face; recover guard.

### 8.7.3 Roll lane appears (no hooks, elbows protected)
**Cues:** you have space; opponent’s control is not locked; lane is clear.  
**Objective:** use roll to re-face/re-guard efficiently.  
**Primary response:** shoulder roll escape family to face/guard.  
**If blocked:** abandon roll and re-face via base building.  
**Fail alarms:** rolling into hooks/seatbelt; exposing neck.  
**Preferred exits:** face → guard.

# 9.0 DRILLING PROGRESSIONS

## 9.1 Principles of Drilling (How to Train This System)
- Drill **checkpoints**, not just finishes: majority inside, elbow–knee connection, knee-line access, elbow position dominance.
- Train **order-of-operations**: Survive → Diagnose → Frame → Move → Insert → Connect → Legs In → Exit.
- Use **constraints** to stop premature finishing and to force correct adaptation (re-frame on transitions).
- Progress through a **resistance ladder** so mechanics survive real pressure.

---

## 9.2 Micro-Drills (Single Skill, High Reps)

### 9.2.1 Frames Under Load (Short-Frame Integrity)
**Goal:** hold structure without pushing.  
**Format:** partner applies progressive chest pressure; defender maintains elbow/forearm frames.  
**Constraints:** no extended arms; elbows must remain connected to body.  
**Metric:** frames hold for 10–20 seconds without collapse or arm extension.

### 9.2.2 Turn-Off-Flat Drill (Crossface Denial → Turn)
**Goal:** restore the ability to turn before shrimping.  
**Format:** start flattened; partner maintains crossface pressure; defender rebuilds head-control denial and turns to side.  
**Constraints:** no shrimp until on side.  
**Metric:** time-to-side decreases; fewer resets due to re-flattening.

### 9.2.3 Shrimp Variants Ladder (Power / Sliding / Reverse)
**Goal:** choose the right shrimp for the situation and timing.  
**Format:** 3 power shrimps → 3 sliding shrimps → partner follows → 3 reverse shrimps.  
**Constraints:** must keep frames; cannot disengage by “running away.”  
**Metric:** hip travel increases without frame collapse.

### 9.2.4 Knee Line Insertion (Point + Angle)
**Goal:** insert knee reliably by seam selection, not force.  
**Format:** partner blocks knee line lightly; defender must insert by changing angle/insertion point.  
**Constraints:** blocked twice = mandatory reset and change of angle/point.  
**Metric:** successful insertion within ≤3 attempts.

### 9.2.5 Elbow–Knee Connection Snap
**Goal:** convert knee insertion into stabilized lane control.  
**Format:** insert knee → immediately connect elbow–knee → hold 3 seconds → reset.  
**Constraints:** if elbow floats, rep doesn’t count.  
**Metric:** 10 clean reps each side.

### 9.2.6 Re-Frame on Transition (Stale Frame Kill-Switch)
**Goal:** automatic re-frame when top changes base/angle.  
**Format:** partner rotates base (sit-out / reverse sit-out / crossface switch) on a cue; defender must re-frame instantly.  
**Constraints:** no escaping; only re-frame correctly.  
**Metric:** reaction time; correct frame selection.

### 9.2.7 Back Control “Demarcation” Reps
**Goal:** achieve elbow-to-mat-between-bodies checkpoint.  
**Format:** start in back control; defender works to demarcation only; reset.  
**Constraints:** if neck battle is lost, rep stops.  
**Metric:** demarcation achieved under increasing resistance.

---

## 9.3 Chain Drills (Two to Four Skills Linked)

### 9.3.1 Mount Chain: Survive → Frame → Shrimp → Knee Line → Elbow–Knee
**Scope:** mount elbow escape spine (without full finish).  
**Format:** timed reps; partner gives realistic pressure but no counters yet.  
**Checkpoint:** elbow–knee connection achieved.

### 9.3.2 Mount Chain: UPA Attempt → Forced Post → Elbow Escape
**Scope:** use UPA as a reaction generator.  
**Format:** defender initiates UPA; partner posts; defender immediately transitions to elbow escape insertion.  
**Checkpoint:** knee line achieved within 5 seconds of post.

### 9.3.3 Side Chain: Frame Set → Turn → Shrimp → Knee Line → Connect → Half Guard
**Scope:** side escape spine to first stable guard endpoint.  
**Format:** partner provides crossface/hip wedge; defender runs sequence to half guard.  
**Checkpoint:** half guard stabilized with elbow–knee connection.

### 9.3.4 Side Chain: Knee Escape → Come Up → Single-Leg Position (No Finish)
**Scope:** convert escape into base-building.  
**Format:** start at knee escape midpoint; defender stands to single-leg shape; reset.  
**Checkpoint:** base established without back exposure.

### 9.3.5 Base-Change Chain: Side Pin → Sit-Out Cue → Re-Frame → Reverse Shrimp → Knee Line
**Scope:** transition-proof the escape.  
**Format:** partner switches to sit-out on cue; defender must re-frame and still reach knee line.  
**Checkpoint:** knee insertion succeeds despite base change.

### 9.3.6 Back Chain: Neck Battle → Bottom Hook Defeat → Head-to-Floor → Demarcation
**Scope:** back control spine (no full face turn initially).  
**Format:** staged reps; partner counters only within stage.  
**Checkpoint:** demarcation achieved without losing neck.

### 9.3.7 Turtle Chain: Hook Denial → Re-Face → Guard Recovery
**Scope:** turtle treated as active transition.  
**Format:** partner hunts seatbelt/hook; defender denies and re-faces to guard.  
**Checkpoint:** re-face achieved with no hooks conceded.

---

## 9.4 Constraint Rounds (Short Sparring With Rules)

### 9.4.1 Mount Constraints
- **Defender wins** by: knee line + elbow–knee connection (not full escape).
- **Top wins** by: high mount achieved or back exposure created.
- **Round:** 2 minutes; switch.

### 9.4.2 Side Pin Constraints
- **Defender wins** by: knee insertion at correct seam + elbow–knee connection.
- **Top wins** by: re-flattening the defender or transitioning to mount.
- Add rule: defender may only shrimp after turning to side.

### 9.4.3 Knee-on-Belly Constraints
- **Defender wins** by: guard recovered or base built to stand.
- **Top wins** by: mount transition.

### 9.4.4 North-South Constraints
- Defender must call the **type** before attempting escape.
- **Win**: face/guard recovered; **Lose**: arm isolation achieved.

### 9.4.5 Back Control Constraints
- **Defender wins** by: elbow demarcation + face turn.
- **Top wins** by: choke finish or re-locking dominant control after demarcation attempt.

### 9.4.6 Turtle Constraints
- **Defender wins** by: re-face to guard without hooks.
- **Top wins** by: one hook + seatbelt dominance established.

---

## 9.5 Resistance Ladder (Progression That Makes It Real)
Use for any technique family:

- **R0: Static** — partner gives shape, no resistance.
- **R1: Structured** — partner gives pressure, no counters.
- **R2: Single Counter** — partner uses one designated counter (e.g., crossface re-entry).
- **R3: Two Counters** — adds base change or transition (sit-out, mount climb).
- **R4: Live** — full resistance within position, with escape goal defined.
- **R5: Chain Entry** — start from worse case (flattened, late, near submission).

**Rule:** do not progress levels until the checkpoint is consistent.

---

## 9.6 Performance Metrics (How You Know It’s Working)
Track these per session:
- **Time-to-side** from flattened in side pin.
- **Knee insertion success rate** (≤3 attempts).
- **Elbow–knee connection rate** after insertion (clean reps).
- **Escape success under R3** (base change included).
- **Back escape demarcation rate** without losing neck battle.
- **Error log** (top 2 failure modes that day: stale frames, wrong seam, hook fixation, etc.)

---

## 9.7 Minimal Weekly Template (Simple Implementation)
- **Day 1:** Frames under load + knee insertion + side spine chain.
- **Day 2:** Mount elbow escape chain + UPA→post→elbow chain.
- **Day 3:** Base-change re-frame chain + KOB constraints + north-south type ID.
- **Day 4:** Back control demarcation + turtle re-face + live constraints.

# 11.0 FAILURE PATTERNS

## 11.1 How to Use This Section (1-minute method)
When an escape fails, identify the failure class:
1) **Safety error** (you lost submission/upgrade battle),
2) **Structure error** (frames collapsed or stale),
3) **Movement error** (wrong engine or timing),
4) **Seam error** (wrong insertion point/angle),
5) **Checkpoint error** (you tried to finish before majority inside).

Then apply the corresponding fix rule in 11.2–11.4 on the next attempt.

---

## 11.2 Universal Failure Patterns (All Pins)

### 11.2.1 Force Without Weight Movement (“Bench-Press Syndrome”)
**Symptoms:** you push and strain; opponent doesn’t move; arms extend; pin stays intact.  
**Root cause:** wedges remain; weight is not structurally inconvenienced.  
**Fix:** add **kuzushi** (asymmetric bridge/hip bump), or change angle; then re-insert inside.  
**Rule:** if you’re pushing, you’re already late.

### 11.2.2 Movement Without Frames (Collapse on Contact)
**Symptoms:** you shrimp/bridge but get re-flattened instantly; no lane persists.  
**Root cause:** no load-bearing structure; movement happens under full compression.  
**Fix:** rebuild **short frames** first (forearm/elbow), then move.  
**Rule:** frames create the lane; movement uses it.

### 11.2.3 Stale Frames on Transitions (You Escaped the Old Pin)
**Symptoms:** opponent changes base/angle; your frames suddenly feel useless; you get re-pinned.  
**Root cause:** frame matched old wedge map; opponent rotated the wedges.  
**Fix:** immediate **re-frame + re-diagnose** (new head/hip/inside responsibilities).  
**Rule:** meaningful top change = automatic re-frame.

### 11.2.4 Staying Flat (No Rotation = No Knee Line)
**Symptoms:** you can’t turn; shrimping feels like sliding in place; knees never enter.  
**Root cause:** crossface/shoulder wedge wins; shoulder line pinned.  
**Fix:** head-control denial first; turn to side before any shrimp.  
**Rule:** no side posture = no escape posture.

### 11.2.5 Outside Fighting (Trying to Win From Outside the Wedges)
**Symptoms:** hands fight; arms get pinned/isolated; you can’t insert elbows/knees inside.  
**Root cause:** you never achieved inside position; wedges stay external to you.  
**Fix:** first insertion is usually **elbow/forearm inside** under tight pressure.  
**Rule:** inside first; legs later.

### 11.2.6 Premature Finish (Skipping the Checkpoint)
**Symptoms:** knee enters briefly; you chase guard closure/turn; you get re-flattened.  
**Root cause:** elbow–knee connection not secured; majority inside not reached.  
**Fix:** connect elbow–knee and stabilize before bringing second leg or switching sides.  
**Rule:** checkpoints before exits.

### 11.2.7 Wrong Seam (Insertion Point / Angle Error)
**Symptoms:** knee gets stapled repeatedly; you repeat the same attempt and stall.  
**Root cause:** knee is driving into a closed wedge seam.  
**Fix:** change **insertion point and knee angle**; use reverse shrimp reset if followed.  
**Rule:** blocked twice = change seam, not effort.

### 11.2.8 Elbow Separation (System-Wide Structural Collapse)
**Symptoms:** elbow pulled away from body; frames weaken; submissions/upgrades appear.  
**Root cause:** opponent wins inside line; you lose structural integrity.  
**Fix:** re-win elbow position before continuing; tighten elbow line to ribs/hip line.  
**Rule:** lose elbows = lose escapes.

### 11.2.9 Submission Neglect (Positional Fixation)
**Symptoms:** you focus on hooks/hips/knees while choke/arm isolation advances.  
**Root cause:** failure to prioritize SURVIVE step.  
**Fix:** pause escape; address the submission control point; then resume protocol.  
**Rule:** escape attempts do not outrank immediate loss.

---

## 11.3 Position-Specific Failure Patterns

## 11.3.1 Mount (UPA + Elbow Escape)

### A) UPA Failure: No Trap / Post Still Alive
**Symptoms:** you bridge and they post; no roll.  
**Fix:** trap arm+leg better OR treat UPA as a post-generator and switch to elbow escape.

### B) UPA Failure: Straight-Up Bridge
**Symptoms:** opponent absorbs and re-centers.  
**Fix:** asymmetric bridge off-line; change angle + head/look direction.

### C) Elbow Escape Failure: High Mount Climb
**Symptoms:** opponent slides up; your elbows separate; knee line disappears.  
**Fix:** frame to block climb; re-center hips; restart knee insertion.

### D) Elbow Escape Failure: Side Switch Before Control Secured
**Symptoms:** you change sides and lose knee/leg capture; opponent re-stabilizes.  
**Fix:** secure the critical control point (knee/leg capture) *then* transition.

### E) Leg Immobilization (Grapevines)
**Symptoms:** hips feel stapled; shrimp ineffective.  
**Fix:** clear grapevines first (leg freedom precedes hip escape).

---

## 11.3.2 Side Pin (Elbow/Knee Spine + Knee Escape)

### A) Crossface Dominance
**Symptoms:** flattened; can’t turn; frames get crushed.  
**Fix:** deny crossface first; shorten frames; turn to side before shrimping.

### B) Hip Wedge Wins / Mount Threat
**Symptoms:** opponent walks hips; your knee line never appears; mount transition.  
**Fix:** restore hip-line frame responsibility before pursuing knee insertion.

### C) Knee Keeps Getting Blocked
**Symptoms:** repeated stapling.  
**Fix:** seam correction: insertion point + angle; reverse shrimp reset if followed.

### D) Coming Up = Back Exposure
**Symptoms:** you build base and opponent takes back/seatbelt.  
**Fix:** keep elbow line tight; maintain inside frames while rising; choose safer angle.

### E) Overusing Alternates
**Symptoms:** chasing high-leg/spin/roll with low success.  
**Fix:** return to primary spine; only take alternates when their cue is clearly present.

---

## 11.3.3 Base Changes (Sit-Out / Reverse Sit-Out)

### A) Stale Frames (Primary Failure)
**Symptoms:** opponent rotates base; your frames are bypassed.  
**Fix:** re-frame immediately; re-diagnose wedge map; win elbow line.

### B) Elbow Drag / Separation
**Symptoms:** elbow pulled away; inside line lost.  
**Fix:** elbow battle first; then resume knee line pathway.

### C) Linear Shrimping Gets Followed Forever
**Symptoms:** no net distance.  
**Fix:** reverse shrimp (timing change) then power shrimp on next beat.

---

## 11.3.4 Knee-on-Belly

### A) Delay Under Pressure (“Resting in Pain”)
**Symptoms:** you hold frames but don’t exit; mount/side transitions occur.  
**Fix:** convert frames into immediate hip angle + knee line or base to stand.

### B) Squared Hips
**Symptoms:** pressure remains centered; no lane opens.  
**Fix:** angle hips; make the knee line a diagonal problem, not straight-on.

---

## 11.3.5 North-South

### A) Wrong Type, Wrong Escape
**Symptoms:** repeated failure; arm isolation increases.  
**Fix:** stop and classify: transition vs double-over vs double-under vs over-under.

### B) Waiting Until Stabilized
**Symptoms:** wedges fully set; escape feels impossible.  
**Fix:** prioritize transition escapes; move while they move.

### C) Elbow Line Loss
**Symptoms:** arms pinned/isolated.  
**Fix:** rebuild inside frames; protect elbows before turning.

---

## 11.3.6 Back Control

### A) Hook Fixation (Neck Battle Lost)
**Symptoms:** you chase hooks; choking hand advances.  
**Fix:** return to strangle-side elbow/wrist control; resume escape only after safety.

### B) Demarcation Skipped
**Symptoms:** you try to face without elbow-to-mat-between-bodies; they re-take back.  
**Fix:** prioritize demarcation as a checkpoint; don’t rush the face turn.

### C) Bottom Hook Re-Inserts Repeatedly
**Symptoms:** you clear it, it returns.  
**Fix:** improve fall direction + hip angle; make hook defeat durable before advancing.

---

## 11.3.7 Turtle

### A) Passive Turtle (Back Capture Happens)
**Symptoms:** hooks/seatbelt lock while you “wait.”  
**Fix:** treat turtle as active: deny hooks, win elbow line, re-face immediately.

### B) Rolling Into Hooks/Seatbelt
**Symptoms:** roll attempts lead to back control.  
**Fix:** roll only when lane is clean (no hooks, elbows protected); otherwise re-face.

---

## 11.4 Training Failure Patterns (Why Drilling Doesn’t Transfer)

### 11.4.1 Drilling Finishes Instead of Checkpoints
**Symptom:** looks good in reps; fails live early.  
**Fix:** drill: knee line, elbow–knee connection, demarcation—then finish.

### 11.4.2 No Transition Training (R0–R1 Only)
**Symptom:** escapes work until opponent changes base.  
**Fix:** train at **R3**: add sit-out, mount climb, re-crossface, re-hook.

### 11.4.3 No Error Logging
**Symptom:** same failures repeat weekly.  
**Fix:** track top 2 failure modes per session and target them next time.

### 11.4.4 Over-Randomization
**Symptom:** “sparring replaces drilling” and learning slows.  
**Fix:** constraint rounds with specific win conditions and limited counters.

---

## 11.5 One-Line Fix Rules (Fast Recall)
- **Flat = re-win head control first.**
- **Frames collapsing = shorten frames and rebuild structure.**
- **Followed shrimp = reverse shrimp reset, then go again.**
- **Knee blocked twice = change point/angle, not effort.**
- **Transition = re-frame now.**
- **No progress = add kuzushi to force posts.**
- **Back control = neck battle first; demarcation before face.**

# 13.0 TERMINOLOGY

## 13.1 Core System Terms

### Pin
A control state where the top player uses **body weight through wedges** to restrict your rotation, insertion, and leg return.

### Body Weight (Load)
The effective downward/forward pressure created by mass, alignment, gravity, and base—how much “load” you must manage.

### Wedge
A structural block (limb, hip, shoulder, head control, hook) that prevents rotation, knee insertion, or leg re-entry.

### Wedge Map
The current set of active wedges (which wedges, where, and how they are reinforced by base and grips).

### Escape (Pin Escape)
A process that converts being pinned into an exit end-state: **guard**, **reversal (top)**, or **neutral/stand-up**.

### Escape Mode
The chosen exit objective: (1) guard, (2) reversal, (3) neutral/stand-up.

### Minimal Escape
An action that reduces suffering or danger but does not reliably improve position or produce a stable exit.

### Positive Escape
An action that exits into a stable position that supports initiative (structured guard, reversal, or stand-up base).

---

## 13.2 Operational Doctrine Terms

### Universal Protocol
The system’s order-of-operations:
**Survive → Diagnose → Frame → Move → Insert → Connect → Legs In → Exit.**

### Survive
Immediate defense against submissions and positional upgrades (mount climb, back take, tighter head control, hook insertion).

### Diagnose
Identify pin family + wedge map + opponent base/grips/head control + where the escape lane can be built.

### Frame
A load-bearing structure (skeletal alignment) that prevents collapse and preserves an escape lane; not “pushing.”

### Frame Lifecycle
**Build → Maintain → Upgrade → Re-frame** (frames must change when the opponent changes base/angle).

### Stale Frame
A frame that matched the previous wedge map but is now bypassed/collapsed because the opponent transitioned.

### Move (Movement Engine)
A fundamental body movement used to displace weight and relocate hips/shoulders: bridge, shrimp, back heist, etc.

### Insert
Progressively placing your limbs **inside** the opponent’s wedges (usually elbow/forearm first under heavy pressure).

### Connect
Stabilizing space so you cannot be re-flattened—most commonly via **elbow–knee connection**.

### Legs In
Reintroducing legs to reclaim guard, build base for stand-up, or set reversal mechanics.

### Exit
The final resolution: guard, reversal/top, or neutral/stand-up.

---

## 13.3 Space and Structure Terms

### Space
Usable distance/angle that enables movement and insertion. “Space” is only meaningful if you can convert it into inside position.

### Lane (Escape Lane)
A path of space that allows insertion or rotation (e.g., knee lane for guard recovery).

### Inside Position
Your limbs occupy the space between the opponent’s controlling structure (wedges) and your torso/hips/neck line.

### Majority Inside
A practical threshold: you have enough inside position that legs can re-enter without immediate re-flattening.

### Elbow Line
The structural integrity of elbows staying tight to body/hip line and winning the inside line (anti-separation).

### Elbow Separation
Opponent pulls your elbow away from your torso/hip line, weakening frames and opening submissions/upgrades.

### Elbow–Knee Connection
A structural distance anchor where the elbow connects to the inserted knee, stabilizing the lane and preventing re-flattening.

### Knee Line
The insertion objective: getting your knee inside the controlling wedges to begin guard recovery or base building.

### Insertion Point
The specific seam/location where a limb enters. Wrong point yields repeated blocking.

### Entry Angle (Knee Entry Angle)
The orientation of your knee/hip relative to the wedge seam; often the decisive variable in successful insertion.

---

## 13.4 Kuzushi and Base Terms

### Kuzushi
Off-balancing: making the opponent’s base and alignment structurally unstable so wedges cannot be maintained cheaply.

### Base
The opponent’s support structure (posts, knees, feet) that maintains alignment and pressure.

### Post
A limb used to stabilize base (hand/foot). Removing or forcing a post is a common escape-enabler.

### Trap
A control that removes a post option (e.g., trapping arm/leg in UPA).

---

## 13.5 Movement Engine Terms

### Bridge (Asymmetric Bridge)
A directional hip drive used to displace weight and compromise base; “asymmetric” implies off-line, not vertical.

### Shrimp (Hip Escape)
Hip relocation away from wedges to open knee lanes. Variants:
- **Power shrimp:** large hip travel.
- **Sliding shrimp:** small, persistent hip travel under pressure.
- **Reverse shrimp:** timing reset when opponent follows your movement.

### Back Heist
A base-building reorientation that elevates hips and converts bottom position into stand-up/single-leg geometry.

### Shoulder Roll
A rotation-based movement used when a safe roll lane exists (alignment + no back capture risk).

### Spin / Inverted Spin
A rotational movement used to reorient when space and alignment make it safe.

---

## 13.6 Position Family Terms

### Mount
Top player straddles hips; wedges restrict hip rotation and knee line entry; upgrades include high mount.

### High Mount
Mount variant where top climbs higher toward chest/shoulders, increasing elbow separation and submission threats.

### Side Pin / Side Control Family
Chest/shoulder pressure + head control + hip wedge; primary defender problem is flattening and knee line denial.

### Knee-on-Belly (KOB)
Mobile pin using knee pressure and posts, often transitional to mount/side.

### North-South
Pin family where the top aligns north-south; must be treated as configuration types (transition, double-over, double-under, over-under).

### Back Control / Rear Mount
Hooks + chest-to-back control with immediate strangle threat (dual battle).

### Hooks
Leg control elements in back control that block turning and control hips.

### Seatbelt
Upper-body control over shoulders/chest used to maintain back control and initiate strangles.

### Turtle
A defensive shell position; unsafe if passive because it invites seatbelt/hook insertion.

---

## 13.7 Outcome Terms

### Guard Recovery
Any exit where legs are reintroduced to form a guard (half, butterfly, full, open variants).

### Reversal
Any exit where you end on top, typically via trapping and weight displacement.

### Neutral / Stand-Up
Any exit where you break contact or build base to stand, returning to a neutral engagement.

---

## 13.8 Common Confusions (Prevent Terminology Drift)

- **Frame vs Push:** frames are skeletal structures; pushing is muscular effort that collapses under load.
- **Space vs Distance:** space is usable distance/angle that converts into insertion; distance without conversion is wasted.
- **Inside Position vs “Hand Fighting”:** inside position is wedge placement, not grip preference.
- **Knee Line vs “Knee Up”:** knee line means knee enters the seam; simply raising a knee is not insertion.
- **Bridge vs Buck:** a bridge displaces weight and base; random bucking is vertical effort with low structure.
- **Turtle vs Safety:** turtle is only safe if hooks/seatbelt are denied and you are actively re-facing/re-guarding.
