## 2.0 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

### 2.1 System Thesis
- The leg game is won by **asymmetry**: you maintain higher access to their legs than they have to yours.
- **Ashi Garami is the engine** (positional control). Grips are the **transmission** (breaking force).
- Your job is to keep cycling: **CONTROL → FOLLOW → BREAK** until the opponent must concede.

### 2.2 North Star Objectives (ordered)
1) **Isolate a leg** while denying their leg access (avoid "pure mutual" unless converting).
2) Establish **knee-line + wedge** control and manage the **second leg** (deny heist/roll escape).
3) Create a **predictable reaction** (toe hold threat, heist threat, pressure line).
4) Route to a **breaking hub** (often Outside Ashi) and finish with clean mechanics.

### 2.3 State Variables (the system runs on these)
- **Knee line**: do you own it, or can they slip/turn?
- **Heel exposure**: is the heel available or hidden?
- **Second-leg control**: can they spin, heist, or convert to mutual?
- **Rotation**: are you turning their knee/hip line or are they turning yours?
- **Separation**: are their knees/feet pinned together or split (your preference depends on hub)?
- **Hip line**: are your hips past their knee line (good) or are you square (risk)?

### 2.4 Position Hierarchy (roles, not labels)
- **Exposure hubs**: positions that safely produce heel/foot access (e.g., Irimi-type control).
- **Breaking hubs**: positions where finishing mechanics are maximally reliable (often Outside-type).
- **Two-leg hubs**: positions that control both legs to prevent escapes and force dilemmas (Cross-type).
- **Dilemma hubs**: positions where toe hold / knee bar / heel hook cross-feed (Top Shiro/Oshiro-type).
- **Symmetry hubs**: mutual/50-50 states; acceptable only if you can **convert** (Far-hip routing).

### 2.5 Transfer Rules (if-then routing)
- If **heel is hard to expose** but you have leg control:
  - Use the exposure hub first, then **transfer to the breaking hub**.
- If you are in **mutual/50-50**:
  - Do not "trade grips"—**convert** (Far-hip) or disengage to re-enter on your terms.
- If they start a **heist / stand / sit-up**:
  - Treat it as a gift: maintain second-leg control and route to a dilemma hub (knee bar / toe hold).
- If they **roll**:
  - You follow with wedges and re-collect; your goal is to land in a breaking hub, not scramble.

### 2.6 Attack Line Selection (what you pick and why)
- **Heel hook line**
  - Primary break line when heel exposure is present and rotation is controlled.
- **Toe hold line**
  - Dilemma lever: it forces hands/feet responses that open heel exposure or knee bar entries.
- **Knee bar line**
  - Requires orientation + knee-line dominance; becomes high percentage when heist energy appears.
- **Achilles/straight ankle line**
  - Stabilizer and finish; also a "heat" tool to keep them defending while you improve position.

### 2.7 Dilemma Engine (how finishes become inevitable)
- Present **one threat** that compels a specific defense, then punish the defense by switching lines:
  - Toe hold threat → heel exposure
  - Heist threat → knee bar line / re-entry to heel
  - Achilles pressure → positional concessions → stronger hub

### 2.8 Failure Patterns (what breaks the system)
- Chasing grips without owning the engine (Ashi) → false positives.
- Accepting symmetry without a conversion plan → mutual becomes coin-flip.
- Losing second-leg control → opponent rolls/heists into safety or counter-attacks.
- Finishing while knee line is not secured → they slip and you lose the leg.

### 2.9 Teaching Order (recommended)
1) Asymmetry + engine/transmission concept (why the system works)
2) Exposure hub mechanics (safe heel/foot access)
3) Breaking hub mechanics (reliable finishing)
4) Dilemma hub transitions (toe hold ↔ heel ↔ knee bar)
5) Symmetry conversion (mutual/50-50 → far-hip → breaking hub)
6) Live constraints: reaction-following and finishing under movement

### 2.10 Coaching Cues (short, repeatable)
- "Own the knee line first."
- "Second leg decides the scramble."
- "Make them move, then follow."
- "Convert symmetry—don’t negotiate it."
- "Ashi is the engine; grips are just the lever."

## 4.0 CORE CONCEPTS

### 4.1 Asymmetry
Asymmetry means you have meaningful access to their legs while their access to yours is limited.
Your leg attacks become inevitable when you keep asymmetric frames, angles, and wedges.

Key indicators:
- Your hips are outside their knee line while their hips are not outside yours.
- Their knee line is trapped while your knee line is free.
- You can attack without exposing your back or giving them a clean heist.

Practicals:
- Prefer positions where their secondary leg cannot freely rotate, post, or step.
- If symmetry occurs, either convert (preferred) or disengage and re-enter.

---

### 4.2 Ashi Garami Is the Engine; Grips Are the Transmission
The Ashi determines whether your breaking mechanics can be applied safely and repeatedly.
The grip only matters once the engine is stable.

Diagnostic:
- If you lose the leg when they move, your engine is weak (even if your hands are strong).
- If you can hold the leg through their movement, finishes become a choice, not a race.

Coaching cue:
- “Win the position before you win the grip.”

---

### 4.3 Control → Follow → Break
This is the system loop. You do not “hunt finishes”; you build a control state, follow their escape,
and break when the escape creates the necessary alignment.

Control:
- Knee line ownership
- Hip line advantage
- Secondary leg management
- Wedge/inside position

Follow:
- Roll-following with wedges and re-collection
- Heist-denial with second-leg control
- Transfers to better hubs rather than stubborn finishing

Break:
- Select the break line that matches the alignment you already have
- Finish when the opponent’s movement is contained

---

### 4.4 State Variables (what you constantly read)
- Knee line: captured / threatened / free
- Heel: exposed / hidden / slipping
- Rotation: you are rotating them / they are rotating you
- Secondary leg: controlled / posting / stepping / pummeling
- Hip line: you are past their knee line / square / behind
- Separation: knees together / knees split / feet connected in symmetry
- Posture direction: they pressure forward / lean back / roll / heist

Rule:
- When something goes wrong, name the variable that changed and correct it before re-attacking.

---

### 4.5 Inside Position (in the leg game)
Inside position is not a slogan; it’s geometry:
- Your knees/feet act as inside wedges that block their turning and reclaiming angles.
- Your lines occupy the space their hips and knees want to rotate through.

Consequences:
- Inside knee position usually wins long exchanges.
- Losing inside position usually forces you into speed-based finishing (low percentage).

---

### 4.6 Second Leg Decides the Scramble
The secondary leg is the opponent’s steering wheel.
If it is free, they can roll, heist, step, pummel, and reset.

Control methods (conceptual):
- Clamp/wedge it to prevent stepping and hip-turning.
- Redirect it to remove posting power.
- Occupy it so they can’t “stand into” your entanglement.

---

### 4.7 Hub Theory (positions have jobs)
Treat positions by function:

Exposure hubs:
- Safest places to create heel/foot access.

Breaking hubs:
- Highest mechanical advantage for finishing.

Two-leg hubs:
- Prevent movement by controlling both legs; force dilemmas.

Dilemma hubs:
- Threat A forces defense B, which opens finish C.

Symmetry hubs:
- Acceptable only with a conversion plan.

Rule:
- Don’t finish in the wrong hub. Transfer to the hub that matches the job.

---

### 4.8 Symmetry Discipline (50/50 and mutual)
Symmetry is not “bad,” but it is unstable and often coin-flip unless you force defense.

Ruleset:
- Attack until they must defend (don’t negotiate mirrored grips).
- Use threats that force structural responses (separation, inside-knee, heel exposure).
- Convert to an asymmetric hub as soon as conversion is available.

---

### 4.9 Dilemma Engineering (how you make people predictable)
A good dilemma:
- Threat is real (they must respond now).
- Defense requires a structural change (hand placement, posture shift, leg movement).
- That structural change exposes your next finish or transfer.

Common dilemma families:
- Toe hold threat → heel exposure / separation
- Achilles pressure → positional concession → better hub
- Heist threat (upper-body posture) → knee bar alignment / return to heel

---

### 4.10 Finish Line Selection (match break to alignment)
Heel hook:
- Needs heel exposure + controlled rotation.

Toe hold:
- Strong when you can isolate foot rotation; also excellent as a forcing tool.

Knee bar:
- Needs knee-line dominance and correct orientation; becomes high percentage off heist energy.

Achilles / straight ankle:
- Stabilizer and finish; excellent for “heat” while you improve position.

Rule:
- Choose the line you are already aligned for, not the one you want emotionally.

---

### 4.11 Follow Mechanics (don’t lose the leg during movement)
Follow means:
- Your wedges move with their hips.
- Your knees track their knee line.
- Your control survives their attempt to roll or stand.

Common errors:
- Staying fixed while they rotate (you lose the engine).
- Chasing the foot while they reclaim knee line (you lose the position).

---

### 4.12 Safety Constraints (what you never trade away)
- Do not expose your back to hold a leg you can’t finish.
- Do not stand/extend without controlling the secondary leg.
- Do not accept mutual symmetry without a conversion plan.
- Do not break your own alignment (hips/knees) to “force” a grip.

### 4.13 Alignment Beats Force
Breaking mechanics are alignment problems first, strength problems last.

Alignment checks:
- Your hips are connected to the opponent’s hip line (no “air gap”).
- Your knees create a wedge that prevents them from turning into safety.
- Your hands are placed to transmit force through their joints, not across skin.

Practical rule:
- If you feel like you need to “pull harder,” fix the alignment variable you’re missing.

---

### 4.14 The Knee Line Is the Primary Constraint
The opponent escapes leg locks by freeing the knee line and reorienting the hip.
Most success/failure is decided before the foot/heel is even addressed.

Knee-line cues:
- If their knee can travel past your hips, you are losing the engine.
- If their knee stays trapped, all finishes stay available.

Practical rule:
- Re-secure knee line before changing grips.

---

### 4.15 Rotation Control (Who Is Turning Whom?)
Heel hooks and many transitions are governed by rotational dominance.

Rotational dominance indicators:
- Their hip line is pinned while their knee line is steered.
- Their turning direction is blocked by your wedge/inside position.

Practical rule:
- If they can freely rotate, you must upgrade the wedge or transfer hubs before finishing.

---

### 4.16 Exposure ≠ Finish
Exposure is creating access (heel/foot); finishing is maintaining access while removing escape options.

Exposure problems:
- Heel appears then disappears (slip).
- Foot is accessible but knee line is free (false access).

Practical rule:
- Treat exposure as a checkpoint: “Can I keep it during movement?” If no, upgrade the engine.

---

### 4.17 Threat Timing (Use “Heat” to Control the Exchange)
“Heat” is sustained credible pressure that forces the opponent to respond and slows their escape choices.

Heat tools:
- Achilles pressure to keep them defensive while you improve position.
- Toe hold threat to force hands and posture changes.
- Heist threat to trigger predictable leg reactions.

Practical rule:
- Apply heat when you are improving your hub, not when you are losing it.

---

### 4.18 Two-Leg vs One-Leg Control (When Each Is Correct)
One-leg control is faster and often enough to finish.
Two-leg control is slower but prevents the opponent’s steering and escape energy.

Rules:
- If the opponent is mobile (roll/heist), upgrade toward two-leg control.
- If the opponent is stalled (knee line trapped, posture controlled), one-leg control can finish.

---

### 4.19 Re-Collection (Winning After They “Almost” Escape)
Re-collection is the system skill of re-trapping the leg after the opponent partially frees it.

Re-collection cues:
- Your wedge stays connected to their hips.
- Your knees track their knee line during movement.
- You re-attach to a hub rather than chasing the foot.

Practical rule:
- If you lose the leg, don’t chase the ankle—rebuild the hub.

---

### 4.20 Hub Switching Is Not Failure
Switching hubs is how the system applies inevitability.
A hub change is a decision, not a bailout.

Rules:
- Exposure hub when heel is hidden or pressure is dangerous.
- Breaking hub when alignment is available and escape routes are contained.
- Dilemma hub when you need to force predictable defense.
- Symmetry hub only if you can convert.

---

### 4.21 “Attack Until You’re Defending” (Symmetry Rule)
In symmetry, the person who becomes defensive first usually loses.

Operational interpretation:
- Threat must force the opponent’s hands/legs to respond, not trade.
- Your goal is to force them to clamp, separate, or turn—anything that reduces their offense.

Practical rule:
- If both players are “attacking,” you are not winning symmetry yet.

---

### 4.22 Conversion Windows (When to Leave Symmetry)
Conversion is leaving symmetry with advantage.

Windows:
- Their knee line drifts out of perfect mirror.
- Their inside-knee position is lost.
- Their hands commit to defense (toe hold threat, Achilles heat).
- Their hips turn in a way that exposes far-hip routing.

Practical rule:
- Convert on the first clean window; do not wait for “perfect.”

---

### 4.23 Decision Tree (Dense, teachable)
1) Knee line free? → re-secure knee line / wedge before anything else.
2) Heel exposed + rotation controlled? → heel hook line.
3) Heel hidden + you are safe? → exposure hub to create heel access.
4) Opponent rolling? → follow with wedges → re-collect → transfer to breaking hub.
5) Opponent heisting/standing? → control 2nd leg → orientation → knee bar / dilemma hub.
6) Symmetry (50/50/mutual)? → attack-to-defend → create separation → convert (far-hip) or exit.
7) You feel “muscling”? → alignment fix: hip line + wedge + knee line, then re-apply.

### 4.23 Decision Tree

1) Knee line threatened or slipping?
   - Re-secure knee line + wedge first.
   - If you cannot re-secure: transfer hubs (two-leg control) or disengage to re-enter.

2) Heel exposed?
   - Yes + rotation controlled → heel hook line.
   - Yes but slipping → upgrade engine (wedge/hip connection) or switch exposure method.
   - No → exposure hub (Irimi-type) to create heel access.

3) Secondary leg free (posting/stepping/pummeling)?
   - Yes → upgrade to two-leg control (Cross / Shoelace / clamp concepts) before finishing.
   - No → proceed with break line selection.

4) Opponent pressures forward (into you)?
   - Use exposure hub that stays safe under pressure.
   - Route to breaking hub once heel access and hip control are stable.

5) Opponent rolls?
   - Follow with wedges; re-collect knee line during rotation.
   - Land in a breaking hub (often Outside) rather than scrambling for the foot.

6) Opponent heists / tries to stand / sits up hard?
   - Treat as an orientation gift.
   - Control second leg → choose knee bar line or dilemma hub (toe hold ↔ heel ↔ knee bar).

7) Symmetry state (50/50 or mutual)?
   - Do not negotiate mirrored grips.
   - Attack until they must defend → create separation / inside-knee advantage.
   - Convert (far-hip routing) to an asymmetric breaking hub, or disengage and re-enter.

8) You feel like you’re muscling a finish?
   - Alignment fix, in order: hip connection → wedge/inside position → knee line → rotation.
   - Then re-apply the break line you are already aligned for.

## 5.0 SYSTEM OVERVIEW

The system attacks legs by creating asymmetry, then using Ashi Garami as the control engine to force
predictable reactions. You do not “hunt submissions”; you build a control state, follow their escape
attempt, and finish when their movement is contained. The system is a loop: ENTER → CONTROL → FOLLOW
→ BREAK → RESET/TRANSFER, repeated until the opponent is forced into a losing alignment.

### 5.1 The System Loop
ENTER
- Acquire a leg in a way that limits their access to your legs.
- Prefer entries that preserve posture and deny easy heists.

CONTROL
- Own the knee line and establish wedges that prevent hip rotation.
- Manage the second leg so they can’t steer the escape (post/step/pummel).

FOLLOW
- When they move (roll/heist/turn), your wedges travel with their hips.
- You re-collect knee line during motion and route into a better hub.

BREAK
- Select the finish line that matches your current alignment (don’t force a favorite).
- Finish only when escape routes are contained; otherwise, apply “heat” and upgrade position.

RESET / TRANSFER
- If alignment is compromised, transfer hubs rather than forcing the end.
- If symmetry appears, convert or exit and re-enter on your terms.

### 5.2 Hub Jobs and Default Routes
Exposure hubs
- Job: create safe heel/foot access while maintaining positional safety.
- Default route: Exposure hub → Breaking hub (once heel access + hip control are stable).

Breaking hubs
- Job: maximize mechanical advantage and constrain opponent movement.
- Default route: Breaking hub → finish (heel/achilles) OR dilemma hub if defense is stubborn.

Two-leg hubs
- Job: control both legs to deny rolls/heists and force predictable defenses.
- Default route: Two-leg hub → dilemma hub → breaking hub.

Dilemma hubs
- Job: present Threat A that forces Defense B, which exposes Finish C.
- Default route: toe hold threat ↔ heel exposure ↔ knee bar alignment.

Symmetry hubs
- Job: temporary state; force defense, then convert or disengage.
- Default route: attack-to-defend → separation/inside-knee → convert to asymmetric breaking hub.

### 5.3 Primary Variables (what the system “reads”)
- Knee line: trapped / slipping / free
- Heel: exposed / hidden / slipping
- Rotation: you control / opponent controls
- Second leg: pinned / posting / stepping / pummeling
- Hip line: you are past / square / behind
- Separation: asymmetric / symmetric / semi-sankaku / crossed
- Posture direction: forward pressure / lean back / roll / heist

Everything you do is an attempt to improve one of these variables while preventing the opponent from
improving theirs.

### 5.4 Finish Line Selection (match break to alignment)
Heel hook
- Chosen when heel exposure is stable and you control rotational dominance.

Toe hold
- Chosen when you can isolate the foot; also used to force predictable reactions that open heel/knee.

Knee bar
- Chosen when knee-line dominance and orientation are present; often triggered by heist energy.

Achilles / straight ankle
- Chosen as a stabilizer and a finish; also used as “heat” while you upgrade hubs.

### 5.5 Reaction Handling (default responses)
If they roll
- Follow with wedges; re-collect the knee line during rotation.
- Route into a breaking hub rather than chasing the foot.

If they heist / stand / sit up hard
- Control the second leg; treat posture as an orientation gift.
- Route to knee bar alignment or dilemma hub (toe hold ↔ heel ↔ knee bar).

If they hide heel
- Return to an exposure hub; rebuild heel access without losing the engine.
- Transfer to a breaking hub only when heel access survives movement.

If symmetry forms (50/50 or mutual)
- Apply attack-to-defend rule; create separation and inside-knee advantage.
- Convert via far-hip routing or disengage and re-enter.

### 5.6 Quality Standards (what “good reps” look like)
- You can hold the leg through movement (engine stable).
- Knee line stays controlled while you change grips (position first).
- Second leg is addressed before you commit to slow finishing.
- You can name the variable that changed when something fails, and fix that variable first.

### 5.7 System Guarantees (what the structure gives you)
- You are never “out of ideas” because every problem maps to:
  (a) a variable that degraded, and (b) a hub whose job is to solve it.
- You do not need speed if your wedges and second-leg control make the opponent predictable.
- Finishes become inevitable when CONTROL and FOLLOW are correct; BREAK is the last step.

### 5.8 Default Ladders (what you do when nothing is forced yet)

Exposure Ladder (create access safely)
- Acquire leg → wedge/knee line → stabilize second leg → expose heel/foot → transfer to breaking hub

Breaking Ladder (turn access into finish)
- Breaking hub control → apply heat → opponent defends → follow → re-collect → finish line selection

Symmetry Ladder (when 50/50 or mutual appears)
- Attack-to-defend → create separation/inside-knee advantage → convert (far-hip routing) → breaking hub

Re-Collection Ladder (after partial escape)
- Keep wedge connection → track knee line → re-trap secondary leg → re-enter a hub → re-apply heat

---

### 5.9 Switch Triggers (when to change hubs or lines)

Switch to an exposure hub when:
- Heel is hidden and you cannot keep it exposed through movement.
- Opponent’s forward pressure threatens your posture/back exposure.
- You are “grip rich” but “position poor” (engine unstable).

Switch to a two-leg hub when:
- Secondary leg is free and opponent is rolling/heisting successfully.
- You keep losing the leg during movement.
- You need to force predictability before you can break.

Switch to a dilemma hub when:
- Opponent’s defense is strong but stable (they’re not escaping, just refusing the finish).
- You need a forced hand/foot/posture reaction to open heel/knee exposure.

Switch to a breaking hub when:
- Heel/foot access is stable and rotation can be controlled.
- Knee line is captured and secondary leg is neutralized.
- You can finish without chasing.

Switch finish lines when:
- Heel hook line stalls due to heel slipping or rotation loss → apply Achilles heat or toe hold threat.
- Toe hold forces a structural response → take heel exposure or knee bar alignment.
- Heist energy appears → knee bar line becomes high percentage.

---

### 5.10 Route Map (compact)
If heel exposure is the problem:
- Exposure hub → breaking hub

If movement control is the problem:
- Two-leg hub → breaking hub

If defense is the problem (they’re stuck but safe):
- Dilemma hub → breaking hub

If symmetry is the problem:
- Symmetry ladder → conversion → breaking hub

---

### 5.11 “Heat” Management (pressure without losing position)
Heat is used to control tempo and force defense, not to finish prematurely.

Rules:
- Apply heat only when knee line + wedge are stable.
- If heat causes you to lose the engine, stop and rebuild the engine.
- Heat should improve a variable (separation, rotation dominance, posture), not just create discomfort.

Common heat-to-upgrade patterns:
- Achilles pressure → posture/hip concessions → stronger hub
- Toe hold threat → hands commit → heel exposure / knee bar alignment
- Upper-body heist threat → opponent counters posture → leg alignment opens

---

### 5.12 Safety and Risk Budget (offense-side constraints)
Risk management is positional, not emotional.

Avoid:
- Finishing while secondary leg is free (invites roll/heist and counters).
- Prolonged symmetry without conversion progress (coin-flip exchanges).
- Sacrificing posture/back safety to keep a leg you cannot control.

Prefer:
- Transfers that keep you facing the opponent and connected through the hips.
- Two-leg control when opponent’s athleticism is high.
- Conversions that create asymmetric hip lines before committing to slow breaks.

---

### 5.13 Coaching Scripts (short decision prompts)
- “Name the variable you’re losing.”
- “Second leg first.”
- “If you can’t keep it through movement, you don’t own it.”
- “Heat to upgrade, not to hope.”
- “Convert symmetry or leave.”

---

### 5.14 Minimal Routing Checklist (in-round)
- Knee line: owned?
- Heel/foot: accessible and stable?
- Second leg: controlled?
- Rotation: yours?
- Symmetry: converting?
If any answer is “no,” fix that before committing to a finish.






## 6.0 TECHNIQUE LIBRARY

### 6.1 Volume 1 — Foundations (positions, gates, continuity tools, drills, submission concept families)

#### 6.1.1 Strategic position map (entanglement progression and what each is “for”)

- Primary hub sequence (as a learning spine): irimi ashi → outside ashi → cross ashi → inside ashi → 50/50 → mutual ashi
  - The value is not “knowing names,” but understanding how each position changes:
    - who has inside position,
    - what the opponent’s best escape direction is,
    - whether heel exposure is easy/hard,
    - how much back-exposure risk exists during exposure/finishing.
- Irimi ashi (strategy frame)
  - Built to protect from counter-offense while you build offense (less back-exposure risk).
  - Breaking mechanics can be very strong once heel exposure exists.
  - Trade-off: heel exposure is harder; opponent can more easily bring pressure forward and more easily heel-slip if exposure is not correct.
- Outside ashi (strategy frame)
  - Built to make outside heel exposure easier.
  - Trade-off: greater requirement to deny back exposure during exposure/finishing.
- Cross ashi (strategy frame)
  - Provides routes to control both of the opponent’s legs and to apply multi-attack trees (Achilles variations, leg laces, etc.).
  - Used as an upgrade when a single-leg entanglement becomes “hard to get a grip on” due to opponent defensive quality.

#### 6.1.2 Non-negotiable finishing requirement: inside + outside heel hooks

- Skill requirement: finish both inside and outside heel hooks.
  - Inside heel hooks are treated as mechanically stronger, but “only inside” is incomplete.
  - If one side is missing, the opponent can steer the exchange into the side you cannot finish.
- Practical implication: exposure + breaking mechanics must exist for both sides, not just “I can get to the position.”

#### 6.1.3 Inside-position battle (primary gate for winning leg-lock exchanges)

- Definition (explicit): keep your feet and knees inside the opponent’s feet and knees.
  - If your feet/knees are inside, you gain access to the opponent’s legs and deny their access to yours.
  - Mixed inside position (each has one foot inside) reduces certainty; the stronger inside-position player dominates exchanges.
- Decision rule:
  - If inside position is lost, do not commit to breaking mechanics; re-win inside position via pummeling/adjustment first.

#### 6.1.4 General escape directions (opponent’s default defensive vectors)

- Expected defensive objectives:
  - Clear the knee line and pull the leg out (extraction).
  - Rotate/roll to change alignment and remove exposure.
  - Drive forward pressure (especially relevant when exposure is weak) to crush the entanglement and/or enable heel slip.
- System response principle:
  - Treat escapes as predictable; build control + continuity to stop them, not “react late” after the leg is already cleared.

#### 6.1.5 Double trouble (two-leg constraint concept)

- Purpose: solve stalemates where single-leg exposure is repeatedly denied.
- Use-case trigger: opponent “locks” defensively and you cannot reliably secure heel exposure on the primary leg.
- Operational idea:
  - Increase constraint by controlling both legs so the opponent cannot freeze a single defensive leg configuration without opening another line.

#### 6.1.6 Continuity tools: following a leg + switching legs

- Following a leg
  - Objective: maintain attachment to the moving target leg through defensive rotation/roll/extraction attempts.
  - Key constraint: do not allow separation between bodies during opponent movement (attachment persists while they try to pull away).
- Switching legs
  - Trigger: primary target leg line becomes difficult to hold (clear/extraction/angle loss).
  - Objective: transfer to the other leg while preserving overall control advantage rather than resetting to neutral.

#### 6.1.7 Foundational drill blocks (what the drills are encoding)

- Basic drills
  - Encode: entanglement entry structure, inside-position awareness, knee-line attachment, and maintaining connection through movement.
- Achilles lock drill
  - Encode: stable straight-foot exposure, bite alignment, and ability to maintain connection while partner resists by pulling away.
- Cowboy drill
  - Encode: maintaining correct leg/hip relationship under resistance so heel slip becomes difficult and finishing alignment stays available.

#### 6.1.8 Submission concept families (what must be true for the break to work)

- Achilles lock concepts
  - Must be applied from a connected structure where the opponent cannot simply pull away and create space.
  - Control + connection are treated as prerequisites to meaningful breaking pressure.
- Knee bar concepts
  - Requires isolating the knee/hip relationship so extension cannot be removed by rotation.
- Toe hold concepts
  - Requires controlling the leg relationship so the opponent cannot freely spin out and remove foot alignment.
- Heel hook concepts
  - Requires reliable heel exposure plus anti-heel-slip structure; breaking mechanics depend on alignment + rotational control.


### 6.2 Volume 2 — Irimi Ashi Garami (structure, stress tests, Achilles engine, heel-slip counters, roll governance, transitions)

#### 6.2.1 Setting irimi ashi (structure before any breaking attempt)

- Objective: build an irimi ashi structure where:
  - the opponent cannot pull themselves away to create separation,
  - the knee line cannot easily clear,
  - your body and their body move as a connected unit during resistance (no “daylight”/gap).
- Connection requirement (explicitly tested later):
  - When the opponent pulls back, you move with them so there is no disconnection.
  - The opponent should “drag you as if you’re one person,” rather than creating separation and extracting.

#### 6.2.2 Stress test (control validation checkpoint)

- Purpose: verify that the ashi structure is tight enough to survive real defense before investing in finishing mechanics.
- Pass/fail cues:
  - Fail: opponent pulls away and you disconnect; knee line clears; you end up chasing late.
  - Pass: opponent pulls and you stay connected; there is no separation between bodies; extraction becomes difficult.

#### 6.2.3 Basic Achilles lock (baseline attack + forcing threat)

- Use as:
  - a legitimate finish when structure is correct,
  - a forcing threat that drives predictable defensive reactions into heel-hook opportunities.
- Key alignment constraint (practical):
  - If the lock is applied while the opponent can pull away and create space, the opponent will eventually pull the foot free.

#### 6.2.4 Countering basic extraction (anti-withdrawal retention)

- Trigger: opponent attempts to straighten/withdraw the leg past your knee line.
- Response goals:
  - stay attached during the withdrawal attempt,
  - close space (remove separation),
  - re-secure the structure before re-applying breaking pressure.

#### 6.2.5 Countering forward pressure with elbow position (anti-crush + anti-heel-slip layer)

- Problem set: opponent comes forward to crush posture and/or initiate heel slip while you are committed to the lock.
- Structural response (explicit cues):
  - Take the elbow in (toward ribs) rather than letting it flare or collapse.
  - Keep hands as high as possible.
  - Keep chin as low as possible.
  - As pressure drives the exchange, start to look belly down rather than remaining flat (directional posture management).
- Failure signal:
  - If hands drop and chin rises, bridging and maintaining breaking alignment become mechanically difficult.

#### 6.2.6 Countering forward pressure with post ashi (wedge creation + stronger break option)

- Trigger: opponent’s forward pressure makes the standard Achilles configuration imperfect/unstable.
- Structural build (explicit mechanics cues):
  - Bring one foot inside and place it on the opponent’s hip.
  - Use the second foot to push down (toward the floor) while raising hips to create a physical wedge.
  - Goal: make it “not easy” for the opponent to drive forward because the wedge blocks the crush.
- Outcome:
  - Post ashi provides a stronger breaking platform once wedge is established.

#### 6.2.7 Countering forward pressure with belly-down Achilles (directional finish choice)

- When pressure/rotation drives the exchange belly down, the finishing direction is treated as acceptable/expected.
- Constraint: structure must remain tight enough that the opponent cannot straighten and pull out past your knee line during the turn.

#### 6.2.8 Switching to butterfly ashi (hub transfer when geometry demands)

- Use-case: opponent’s posture/angle makes standard irimi continuation suboptimal.
- Objective: preserve attachment while changing entanglement geometry so exposure and control return.

#### 6.2.9 Achilles ↔ heel hook dilemma (forcing pair)

- Conceptual engine:
  - Achilles threat forces a defensive movement that exposes the heel.
  - Heel-hook threat forces defense that reopens Achilles pressure.
- Decision rule:
  - When the opponent’s defense meaningfully closes one line, immediately take the paired line (do not persist with the closed line).

#### 6.2.10 Heel slip counter: “high hands” recovery (anti-slip structure)

- Trigger: heel starts slipping as opponent comes forward.
- Key cues (explicit checklist used for maintaining the break under slip pressure):
  - Back heel engagement.
  - Knees pinched together.
  - Opponent’s toes drawn toward their butt.
  - Opponent’s heel aligned toward your chin.
  - Bridge and look over the bottom shoulder.
- Stress-test implication:
  - After establishing high hands + correct ashi, the opponent moving should not create separation; you should remain tight enough to proceed into mechanics.

#### 6.2.11 Heel slip counter: roll-through recovery (“reboot the system”)

- Trigger: opponent comes forward, manages to heel slip, and you do not want to be crushed.
- Response concept: roll through and re-establish a stable entanglement rather than fighting from a collapsing position.
- Explicit mechanics cues included in the roll-through response:
  - Point toes to the ground (foot to floor).
  - Use the roll to transition into a more stable entanglement (including a cross-ashi outcome) or roll the opponent through into a position where you can re-select the finishing route.

#### 6.2.12 Switching to outside ashi (transition to stronger exposure/breaking mechanics)

- Trigger: during transitions, the leg can “sneak out” in a way that favors outside-ashi breaking mechanics.
- Principle:
  - Choose the entanglement that makes your breaking mechanics strongest for the current geometry rather than staying committed to the initial hub.

#### 6.2.13 Single roll theory (roll governance model)

- Purpose: determine how many times and how fast the opponent can roll, and structure the position so the roll resolves into your advantage.
- Core constraint:
  - The directionality of force and your connection determine whether rolling becomes an escape or becomes a controlled transition.
- Explicit structural cues:
  - Create a strong connection of your leg to their leg (remove “daylight”/space).
  - Point knee toward the floor during the roll response (directional control cue).
  - Re-run the stress test logic: if connection is correct, the opponent should not be able to pull free during/after the roll.

#### 6.2.14 Figure-four switch when the opponent refuses to roll (forced reconfiguration)

- Trigger: opponent turns back into you, peels the foot, defends patiently, and does not give the expected roll.
- Execution constraints (explicit):
  - Do not perform the switch slowly; push immediately and pull your knee in.
  - Do not flare the knee in a way that allows full leg extraction.
  - Establish the figure-four configuration.
  - Flare knees and bring hips up; point toes as part of the finishing alignment.
- Additional distinction encoded:
  - Belly-up vs belly-down breaking mechanics differ; the position switch can change which mechanics set applies.

#### 6.2.15 Breaking mechanics (finish layer after exposure/control)

- Principle: breaking mechanics are a distinct layer after structure + exposure are correct.
- Included distinctions:
  - Breaking mechanics differ belly up vs belly down.
  - Slippage becomes more likely (e.g., when sweaty), so anti-slip structure and correct alignment are treated as mandatory.

---

### 6.3 Volume 3 — Outside Ashi (hip control, exposure variants, anti-back-exposure constraint)

#### 6.3.1 Irimi ashi vs outside ashi (selection rule)

- Outside ashi is selected when:
  - outside heel exposure pathways are more available than in irimi,
  - and you can maintain the additional safety constraints required to deny back exposure.

#### 6.3.2 Control while denying back exposure (non-negotiable constraint)

- The outside-ashi game is defined by two simultaneous requirements:
  - expose the heel,
  - deny the opponent’s turn-away lane that creates back exposure.
- Practical evaluation rule:
  - Any exposure pathway that opens an easy turn-away/back-take route is treated as structurally incorrect/incomplete.

## 8.0 APPLICATION SCENARIOS

### 8.1 Inside-position scramble: both players hunting legs (who gets to attack first)

- Problem shape
  - Both players have partial access; inside lanes are contested (feet/knees pummeling).
  - Attacks initiated without inside position become mutual-attack races rather than controlled offense.
- Primary objective
  - Win inside position (feet + knees inside) before committing to exposure/finishing.
- Decision rules
  - If inside position is lost mid-attack, treat it as an emergency: repair lanes first, then re-apply threat engine.
  - If inside position is mixed (each has one inside lane), treat it as unstable: continue pummeling until you have a clear lane advantage.
- Success indicators
  - You can access their legs while their access to your legs is blocked/delayed by your inside lanes.
- Common failure patterns
  - Chasing a heel/foot while losing lane control, resulting in counter-entry or mutual exposure.
  - Treating “touching the leg” as control while knee line is not governed.

### 8.2 Opponent attempts a basic extraction (pulling the leg out / clearing knee line)

- Problem shape
  - Opponent withdraws the trapped leg; if separation appears, the leg clears.
- Primary objective
  - Maintain attachment (no separation), re-secure knee-line governance, then re-enter the threat engine.
- Immediate priorities
  - Follow the leg during the withdrawal attempt (do not reach late after the leg has cleared).
  - Re-establish the entanglement clamp before applying breaking pressure again.
- Decision rules
  - If your connection breaks (separation appears), treat the finishing attempt as over; rebuild control first.
  - If extraction attempts repeatedly succeed, treat it as a structural problem (failed stress test): rebuild the hub, then retest.
- Failure patterns
  - Continuing to pull on a lock while the opponent is already clearing the knee line.
  - “Foot chasing” after extraction begins instead of maintaining a connected entanglement.

### 8.3 Opponent drives forward pressure into the entanglement (crushing posture + heel-slip pressure)

- Problem shape
  - Forward pressure collapses posture, reduces finishing leverage, and increases the chance of heel slip.
- Primary objective
  - Preserve structure under pressure while keeping the break line available; choose the correct counter-family rather than fighting the crush directly.
- Response families (selection by what pressure is doing)
  - Elbow-position solution (structure-first)
    - Keep elbow position correct (no flaring/collapse) to preserve mechanical advantage under pressure.
    - Keep hands high and chin low to prevent posture collapse from killing the break.
    - If the exchange is driving belly-down, accept the directional finish vector rather than resisting into collapse.
  - Post-ashi wedge solution (wedge-first)
    - Place a foot to the hip and use the other foot to push down while raising hips to form a wedge.
    - Objective: make forward pressure mechanically “expensive” and preserve a stronger break platform.
  - Belly-down Achilles option (directional finish choice)
    - When posture/pressure forces the vector, finish belly down while maintaining knee-line control.
- Failure patterns
  - Letting posture collapse (hands drop; chin rises), then trying to “muscle” the finish.
  - Ignoring the wedge requirement and accepting continuous forward pressure until structure breaks.

### 8.4 Opponent uses defensive rolling as the main escape tool

- Problem shape
  - Opponent rolls to change alignment, strip heel exposure, or clear the knee line.
- Primary objective
  - Convert the roll into continuity: follow the leg, govern the roll outcome, and re-land in control/exposure.
- Core tools (how the system expects you to solve rolls)
  - Single-roll theory (roll governance)
    - Structure the position so the roll resolves predictably (not infinite escape).
    - Maintain tight connection; if connection is correct, rolling does not create separation.
  - Follow-the-leg rule (attachment through rotation)
    - Maintain attachment as the opponent rotates; do not allow the leg to “leave your body.”
  - Switch-legs rule (continuity if target clears)
    - If the primary target leg clears during the roll, transfer to the other leg or transfer hubs rather than resetting.
- Failure patterns
  - Letting the roll create separation, then trying to recover with late grips.
  - Persisting on a defended line through the roll rather than converting to the paired line when exposure changes.

### 8.5 Opponent defends by refusing to roll (stalling, peeling, “patient defense”)

- Problem shape
  - Opponent denies the expected rolling response and instead defends in place, attempting to peel and extract.
- Primary objective
  - Force a reconfiguration that reopens exposure rather than stalling on a defended line.
- System response
  - Execute the figure-four switch as the forced reconfiguration when the opponent refuses to roll.
  - Key constraints: the switch is decisive and structured (not slow), and the knee/hip relationship must not open an extraction window during the switch.
- Failure patterns
  - Switching too slowly and allowing full extraction.
  - Remaining committed to the original line when defense has stabilized.

### 8.6 Heel slip occurs during exposure/finishing (loss of heel control)

- Problem shape
  - Heel begins to slip from control, often paired with forward pressure or rotation.
- Primary objective
  - Recover heel control before it fully escapes; restore alignment so the same defense cannot immediately re-strip the heel.
- Response families
  - High-hands recovery (structure-first)
    - Re-secure heel by adjusting hand position and tightening structure so forward pressure cannot strip the heel.
    - Maintain knee pinch and alignment constraints that keep the heel “loaded” into the breaking line.
  - Roll-through recovery (movement-first)
    - Use a roll-through to rebuild a stable entanglement when forward pressure + heel slip are collapsing the structure.
    - Treat roll-through as a controlled transition to regain a finishing platform, not a disengagement.
- Failure patterns
  - Attempting recovery after full separation has already occurred (too late).
  - Re-catching the heel without restoring knee-line/hip control, resulting in immediate repeat slip.

### 8.7 Outside-ashi exposure attempt creates back-exposure risk (turn-away lane opens)

- Problem shape
  - As you chase heel exposure, the opponent gains a clear turn-away lane that can expose your back.
- Primary objective
  - Deny the turn-away lane first, then re-attempt exposure; never treat back exposure as an acceptable trade for heel exposure.
- Safety rule
  - Any exposure method that gives a clean turn-away lane is structurally incorrect/incomplete and must be changed.
- Practical corrections
  - Re-govern hips (increase hip control) before recommitting to heel exposure.
  - Adjust the control method (e.g., re-establish hip control format) so exposure is created without opening the back.
- Failure patterns
  - Prioritizing heel contact over hip governance.
  - Allowing the opponent to rotate freely during the exposure moment.

### 8.8 Opponent presents a “high leg” defensive problem (harder to enter or exposes different lines)

- Problem shape
  - High-leg positioning changes access routes and can deny the standard entry/exposure path.
- System responses (as framed in the series)
  - Use dedicated counters for the high-leg scenario rather than forcing standard entries.
  - Wrist-to-wrist toe-hold option appears as a named solution in this scenario class (use when alignment presents it).
- Failure patterns
  - Trying to force standard exposure when the leg height/angle denies it.
  - Ignoring the alternate line and burning time into a structurally blocked pathway.

### 8.9 Opponent drops to a kneeling posture (exposure geometry changes)

- Problem shape
  - Kneeling posture changes the exposure pathway and the direction of control required.
- Primary objective
  - Use kneeling-opponent-specific exposure to maintain advantage rather than losing the leg line as posture changes.
- Failure patterns
  - Treating the kneeling opponent like a standing/squared opponent and losing exposure timing.

### 8.10 “Locked legs” / stalemate defense (opponent freezes the position)

- Problem shape
  - Opponent locks legs and uses structure to deny exposure; the exchange stalls.
- Primary objective
  - Apply multi-leg constraint tools and dilemma escalation to force openings; do not wait out the stall.
- System tools
  - Double trouble (two-leg constraint)
    - Control both legs so the opponent cannot freeze one defensive configuration without opening another line.
  - Toe-hold dilemma escalation (toe hold ↔ knee bar) where applicable
    - Use a paired threat to prevent the opponent from defending with one static alignment.
- Failure patterns
  - Persisting in single-heel hunting while the opponent’s locked structure prevents exposure.
  - Treating stalemate as neutral time instead of an active “problem to solve.”

### 8.11 50/50: opponent tries to separate as the “safe exit” (separation dilemma activation)

- Problem shape
  - Opponent attempts to separate and reset from 50/50; symmetry often stalls if separation is unopposed.
- Primary objective
  - Run the toe-hold ↔ separation dilemma so separation is no longer a free defensive choice.
- Sequencing constraints (internal order)
  - Inside-knee position gate first.
  - Hand-fight constraint second (win grips/deny grips).
  - Separation work third (create usable geometry).
  - Then run dilemma pair(s) (toe hold ↔ separation; toe hold ↔ heel hook).
- Failure patterns
  - Attempting to attack without solving hand fight.
  - Attempting to “hold 50/50” without separation mechanics, resulting in stall.

### 8.12 Mutual ashi: both players have access (priority sequencing decides winner)

- Problem shape
  - Mutual access exists; committing first can expose you to reactive counters.
- Primary objective
  - Apply priority rule “going last” and exit mutuality into a favorable hub rather than trading blind finishes.
- System tools
  - Going last (timing rule)
    - Induce or detect opponent commitment, then respond to end in advantage.
  - Rolling through (exit method)
    - Use roll-through as a controlled exit to land in a favorable entanglement rather than staying in mutuality.
  - Far-hip ashi (anti-rotation control)
    - Govern hips during the exit so the opponent cannot rotate free mid-transition.
- Failure patterns
  - Initiating first without control advantage.
  - Exiting mutuality without hip governance, allowing the opponent to clear and reset.


## 9.0 DRILLING PROGRESSIONS

### 9.1 Foundational connection and “no separation” standard (baseline for all hubs)

- 9.3.1 Stress test (general)
  - Goal: validate that position is stable before you treat finishing mechanics as relevant.

- 9.3.2 Irimi-specific stress test emphasis (connection + pressure management)

  - Drill objective: partner moves (withdrawal/roll/turn) and you remain connected to the moving leg so you do not end in late chases.

    - Light movement → continuous movement → partner attempts to “leave the entanglement” with committed withdrawals/turns.
  - Pass/fail
    - Pass: your attachment survives; you stay on the leg line.

- 9.4.2 Switching legs (continuity when primary target clears)
  - Drill objective: when the primary target leg becomes non-viable, you transfer to the other leg (or transfer hub) without resetting to neutral.

  - Drill objective: partner rolls defensively; you structure attachment and angle so the roll resolves into maintained control rather than repeated resets.

### 9.5 Heel-slip recovery progressions (two named recovery families)

- 9.5.1 “High hands” recovery reps (structure-first heel-slip defense)

    - Heel/line alignment oriented into your breaking mechanics.
    - Posture/bridge alignment preserved under pressure.
  - Resistance ladder
    - Partner lightly slips → partner aggressively drives forward while slipping → partner combines slip with rotation.

- 9.5.2 Roll-through recovery reps (movement-first heel-slip defense)
  - Drill objective: when forward pressure + heel slip collapses the position, roll-through to re-establish a stable entanglement rather than fighting from collapse.

### 9.6 “Locked legs / stalemate” breaking progressions (double trouble + dilemma escalation)

  - Success indicator
    - The opponent is forced to open a line (by reacting) and you regain a clean exposure path.
  - Failure pattern
    - You treat double trouble as static holding (no forced reaction) and the stalemate remains.

- 9.6.2 Toe hold ↔ knee bar dilemma reps (when used to defeat stalls)
  - Drill objective: partner defends one line with alignment; you take the paired line rather than persisting into a stabilized defense.
  - Pass criteria
    - Clear, immediate conversion on defense trigger (defense dictates the switch).

### 9.7 50/50 drilling progression (gates → separation → dilemmas)

    - Drill until you can consistently establish/repair inside-knee relationship before attacking.

  - Step 4: Dilemma pairs
    - Toe hold ↔ separation.

### 9.8 Mutual ashi drilling progression (priority rule → controlled exits)

  - Drill objective: do not initiate blindly; recognize/induce partner commitment and respond to end in advantage.
  - Pass/fail
    - Pass: your response is structurally advantaged (control/exposure improves).

- 9.8.2 Rolling-through exit reps (leave mutuality into a favorable hub)
  - Drill objective: use roll-through as a controlled exit that maintains attachment and lands you in a favorable entanglement family.
  - Constraint
    - Exit must preserve hip governance (far-hip control concept) so partner cannot rotate free mid-transition.

## 11.0 FAILURE PATTERNS

### 11.1 Inside-position failure: losing lanes while “attacking”

- What goes wrong
  - You initiate exposure/finishing while your feet/knees are not inside the opponent’s feet/knees.
  - You lose inside lanes mid-attack and continue anyway, turning the exchange into a mutual-attack race.
- Why it happens (structural cause)
  - Lane control is treated as “nice to have” rather than an attack-permission gate.
  - Focus shifts to heel/foot chasing; legs stop doing denial work.
- Typical symptoms
  - Opponent pummels inside and immediately gains their own entry to your legs.
  - You feel forced to rush finishing mechanics because the opponent is threatening counters.
- Corrections (decision rules)
  - If inside position is unclear or lost: pummel / re-win lanes first, then re-apply the threat engine.
  - If you can’t re-win inside lanes from the current hub: switch legs or transfer hubs rather than forcing.
- Drill fix
  - Start every positional round with a lane goal: first 10–15 seconds are lanes only; no grips/finishes until lanes are clearly won.

### 11.2 Connection failure: separation appears and you keep “working”

- What goes wrong
  - Partner pulls away and you allow daylight; you continue pulling on locks while the leg is already clearing.
- Why it happens
  - Treating the submission grip as “control” instead of treating body-to-body attachment and knee-line clamp as control.
- Typical symptoms
  - Foot/heel repeatedly slips out during normal withdrawal attempts.
  - You are constantly reaching and re-gripping instead of staying attached.
- Corrections
  - If separation appears: finishing attempt is over; rebuild entanglement structure first.
  - Re-run stress test logic: if the position does not survive simple withdrawal, do not invest in breaking mechanics.
- Drill fix
  - “No daylight” rounds: partner’s only job is to pull away; your only job is to stay attached (no finishing allowed).

### 11.3 Knee-line failure: allowing the opponent to clear before exposure is stabilized

- What goes wrong
  - The opponent’s knee line clears during exposure attempts, turning the position into foot chasing.
- Why it happens
  - You chase heel/foot early without first stabilizing the leg relationship and clamp.
- Typical symptoms
  - Exposure is momentary; heel appears then disappears as the knee line retracts.
  - You repeatedly “almost had it” but never get sustained exposure.
- Corrections
  - Treat knee-line governance as a prerequisite to exposure and breaking.
  - If the knee line is drifting free, stop the exposure attempt and re-secure the control layer.
- Drill fix
  - Knee-line retention rounds: attacker scores only by keeping the knee line trapped for time; not by finishing.

### 11.4 Forward-pressure failure: posture collapses and you try to “pull harder”

- What goes wrong
  - Opponent drives forward pressure into the entanglement; your posture collapses and your finish becomes weaker.
- Why it happens
  - Missing the structural solutions (elbow positioning; high hands/chin low; post-ashi wedge; directional belly-down option).
- Typical symptoms
  - Hands drop, chin rises; you feel compressed and lose leverage.
  - Heel slip accelerates because structure degrades under pressure.
- Corrections (selection rule)
  - If pressure threatens posture: apply elbow-position structure (hands high, chin low, elbow managed) before continuing.
  - If pressure remains dominant: build post-ashi wedge.
  - If the pressure/rotation dictates: accept belly-down line while maintaining knee-line control.
- Drill fix
  - Pressure rounds: defender’s only job is forward pressure; attacker’s job is to select the correct counter-family on time.

### 11.5 Heel-slip failure: “catching” the heel without anti-slip structure

- What goes wrong
  - Heel exposure is obtained briefly, but heel slips immediately; you re-catch repeatedly with no durable control.
- Why it happens
  - Exposure is treated as the finish; anti-heel-slip structure is not rebuilt after the slip begins.
- Typical symptoms
  - The heel slides out at the moment you apply finishing rotation or as the opponent pressures forward.
- Corrections (recovery rule)
  - As soon as heel slip starts: choose a recovery family (high-hands or roll-through) rather than “fight the slip” in place.
  - Recovery is only “complete” if it restores a state where the same defense cannot immediately re-strip the heel.
- Drill fix
  - Start reps from “heel starting to slip” and require the recovery to finish with stable exposure (not just a re-grip).

### 11.6 Roll failure: opponent rolls and you lose attachment (late chasing)

- What goes wrong
  - Defensive rolling creates separation; you end up reaching after the roll resolves.
- Why it happens
  - Lack of follow-the-leg habit; single-roll governance not applied; focus on hands instead of maintaining structural attachment.
- Typical symptoms
  - After a roll you are no longer connected at the knee line.
  - Opponent resets and you must rebuild from scratch repeatedly.

  - Treat rolling as expected; follow the leg during the roll.

  - Roll-only rounds: defender must roll; attacker must maintain attachment and re-land in control without re-gripping late.

## 12.0 KEY PASSAGES

### 12.1 Inside-position battle as the governing priority (lanes decide offense/defense)

- Inside position (feet and knees inside the opponent’s feet and knees) is treated as the primary positional battle that determines who can attack legs first and who is forced into defense.

### 12.2 “No separation” as the definition of real control (entanglement quality standard)

- A stable entanglement is defined by the inability of the opponent to pull away and create daylight between bodies; if they can create separation, finishing attempts are premature.

- Repeated emphasis appears via:

  - heel-slip recovery (assume exposure attempts will be contested),
  - roll-following (assume opponent will try to move to escape).

- Inside lanes determine:
  - who can enter first,
  - who must defend,
  - whether exchanges become mutual-attack races.
- The system repeatedly routes back to lane repair (pummeling) when lanes are lost.
- Practical training implication:
  - Dedicated lane-only rounds are a high leverage training tool because they build the true permission structure for attacks.

- The stress test concept is used to prevent:
  - false confidence (positions that only work when the opponent is passive),
  - premature focus on breaking mechanics when the platform is unstable.
- “No separation” functions as a universal quality filter:
  - if the opponent can pull away and create daylight, they can usually clear the leg line eventually.
- Practical implication:
  - Measuring entanglement quality by “could I finish a compliant partner” is replaced by “could they extract if they try.”

### 14.5 Dilemma logic as the system’s conversion engine (what makes it work vs fail)

- Dilemma success depends on two prerequisites:
  - both threats are credible (they require real defensive action),
  - conversion happens early (defense trigger causes immediate switch).
- Dilemma failure patterns are consistent:
  - one threat is not credible (e.g., Achilles without tight connection; heel exposure that slips immediately),
  - conversion is delayed (attacker insists on the defended line until defense stabilizes).

### 14.6 Risk management: outside-ashi back-exposure denial is not optional

- Outside-ashi is treated as a “higher exposure, higher constraint” hub:
  - heel exposure is more available,
  - but it is only usable if hip governance denies turn-away/back exposure routes.
- Practical implication:
  - Outside-ashi training should explicitly score:
    - “exposure created while denying turn-away” (success),
    - “exposure created but turn-away lane opened” (failure, even if heel was briefly caught).

### 14.7 Cross-ashi as an “upgrade-first” subsystem (why passing-a-leg families exist)

- Cross-ashi is presented with known “issues” that require upgrades:
  - pummeling to re-win lanes,

  - if exposure is not stable, upgrade structure rather than force finish attempts.
