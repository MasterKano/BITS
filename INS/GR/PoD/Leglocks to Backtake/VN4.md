# VIEWING NOTES – Gordon Ryan: Pillars of Defense — Leg Locks to Back Takes

## 4.0 Volume 4 — Intro to Outside Ashi

### 4.1 Intro to outside ashi
**Key concepts**
- Outside ashi is framed as a higher-attack, higher-conversion hub than Irimi in this context (more ways to come forward and connect upper body).
- Core conversion objective remains: exit the leg exchange into either (a) chest-to-chest pin for passing, or (b) chest-to-back connection for back takes.
- Early theme introduced: ability to come forward / put knee to the floor changes the entire decision tree.

**Position–structure**
- Start state: outside ashi configuration where you can come forward and potentially place knee near opponent’s hip line.
- Finish states: cross-body ride / chest-to-chest passing, or back exposure leading to chest-to-back attachment.

**Decision rules (IF/THEN)**
- IF you can come forward and get knee-to-floor next to the hip line -> THEN you can force a chest-to-chest vs chest-to-back branch.
- IF forward pressure creates turn-away -> THEN pursue back connection; IF it creates turn-in -> THEN accept chest-to-chest pin and pass.
- IF you cannot safely come forward (constraints active) -> THEN solve the constraint first (don’t force forward commitment).

**Failure signatures + fixes**
- You’re “in outside ashi” but cannot come forward -> fix by identifying the active constraint (often Achilles/foot control) and addressing it before committing.
- You come forward without upper-body control -> fix by building cranial/shoulder control as you advance.

**Completion checks**
- You can reliably enter a forward-pressure phase (knee placement + upper-body connection).
- You can force a clear branch into pin (pass) or attachment (back).

**System tags**
- Outside ashi hub; Forward-pressure phase; Pass/back dilemma; Constraint-first gate

---

### 4.2 Back step to cross body ride
**Key concepts**
- Cross-body ride is presented as a primary conversion landing from outside ashi: it creates a stable top alignment that feeds both pass and back.
- The “cross body ride” entry is linked to rolling/turning mechanics and immediate upper-body connection (shoulder/upper-body control before chasing finish).

**Position–structure**
- Outside ashi -> roll/backstep style entry -> cross-body ride alignment -> branch:
  - chest-to-chest (pass),
  - chest-to-back (back exposure when they turn away).

**Decision rules (IF/THEN)**
- IF you arrive in cross-body ride and opponent turns away -> THEN bring your head up high with theirs and close distance (deny them space to run away).
- IF you cannot get close enough via wrist control (they’re far) -> THEN upgrade grip to shoulder/upper-body control.
- IF they bring their back back to the floor -> THEN treat it as a pin/passing continuation, not a reset.

**Failure signatures + fixes**
- They’re “too far away” for you to climb -> fix by re-gripping higher (shoulder/upper-body) and pulling them into you before climbing.
- You chase the back while their head line is low/escaping -> fix by raising head position and re-closing distance before hook pursuit.

**Completion checks**
- Cross-body ride is stable enough that you can (a) keep them close, and (b) advance to upper-body control.
- You can convert cross-body ride into either a pin lane or a back lane on command.

**System tags**
- Cross-body ride entry; Head-height rule; Upper-body grip escalation; Connection-before-finish

### 4.3 Failed cross body ride to guard pass
**Key concepts**
- Failure branch is explicit: if the cross-body ride entrance fails, you “lose the pass” and must immediately transition to a stabilizing top layer (camping) and continue.
- Passing and back taking are treated as the same loop: failed backtake/pass attempts feed the next top-control position.

**Position–structure**
- Attempt cross-body ride -> if it breaks -> enter camping/top stabilization -> continue pass/back branches from there.

**Decision rules (IF/THEN)**
- IF cross-body ride entrance fails -> THEN go directly to camping (don’t scramble/re-guard yourself).
- IF you lose one lane (pass) -> THEN immediately play the other lane (camping stabilization to re-open pass/back options).
- IF you can point knees away during the race -> THEN you pass or take the back (either outcome is acceptable, but you must pick a lane).

**Failure signatures + fixes**
- You stall after the failed entry -> fix by immediately adopting camping posture/connection and re-running the dilemma.
- You try to “save” the failed entry with loose movement -> fix by stabilizing first (camping), then progressing.

**Completion checks**
- You land in a stable top layer after a failed entry (not neutral).
- You can immediately re-enter passing progression from camping without reset.

**System tags**
- Failure-branch conversion; Camping stabilization; No-reset rule; Pass/back loop

### 4.4 Full roll to side body lock
**Key concepts**
- Full roll is introduced as a converter into a side body lock, especially when the opponent uses leg/heel exposure attempts during transitions.
- Half roll vs full roll distinction is explicitly stated:
  - half roll = stay belly-down,
  - full roll = roll fully and re-orient (including leg switching/position changes).

**Position–structure**
- Outside ashi exchange -> opponent attempts to expose heel -> full roll -> side body lock control -> continue to pass/back outcomes.

**Decision rules (IF/THEN)**
- IF heel exposure threat appears during the transition -> THEN choose the roll family that keeps you safe while advancing control (full roll to body lock).
- IF you stay belly down (half roll) -> THEN you are choosing the “containment” variant; IF you roll through (full roll) -> THEN you are choosing the “re-orient” variant.
- IF you see the opportunity to take a body lock “right off the bat” as they reset -> THEN take it immediately (don’t let them return to neutral).

**Failure signatures + fixes**
- You roll but lose connection -> fix by treating the roll as a control entry (body lock), not a movement.
- You exit the roll without a clear lane -> fix by immediately establishing body lock pressure and then choosing pass vs back.

**Completion checks**
- You complete the roll into a usable control (side body lock) rather than a scramble.
- You can progress from the body lock into a top pin or back exposure sequence.

**System tags**
- Full roll converter; Side body lock; Half vs full roll; Control-on-landing

### 4.5 Knee down theory
**Key concepts**
- Knee-to-floor is treated as a key “unlock” for forward pressure; but it is not always available (it depends on constraints, especially Achilles/foot control).
- Achilles grip is repeatedly tied back to whether you can effectively pressure forward / sprawl / keep heel-to-butt positioning.

**Position–structure**
- Outside ashi forward phase: knee-to-floor attempt -> forward pressure -> elbow/shoulder positioning battle -> chest-to-chest or chest-to-back outcome.

**Decision rules (IF/THEN)**
- IF opponent has an effective Achilles grip -> THEN knee-to-floor pressure and forward sprawl become difficult; address the constraint first.
- IF you can get knee to the floor effectively -> THEN begin separating legs and elongating the opponent (pressure-forward mechanics become available).
- IF you cannot come forward because elbow/structure blocks you -> THEN you must win the elbow/shoulder battle before knee-down pressure is meaningful.

**Failure signatures + fixes**
- You attempt knee-down while Achilles control is active -> fix by neutralizing Achilles/foot control before committing forward.
- You get knee down but can’t pressure forward -> fix by separating their legs + winning the elbow/shoulder alignment (otherwise your pressure is “stuck”).

**Completion checks**
- Knee-to-floor is achieved with the ability to apply forward pressure (not merely touching the knee down).
- You can separate their legs and prevent them from re-threading while you advance.

**System tags**
- Knee-down gate; Achilles constraint; Forward pressure mechanics; Leg separation requirement

### 4.6 Elbow vs shoulder battle
**Key concepts**
- A central limiter is stated: if their elbow stays “inside your shoulder” (their elbow line blocks your shoulder line), you can’t come forward and pressure effectively.
- The winning action is framed as pushing the elbow across and inserting your shoulder position relative to their elbow (shoulder-inside-elbow concept).

**Position–structure**
- You attempt to come forward -> they block with elbow/frames -> you win elbow position -> you achieve chest-to-chest or chest-to-back contact.

**Decision rules (IF/THEN)**
- IF their elbow is inside your shoulder and blocking -> THEN you must push the elbow across before forward pressure will work.
- IF you can get your shoulder inside their elbow -> THEN you can start pressuring and choosing chest-to-chest vs chest-to-back.
- IF you cannot win the elbow line -> THEN do not force the forward phase; re-stabilize and re-enter the battle.

**Failure signatures + fixes**
- You keep driving but your shoulder can’t advance -> fix by first moving their elbow across (don’t “drive into a frame”).
- You win elbow briefly then lose it on transition -> fix by immediately converting elbow win into a connection (ear/shoulder positioning and pressure).

**Completion checks**
- Their elbow is no longer blocking your shoulder from coming forward.
- You can achieve meaningful chest contact (pin or back-contact initiation).

**System tags**
- Elbow line control; Shoulder-inside-elbow; Frame-breaking; Forward pressure enablement

### 4.7 Top side shoulder rule
**Key concepts**
- A positional heuristic is introduced using a vertical “line” concept: where the top-side shoulder sits relative to a vertical reference determines the likely best lane (chest-to-back vs chest-to-chest).
- The rule is used to quickly decide between back exposure pursuit versus guard-pass pinning.

**Position–structure**
- Outside ashi forward phase -> evaluate top-side shoulder position -> select lane:
  - chest-to-back when shoulder position indicates back exposure,
  - chest-to-chest when shoulder position indicates pin/passing opportunity.

**Decision rules (IF/THEN)**
- IF top-side shoulder is “in front” of the vertical reference -> THEN prioritize chest-to-back work (back exposure lane).
- IF top-side shoulder is “behind” that reference -> THEN prioritize chest-to-chest pin (guard pass lane).
- IF the shoulder position changes during pressure -> THEN re-evaluate and switch lanes immediately (rule is dynamic, not static).

**Failure signatures + fixes**
- You choose the wrong lane and get stalled -> fix by re-checking shoulder position and switching (don’t insist).
- You see the lane but delay -> fix by committing immediately once the rule indicates advantage.

**Completion checks**
- You can call the lane based on shoulder position quickly and act without hesitation.
- Your chosen lane produces actual progress (back contact or chest-to-chest pin).

**System tags**
- Shoulder-position heuristic; Lane selection rule; Dynamic branching; Fast decision gate

### 4.8 Clearing a cross lat
**Key concepts**
- Cross-lat post is explicitly named as a common strong defensive structure (“a good person is always going to have a cross lat”) that must be beaten to come forward.
- Multiple clearing concepts are described, including far-track grips and frame/crossface style flattening mechanics.

**Position–structure**
- Opponent uses cross-lat post to block your forward pressure -> you beat the post -> you flatten the far shoulder -> you enter chest-to-chest or chest-to-back.

**Decision rules (IF/THEN)**
- IF cross-lat post is present -> THEN you must beat it before you can reliably come forward.
- IF you can establish a far-track grip -> THEN you can start pulling the shoulder line through and flattening them.
- IF they retract/pull the elbow back after you begin clearing -> THEN respond with framing/crossface style control to keep the shoulder flattened and continue.

**Failure signatures + fixes**
- You try to pressure forward while cross-lat post is intact -> fix by first clearing the post (grip/angle change), then pressuring.
- You clear briefly but they re-post -> fix by immediately transitioning into shoulder-flattening control (don’t let the shoulder re-square).

**Completion checks**
- Cross-lat post is no longer structurally blocking your forward pressure.
- Far shoulder is flattened enough that you can connect (pin or back contact).

**System tags**
- Cross-lat post; Post-beating sequence; Far-track grip; Shoulder flattening; Entry gate

### 4.9 Chest-to-chest passes to backtakes
**Key concepts**
- A simple “race” framing is stated during transitions: if you can point their knees away from you, you pass or take the back; either is acceptable, but you must win the knee direction.
- Chest-to-chest pinning is treated as a stable base that can force reactions (especially turn-away) that reopen the back.

**Position–structure**
- From outside ashi conversions -> chest-to-chest pin lane -> knee-direction win -> either pass completion or turn-away reaction into back.

**Decision rules (IF/THEN)**
- IF you can point their knees away from you -> THEN you can pass or take the back (choose based on reaction and control).
- IF chest-to-chest pin is stable -> THEN keep pressuring; IF they turn away -> THEN attach to back; IF they turn toward -> THEN continue pass.
- IF you lose the pin during the transition -> THEN re-stabilize (camping/north-south/cross-body) before re-attacking.

**Failure signatures + fixes**
- You can’t redirect knees (they keep them pointed into you) -> fix by returning to leg-separation + elbow/shoulder wins that enable forward pressure.
- You over-chase back while pin is available -> fix by accepting pin first and letting turn-away open the back.

**Completion checks**
- Knee direction is controlled (you can keep knees away enough to progress).
- Chest-to-chest pressure is stable enough to force predictable turning reactions.

**System tags**
- Chest-to-chest pin; Knee-direction race; Reaction forcing; Pass-to-back conversion

### 4.10 Kata gatame backtake (transcript: “kagatame/karagatame”)
**Key concepts**
- “Kagatame” is used as a controlling configuration that creates delayed reactions: the opponent can’t explode immediately because of the elbow/upper-body positioning.
- Key finishing objective: ear/shoulder positioning (ear behind shoulder / “ear to back” / ear glued to rear deltoid) to establish chest-to-back contact and control.
- Transition described: from kagatame control, you can slide knee up, then transfer into a back head-and-arm style connection and take the back.

**Position–structure**
- From top pressure -> establish kagatame control with ear/shoulder alignment -> lock hands / open elbow -> slide knee up -> transfer to back-head-and-arm -> back take.

**Decision rules (IF/THEN)**
- IF you achieve kagatame control -> THEN use the delayed-reaction window to move between legs/hips and build the back lane.
- IF they try to force you back into chest-to-chest -> THEN keep the ear/shoulder alignment and continue building chest-to-back.
- IF kagatame isn’t producing movement -> THEN threaten strangle/pummel-type follow-ups to force reaction, then convert (do not stall).

**Failure signatures + fixes**
- You “lose kagatame, lose the back” -> fix by prioritizing maintaining the kagatame control while you move (don’t abandon control to chase).
- You try to step up/over but feel blocked by their structure -> fix by adjusting elbow/hip control first, then stepping.

**Completion checks**
- Chest-to-back contact is established (ear positioning + upper-body control) before you chase hooks.
- You can transition from kagatame into back-head-and-arm without losing hips/control.

**System tags**
- Kagatame control; Delayed reaction window; Ear-to-back alignment; Control-before-transition; Backtake bridge

### 4.11 Back head-and-arm backtake
**Key concepts**
- Back head-and-arm appears as a repeated stabilization/finish connector once chest-to-back contact is being formed.
- Head/arm connection is used to keep them from turning/turtling freely while you insert hooks or finish the back.

**Position–structure**
- Chest-to-back contact -> establish head-and-arm style connection -> insert hook(s) / stabilize back control.

**Decision rules (IF/THEN)**
- IF you have chest-to-back contact but hooks are not yet in -> THEN use head-and-arm control to stabilize and prevent rotation.
- IF they attempt to get up/turtle during transition -> THEN clamp and use the head-and-arm control to keep their back from escaping.
- IF the head-and-arm connection is slipping -> THEN re-close the head line immediately before advancing.

**Failure signatures + fixes**
- You chase hooks and lose upper-body control -> fix by prioritizing the head-and-arm stabilizer before hook insertion.
- Their rotation accelerates during transition -> fix by tightening head/arm control and re-centering your connection.

**Completion checks**
- Head-and-arm control is secure enough that they cannot freely rotate to face you.
- You can insert a hook while maintaining chest-to-back connection.

**System tags**
- Head-and-arm stabilizer; Rotation suppression; Connection-before-hooks; Back control entry

### 4.12 Backtakes from inversion
**Key concepts**
- Inversion response is addressed explicitly from outside ashi: when they invert, you must manage ankle posts/pummels and keep your own legs safe.
- “Pommel first” + post on ankle is described as a repeated response pattern (recreate the same entries you used earlier from safer alignment).
- Practical safety detail appears: crossing ankles to be safe before continuing pummeling/entries.

**Position–structure**
- Opponent inverts from outside ashi -> you post on ankle(s) and pommel -> re-enter prior forward-pressure / rolling families (cross-body ride, rolls, etc.).

**Decision rules (IF/THEN)**
- IF opponent begins inversion -> THEN post on the ankle and begin pummeling to re-orient (don’t let the inversion fully develop unaddressed).
- IF pummeling creates foot exposure risk for you -> THEN cross ankles / stabilize your legs before continuing.
- IF inversion forces you back and away -> THEN re-establish a safer angle first (distance/angle management), then re-enter.

**Failure signatures + fixes**
- You try to chase during inversion without ankle control -> fix by anchoring with an ankle post first.
- Your pummel exposes your right leg/foot -> fix by stabilizing (cross ankles) before continuing.

**Completion checks**
- You can stop/redirect the inversion enough to return to your forward-pressure decision tree.
- Your legs remain safe while you re-enter (no self-exposure during pummel).

**System tags**
- Inversion response; Ankle post; Pommel-first rule; Self-safety; Re-entry to prior families

### 4.13 V-grip and scoot
**Key concepts**
- V-grip + scoot is presented as a conversion tool when the opponent is digging for a heel (heel-seeking creates an opening to move around).
- The sequence is described as a match-proven pattern: far-trap style grip -> V-grip -> scoot around to improve angle/control.

**Position–structure**
- Opponent digs for heel -> you secure V-grip control -> scoot around the hip line -> establish improved pin/back lane.

**Decision rules (IF/THEN)**
- IF they are digging for your heel -> THEN V-grip + scoot becomes high-percentage (their hands/attention create the opening).
- IF they turn toward you during the scoot -> THEN they get pinned; IF they turn away -> THEN back exposure opens (sequence feeds the same dilemma).
- IF you can’t advance with your current grip -> THEN upgrade to the V-grip and move your body (don’t stay static).

**Failure signatures + fixes**
- You try to scoot without a strong V-grip -> fix by securing the grip first; scoot is downstream of control.
- They abandon heel-dig and re-square mid-move -> fix by converting immediately into pin pressure (don’t chase the heel hand-fight).

**Completion checks**
- V-grip is secure enough that your scoot produces position change (not slip).
- You end in a clear lane: pin (turn-toward) or back exposure (turn-away).

**System tags**
- V-grip control; Scoot-around conversion; Heel-dig trigger; Reaction-led finish

### 4.14 Half roll / full roll to back
**Key concepts**
- Roll families are repeatedly used as converters out of outside ashi exchanges and heel-hook threats:
  - half roll (belly-down containment),
  - full roll (re-orient and come through to new top alignment).
- Rolling is framed as a controlled re-orientation into prior known top positions (e.g., cross-body ride roll) rather than freestyle scrambling.

**Position–structure**
- Heel hook / leg threat appears -> choose half roll or full roll -> land in cross-body ride / top alignment -> progress to pass/back lanes.

**Decision rules (IF/THEN)**
- IF opponent attacks heel hook from the outside ashi exchange -> THEN half roll belly-down is used as a defensive/positional converter.
- IF the roll can land you into a known top alignment (cross-body ride roll) -> THEN roll through and immediately continue.
- IF you roll but don’t land in structure -> THEN treat it as incomplete and re-stabilize before advancing.

**Failure signatures + fixes**
- You roll but end up neutral -> fix by linking the roll to a specific landing (cross-body ride/body lock) and holding that landing.
- You focus on grips over body position during roll -> fix by prioritizing getting to the position first; grips are secondary once structure exists.

**Completion checks**
- The roll lands in a recognizable, stable top position.
- You can immediately progress into pin/back work from the landing.

**System tags**
- Roll-family converter; Half vs full roll; Structured landing; Cross-body ride roll; Defensive-to-offensive bridge

### 4.15 Build to standing (recap)
**Key concepts**
- A “heist” style structure is referenced: heisting foot, post foot, post hand as the base configuration for coming up without losing control.
- Standing build is treated as a method to keep pressure forward and continue the pass/back dilemma rather than remaining static in the leg exchange.

**Position–structure**
- From outside ashi forward phase -> establish posts (foot/hand) -> come up -> continue pressure into pin/back lanes.

**Decision rules (IF/THEN)**
- IF you have the post structure (heisting foot + post foot + post hand) -> THEN you can safely build height and continue pressuring.
- IF posts are not set -> THEN do not commit to standing; build the base first.
- IF building height causes the opponent to turn -> THEN branch immediately (toward = pin/pass, away = back).

**Failure signatures + fixes**
- You build without base and get tipped/reset -> fix by setting the three-post structure before height.
- You stand and separate -> fix by closing distance immediately into a control lane (pin/attachment).

**Completion checks**
- Base is established before rising.
- You reach height with continuous control and immediate lane selection.

**System tags**
- Heist structure; Post-based rise; Stand-to-convert; Continuous pressure

### 4.16 Clearing the knee from Top Ushiro (transcript: “Tapo Shiro”)
**Key concepts**
- The segment is explicitly introduced as “Clearing the knee from Tapo Shiro,” and is described as a short clip that is logically part of the broader “Tapo Shiro” section.
- Key problem statement: if the opponent gets your chest flat and sits back on your hips while they’re belly-down, the position deteriorates (“turns into a disaster”).
- Immediate priorities stated: hide your foot; immediately pull the leg out; you need a “drive leg” to create the extraction and avoid being stuck.

**Position–structure**
- Start state: opponent moving belly-down with hip weight back onto you; your chest getting flattened; your knee trapped inside their knee line.
- Objective: invert/extract the knee, free the leg line, then return to a safer top/control lane.

**Decision rules (IF/THEN)**
- IF you feel chest getting plastered flat and they sit back on hips -> THEN treat it as emergency: hide foot and begin extraction immediately.
- IF you lack a drive leg -> THEN you must first create it (re-position legs) before attempting to extract; otherwise you get stuck.
- IF you can invert the knee (left knee inversion described) -> THEN you can begin extracting the knee and freeing the leg line.

**Failure signatures + fixes**
- You delay and allow full hip-weight sitback -> fix by initiating extraction as soon as belly-down + sitback begins (don’t wait).
- You try to extract with no drive leg -> fix by first pulling leg out / creating a drive base, then invert/extract.
- Foot is exposed during the scramble -> fix by prioritizing foot hiding before any larger movement.

**Completion checks**
- Knee is freed from their knee line (leg line cleared).
- You are no longer chest-flat under their hip weight (you have regained posture/angle enough to continue).

**System tags**
- Knee-line extraction; Belly-down emergency; Foot hiding; Drive-leg requirement; “Tapo Shiro” (Top Ushiro) clip

### 4.17 Heel hooks to backtakes / passing
**Key concepts**
- Heel hook threats are treated as triggers for positional conversion (rolling families + re-entry to cross-body ride/body lock), not only as submission exchanges.
- Safety emphasis returns at the end: when the exchange becomes unstable, the first instruction is to hide the foot and remove the leg from danger before continuing.

**Position–structure**
- Opponent initiates heel hook threat -> you roll/convert (half roll belly-down, cross-body ride roll, full roll options) -> land in top alignment -> continue pass/back lanes.

**Decision rules (IF/THEN)**
- IF heel hook is initiated against you from this outside ashi context -> THEN use the roll/convert family (half roll / cross-body ride roll / full roll) that preserves safety and lands in structure.
- IF the sequence becomes unstable (“disaster” cue) -> THEN hide foot immediately and pull leg out before any other goal.
- IF you regain structure after the roll -> THEN continue the standard branch (turn toward = pin/pass; turn away = back).

**Failure signatures + fixes**
- You focus on grips while your foot is exposed -> fix by prioritizing foot hiding and leg extraction first.
- You roll without a defined landing -> fix by choosing the roll that lands you into a known top position (cross-body ride/body lock).

**Completion checks**
- Foot/heel line is safe after the conversion.
- You land in a stable top/control lane that allows immediate passing/back progression.

**System tags**
- Heel hook trigger; Safety-first rule; Roll-to-structure; Pass/back continuation; Foot hiding
