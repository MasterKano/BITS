# VIEWING NOTES – Gordon Ryan: Pillars of Defense — Leg Locks to Back Takes

## 5.0 Volume 5 — Top Ushiro (transcript: “Tapo Shiro”) + Cross Ashi

### 5.1 Intro to Top Ushiro (transcript: “Tapo Shiro”)
**Key concepts**
- Top ushiro is framed as an “offense from top” hub: build up on top of partner and convert to guard passes and back takes.
- Threat of falling back to leg locks is used to “keep them honest” while you build the top game.

**Position–structure**
- From leg entanglement -> move up to top ushiro -> build height/pressure -> branch into: underhook series, back head-and-arm / backhand-arm series, kimura series, cross-body ride / mount-to-ride conversions.

**Decision rules (IF/THEN)**
- IF you can build to top ushiro with control -> THEN run upper-body attacks while maintaining the “fall back to legs” threat.
- IF there is no meaningful back exposure -> THEN prioritize a pin/passing lane (don’t chase a dead back).

**Failure signatures + fixes**
- You reach top ushiro but stall (no progress) -> reintroduce leg-fallback threat and re-enter an upper-body lane (underhook / backhand-arm / kimura).
- You chase back exposure that isn’t there -> switch to pin/passing (cross-body ride / mount-to-ride).

**Completion checks**
- You can build height without losing control or conceding leg exposure.
- You consistently exit top ushiro into either (a) stable pin/passing, or (b) stable chest-to-back connection.

**System tags**
- Top ushiro hub; Build-to-top; Leg-fallback threat; Pass/back dilemma; Conversion platform

### 5.2 Underhook to guard pass / back take
**Key concepts**
- Punching the underhook is a primary entry from top ushiro; the opponent’s hand position can prevent underhooks.
- Underhook pressure is used to create turning reactions that select pass vs back.

**Position–structure**
- Top ushiro -> punch underhook -> chin/head positioning + pressure -> branch: chest-to-chest pin (pass) or chest-to-upper-back attachment (back).

**Decision rules (IF/THEN)**
- IF underhook is available -> THEN punch it and immediately connect your head/pressure (don’t underhook “loose”).
- IF disciplined hand position blocks underhook -> THEN switch to an alternate lane (backhand-arm / kimura / ride).

**Failure signatures + fixes**
- Underhook attempt is stuffed by hand position -> stop forcing; switch lane immediately.
- You underhook but they “walk away” (distance increases) -> close distance with head/pressure (don’t chase with arms only).

**Completion checks**
- Underhook produces either a real pin (pass lane) or real back connection (not a pummel stalemate).
- You can keep them from freely walking away once underhook is established.

**System tags**
- Underhook gate; Hand-position denial; Head/pressure connection; Branching

---

### 5.3 Back head-and-arm backtakes / guard pass (backhand-arm series)
**Key concepts**
- Backhand-arm vs kimura tradeoff: backhand-arm gives chest-to-back connection faster; kimura gives more ability to move around partner.
- Head-and-arm control quality rule: “smallest circle” around partner’s head/arm; larger circle makes it easier for them to walk head out.
- Chin placement cue: chin over partner’s trap to tighten head/arm control.

**Position–structure**
- Underhook/pressure -> partner walks away -> you fall to backhand-arm / head-and-arm connection where there is already upper-back exposure -> finish via hook insertion or convert to pass lane.

**Decision rules (IF/THEN)**
- IF you have upper-back exposure already -> THEN backhand-arm/head-and-arm is high value (fast chest-to-back connection).
- IF the head starts walking out -> THEN shrink the circle immediately (chin over trap; tighter clamp).

**Failure signatures + fixes**
- Head escapes because your circle is too large -> tighten the clamp (reduce circle; chin over trap).
- You chase hooks while head/arm is loose -> stabilize head-and-arm first, then insert hooks.

**Completion checks**
- Head-and-arm clamp prevents head-walk escape.
- You can insert a hook without losing chest-to-back connection.

**System tags**
- Head-and-arm clamp; Small-circle rule; Chin-over-trap; Connection-before-hooks

### 5.4 Kimura to back
**Key concepts**
- Kimura is used as a control that slows/“labours” explosive turning/turtling, bringing it to your pace.
- Key mechanical emphasis: punch their hand to the far hip while lifting the elbow (elbow lifts toward ceiling, not driven to floor).
- Knee/hip positioning + top hook insertion appears as the back completion pathway once the kimura turn is controlled.

**Position–structure**
- From top ushiro/back exposure context -> lock kimura -> partner tries to explosively turn/turtle -> you punch hand to far hip + lift elbow -> slide knee/position -> insert top hook -> clamp and finish second hook/body triangle.

**Decision rules (IF/THEN)**
- IF partner turns toward explosively to clear kimura -> THEN punch their hand to far hip and lift elbow to slow/kill the explosion.
- IF top hook is inserted -> THEN clamp down and finish second hook/body triangle (don’t float once hook is in).

**Failure signatures + fixes**
- You drive elbow down (wrong vector) and lose control -> correct: lift elbow while punching hand to far hip.
- Their explosive turn beats you -> re-establish far-hip punch + elbow lift before trying to insert hooks.

**Completion checks**
- You can reliably insert the top hook off the kimura control.
- Partner’s turn speed is reduced to “your pace” before you finish.

**System tags**
- Kimura control; Far-hip punch; Elbow-lift vector; Explosive-turn suppression; Hook-first finish

### 5.5 Kimura to guard pass (failed kimura branch)
**Key concepts**
- If kimura does not convert to back (or is being cleared), you convert into a guard pass rather than stalling.
- “Walking” mechanic: opponent clears by walking; your response uses the same mechanics (you walk and reorient), but you must avoid getting stuck on frames.

**Position–structure**
- Lock kimura -> opponent begins clearing by walking head/shoulder line -> you track/walk with them -> switch into a pin/passing lane as the kimura fails.

**Decision rules (IF/THEN)**
- IF partner is clearing the kimura by walking -> THEN walk with them to maintain advantage and re-angle to passing.
- IF passing attempt runs into frames -> THEN adjust angle/position before driving (don’t force into frames).

**Failure signatures + fixes**
- Kimura clear succeeds and you pause -> immediate conversion to pass lane (no reset).
- You come onto frames and stall -> re-angle to remove frames before pressure.

**Completion checks**
- You end in a stable passing configuration after the kimura fails.
- Their frames are not the primary limiter (you’ve re-angled around them).

**System tags**
- Failed-kimura branch; Walk-with-clear; No-reset rule; Frame avoidance

### 5.6 3 quarter mount to cross body ride
**Key concepts**
- From top ushiro, three-quarter mount is used as a bridge into cross-body ride via rolling mechanics.

**Position–structure**
- Top ushiro -> three-quarter mount -> roll/transition -> cross-body ride -> branch to pass/back.

**Decision rules (IF/THEN)**
- IF you enter three-quarter mount -> THEN use it as a controlled bridge into cross-body ride (not as a stall).

**Failure signatures + fixes**
- Transition becomes loose and you lose ride -> re-establish control first, then roll to ride.

**Completion checks**
- Cross-body ride achieved with enough connection to progress to pin/back options.

**System tags**
- Three-quarter mount bridge; Cross-body ride entry; Structured roll

### 5.7 Intro to Cross Ashi
**Key concepts**
- Cross ashi is introduced after outside ashi and top ushiro; key issue stated: the chest-to-back distance is far, so you must “sell” threats to close distance.
- Cross ashi is framed as another platform to build on top and convert to passes/back takes; includes misdirectional backtakes.

**Position–structure**
- Cross ashi orientation creates distance -> you close distance via extraction/come-ups, then run pass/back branches.

**Decision rules (IF/THEN)**
- IF distance to back is large -> THEN you must create believable threats (leg attacks) to force reactions that shorten distance.
- IF they turn away under pressure -> back lane; IF they turn toward -> pass lane.

**Failure signatures + fixes**
- You try to “reach” the back across distance -> first force reactions that reduce distance (threat, then climb).
- You stall in cross ashi without climbing -> initiate extraction/come-up immediately once safety is solved.

**Completion checks**
- You can convert cross ashi into (a) come-up top lane or (b) back exposure via reaction.

**System tags**
- Cross ashi hub; Distance management; Threat-to-climb; Pass/back dilemma

### 5.8 Hiding your foot
**Key concepts**
- Foot safety is emphasized immediately in cross ashi: toe/foot orientation affects heel exposure difficulty.
- Injury note: pointing toes with straight leg under weight can risk MCL; instruction is to avoid that alignment under load.

**Position–structure**
- Cross ashi safety layer: foot is tucked/hidden while you prepare extraction and come-up.

**Decision rules (IF/THEN)**
- IF your toes point toward partner (cross ashi context) -> THEN heel exposure is harder for them (safety benefit).
- IF there is weight on your leg AND your knee is straight with toes pointed -> THEN do not force; adjust to remove injury risk before continuing.
- IF you begin coming up -> THEN tuck/hide foot as you move (don’t “leave it behind”).

**Failure signatures + fixes**
- Heel begins to expose as you move -> re-tuck foot immediately before advancing.
- You feel knee stress under load -> change alignment before continuing (don’t push through).

**Completion checks**
- Heel line is protected while you initiate extraction/come-up.
- You can move without creating knee injury mechanics.

**System tags**
- Foot hiding; Heel exposure prevention; Toe orientation; Injury-risk gate (MCL)

### 5.9 Primary leg extraction to body lock / backtake
**Key concepts**
- Extraction is linked directly to coming up into body lock on top.
- Partner’s typical reactions described as two common categories; your extraction must account for their attempt to come up/top.

**Position–structure**
- Cross ashi -> extract primary leg (while hiding foot) -> post/grip on far knee -> come up -> body lock / top lane -> branch to pass/back.

**Decision rules (IF/THEN)**
- IF partner begins coming up as you extract -> THEN take a posting grip (on far knee) to block their rise while you come up.
- IF your knee line is too far past your centre line during the come-up -> THEN correct before committing upward (prevents them re-entering/coming up clean).

**Failure signatures + fixes**
- You start coming up and they come up with you -> block with post on far knee and re-angle.
- You come up with leg too far past centre line -> re-center before continuing.

**Completion checks**
- Primary leg is extracted without exposing heel line.
- You come up into a top control lane (body lock / pin / back connection), not neutral.

**System tags**
- Primary extraction; Post on far knee; Centre-line alignment; Come-up to body lock

### 5.10 Backstep from primary leg extraction
**Key concepts**
- After extraction/come-up, backstepping is used as a continuation that can feed camping stabilization when the opponent locks or re-enters.
- Camping is explicitly named as the stabilization layer when the opponent locks the entanglement during the backstep sequence.

**Position–structure**
- Extraction -> come-up -> backstep -> (if they lock) camping -> continue pass/back branches.

**Decision rules (IF/THEN)**
- IF you backstep and they lock the entanglement -> THEN transition to camping (stabilize) rather than forcing.
- IF they try to get up during the sequence -> THEN insert hook and progress (don’t allow free rise).

**Failure signatures + fixes**
- You backstep loose and lose stability -> convert to camping immediately and rebuild control.
- Opponent rises because you didn’t control the get-up -> insert hook/control as the rise begins.

**Completion checks**
- Backstep yields either (a) stable camping, or (b) immediate control progression (hook/upper-body).
- No scramble/re-guard occurs during the backstep phase.

**System tags**
- Backstep continuation; Camping stabilization; Anti-get-up hook; No-reset rule

### 5.11 Building to a knee
**Key concepts**
- Two build-to-knee methods are presented: one works better vs naive opponents; the other is framed as necessary vs the absolute best.
- Inside bicep tie can make certain grips harder; over-tie option is used to pull in and prevent opponent extending out of range.
- Safety reminder persists: opponent hunting heel during your build changes your priorities.

**Position–structure**
- From cross ashi / postures -> establish ties (inside bicep tie / over-tie) -> pull in -> build to knee -> prevent scooping into far leg -> continue to top lane.

**Decision rules (IF/THEN)**
- IF opponent is heel-hunting as you build -> THEN prioritize heel safety and control before committing height.
- IF inside bicep tie prevents your preferred control -> THEN switch to over-tie and pull yourself in.
- IF space is created -> THEN post to prevent them scooping into far leg and re-entering legs.

**Failure signatures + fixes**
- You build and they extend out of range -> switch to over-tie and pull in.
- They scoop into far leg during your build -> post/structure to deny the scoop before continuing.

**Completion checks**
- You reach a knee with control (they can’t freely extend away or scoop legs).
- Heel line remains safe throughout the build.

**System tags**
- Build-to-knee; Over-tie vs inside bicep tie; Anti-extension; Anti-scoop; Heel-safety gate

### 5.12 Beating a cross lat post (transcript: “cross lat post”)
**Key concepts**
- Cross lat post is stated as a common strong defense: “a good person is always going to have a cross lat.”
- Key dilemma stated: the bottom hand can stop stepping over the knee OR stop kata gatame (“kala thai” transcript token), but it can’t do both.
- Tactical sequence: force them to choose; when they clear one threat, the other opens.

**Position–structure**
- You try to come forward -> cross-lat post blocks -> you introduce kata gatame threat / stepping-over-knee threat -> they commit bottom hand -> you take the other lane.

**Decision rules (IF/THEN)**
- IF bottom hand is posting to stop step-over-knee -> THEN bring kata gatame threat in (forces clear).
- IF they clear kata gatame -> THEN step over the knee while bottom hand is no longer available to stop it.
- IF they try to do “step-step-step” stop with bottom hand -> THEN use the other threat to remove that hand.

**Failure signatures + fixes**
- You keep driving into a post -> instead, force the bottom-hand choice (they cannot cover both).
- You delay after they clear -> step over immediately while the defense is committed.

**Completion checks**
- Cross-lat post is functionally neutralized (no longer preventing forward progress).
- You can step over knee or lock kata gatame without being stalled by the post.

**System tags**
- Cross-lat post; Two-threat hand split; Kata gatame (“kala thai”); Step-over-knee lane; Defense overload

### 5.13 Passing / backtake dilemma (cross ashi continuation)
**Key concepts**
- Once you can come forward, the same dilemma loop is enforced: turn-toward produces pin/pass; turn-away produces back exposure.
- Hook insertion is described as delayed when controlling far knee to chest; timing matters (control first, then hook).

**Position–structure**
- Cross ashi -> come forward -> control far knee/shoulder line -> insert hook(s) or step to pass depending on reaction.

**Decision rules (IF/THEN)**
- IF they turn away under forward pressure -> THEN attach and insert hook(s) as control is established.
- IF they turn toward and frame -> THEN stay chest-to-chest and progress passing.

**Failure signatures + fixes**
- You rush hook insertion before the control is set -> stabilize knee/shoulder control first, then insert.
- You chase back when turn-toward is present -> accept pin/pass immediately.

**Completion checks**
- Back lane: at least one hook inserted off stable upper-body control, not a reach.
- Pass lane: chest-to-chest control with real step-over progress.

**System tags**
- Dilemma enforcement; Hook timing; Pin-first; Reaction-led branching

### 5.14 Roll to cross body ride
**Key concepts**
- Rolling into cross-body ride is used as a structured landing when advancing forward (especially when upper-body control is challenged).
- Cross-body ride is treated as a stable platform to continue pass/back work.

**Position–structure**
- Forward progress / pressure -> roll -> cross-body ride landing -> branch to pass or back.

**Decision rules (IF/THEN)**
- IF forward pressure is met with off-balancing/blocks -> THEN roll into cross-body ride to land in structure rather than forcing.
- IF cross-body ride is achieved -> THEN continue the standard lane selection (turn away = back, turn toward = pass).

**Failure signatures + fixes**
- You roll without a defined landing -> select cross-body ride as the target and hold it on landing.
- You land but don’t connect -> re-establish upper-body grip higher (shoulder/torso) before advancing.

**Completion checks**
- Roll lands you in cross-body ride with enough connection to control turning.
- You can progress to pin/back without reset.

**System tags**
- Roll-to-ride converter; Structured landing; Cross-body ride base; No-reset continuation

### 5.15 Backtakes from inversion
**Key concepts**
- Inversion response includes immediately posting on the partner’s ankle and re-entering via pummeling.
- Safety note appears: cross ankles to keep your own leg safe before continuing pummeling/re-entry.

**Position–structure**
- Partner inverts -> you post ankle -> pummel to re-orient -> re-enter prior sequences (ride / forward progress) while maintaining your own leg safety.

**Decision rules (IF/THEN)**
- IF inversion begins -> THEN post on ankle immediately and begin pummeling to re-orient.
- IF pummeling exposes your leg -> THEN cross ankles / stabilize your leg safety before continuing.

**Failure signatures + fixes**
- You chase without ankle post -> establish ankle post first.
- Your leg becomes exposed during pummel -> cross ankles and reset safety before re-entry.

**Completion checks**
- Inversion is stopped/redirected enough to return to your forward-pressure decision tree.
- Your legs remain safe throughout the inversion exchange.

**System tags**
- Inversion response; Ankle post; Pommel-first; Self-safety (cross ankles); Re-entry

### 5.16 Half roll to backstep
**Key concepts**
- Half roll is explicitly associated with going belly-down; it is used to hide heel and maintain safety during transitions.
- Belly-down phase is linked to re-orienting into backstep/camping continuations rather than stopping.

**Position–structure**
- Heel/leg threat appears -> half roll to belly-down -> hide heel -> transition into backstep / camping continuation.

**Decision rules (IF/THEN)**
- IF you need to protect heel during transition -> THEN half roll belly-down and hide heel before continuing.
- IF belly-down is established -> THEN link it to a backstep/camping continuation (don’t freeze).

**Failure signatures + fixes**
- You belly-down but don’t progress -> connect into backstep/camping immediately.
- Heel becomes available during roll -> re-hide heel first (priority over position gain).

**Completion checks**
- Belly-down phase keeps heel safe.
- You can exit belly-down into a stable top/stabilization lane.

**System tags**
- Half roll (belly-down); Heel hiding; Backstep linkage; Safety-first continuation

### 5.17 Misdirectional backtakes / passes
**Key concepts**
- Misdirection is framed as a reliable method once you’re good at the base actions: threaten one direction to force defense, then take the opposite lane.
- Rolling through is referenced as a mechanism to create the misdirectional switch.

**Position–structure**
- Establish baseline pressure/controls -> present one lane (back or pass) -> opponent commits -> roll/switch -> take the other lane.

**Decision rules (IF/THEN)**
- IF defense commits hard to your initial lane -> THEN switch lanes immediately (misdirection is timing-dependent).
- IF you can roll through into a better angle -> THEN use it to complete the opposite lane.

**Failure signatures + fixes**
- You attempt misdirection without forcing a commitment -> build the initial threat until defense commits, then switch.
- Switch happens too slowly -> tighten timing: switch at the moment they commit, not after.

**Completion checks**
- Defense commitment is visibly “stuck” to the initial threat as you take the other lane.
- You land in a stable lane (pin/back), not a scramble.

**System tags**
- Misdirection; Commitment timing; Roll-through switch; Lane swapping

### 5.18 Moving back to Top Ushiro (cross ashi -> top ushiro transfer)
**Key concepts**
- Transfer concept is emphasized: using cross ashi situations to transition into a shoelace-style/top-ushiro control (transcript references “transferring from cross ashi … into a shoelace”).
- This transfer is framed as a match-proven success pattern (explicitly referenced as a big part of success in major competitions).

**Position–structure**
- Cross ashi -> transfer/upgrade to top ushiro/shoelace control -> re-enter top offense (front tight waist / body lock / hook insertions).

**Decision rules (IF/THEN)**
- IF you can upgrade from cross ashi into a top-ushiro control -> THEN do it (use it to shorten distance and restart top offense).
- IF partner reacts to leg threats -> THEN use that reaction to enter the top control (threat-to-transfer).

**Failure signatures + fixes**
- You stay at long distance in cross ashi -> transfer into top ushiro/shoelace to close distance and progress.
- Transfer attempt is loose -> secure the control structure first, then move.

**Completion checks**
- Transfer results in a recognizable top ushiro/shoelace control.
- You can immediately begin top offense (tight waist / body lock / hook entries).

**System tags**
- Hub transfer; Cross ashi -> top ushiro; Shoelace control; Threat-to-upgrade

### 5.19 Body lock to back
**Key concepts**
- Body lock entry is linked to (a) extending partner’s leg out and away, and (b) using front tight waist as a bridge to come up and body lock.
- Threat of leg locks is used to open the pathway; once leg comes through, front tight waist can be shot.

**Position–structure**
- Hand to far hip + extend leg out/away -> insert hook as they come up -> shoot front tight waist -> come up -> body lock -> back progression.

**Decision rules (IF/THEN)**
- IF you can extend partner’s leg out and away -> THEN you create the angle to come up and start entering the top lane.
- IF your leg “comes through” and the opening appears -> THEN shoot front tight waist immediately.
- IF front tight waist is secured -> THEN come up to body lock (don’t linger mid-transition).

**Failure signatures + fixes**
- You miss the timing to shoot tight waist -> take it “the second” the opening appears (timing cue).
- You come up without upper-body connection -> establish front tight waist/body lock before advancing.

**Completion checks**
- Front tight waist is established before you commit to the body lock rise.
- You can progress to a hook insertion / back-control phase off the body lock.

**System tags**
- Front tight waist; Body lock entry; Far-hip hand; Leg extension angle; Timing cue

### 5.20 Heel hook to back
**Key concepts**
- Offense is framed as: hide heel, come up with pressure, form two-on-one on partner’s leg, then use pendulum/hip entries to reach the back.
- “Pendulum” is explicitly referenced as the movement that brings you up and to partner’s hip, enabling back exposure and entry into turtle/back game.

**Position–structure**
- Pressure over partner -> two-on-one on leg -> pull up -> knee positioned in front of partner -> pendulum -> reach partner’s hip -> come up -> enter turtle/back game -> two hooks.

**Decision rules (IF/THEN)**
- IF you secure two-on-one on the leg -> THEN pull yourself up and bring knee in front of partner (sets pendulum path).
- IF pendulum brings you to partner’s hip -> THEN come up immediately and enter turtle/back attack lane.

**Failure signatures + fixes**
- You try to pendulum without controlling the leg (no two-on-one) -> secure two-on-one first.
- You arrive at hip but delay the come-up -> come up immediately while the angle is won.

**Completion checks**
- Two-on-one established and maintained through the pendulum.
- You reach partner’s hip and can enter back/turtle game to finish with two hooks.

**System tags**
- Heel hook offense; Two-on-one leg control; Pendulum entry; Hip access; Turtle-to-back finish
