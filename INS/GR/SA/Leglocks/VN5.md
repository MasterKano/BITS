### 5.1 Cross Ashi family taxonomy (Outside Ashi vs Inside Sankaku vs Criss-Cross) (00:08–06:39)

- Objective
  - Select the correct **cross-ashi “look”** to convert cross-alignment into: **(a) mobility denial**, **(b) reliable exposure**, **(c) finish-ready structure**.

- Key concepts
  - Cross Ashi = **high-finishing alignment**; often “powerful but loose” unless secondary-leg/hip mobility is addressed.
  - Three distinct tools (not synonyms):
    - **Outside Ashi (cross-family look)**: finish-forward, lower clamp on secondary leg.
    - **Inside Sankaku (triangle)**: clamp/immobilize; reduces turn/stand/spin.
    - **Criss-Cross**: control + transition platform; organizes the entanglement to manage turning while staying ready to feed other looks.
  - Primary escape engine is often the **secondary/free leg** + hip rotation.

- Position–structure (control anatomy)
  - Baseline cross ashi: cross-rotation / breaking alignment is present but must be **supported by rotation denial**.
  - Inside sankaku: triangle clamp = **hip/rotation limiter** + secondary leg management.
  - Criss-cross: cross entanglement variant = **turning control** + **transition readiness**.

- Gates (must be true before escalating)
  - You can explicitly identify which “look” you are in (Outside vs Sankaku vs Criss-Cross).
  - Opponent’s turning/standing/spinning is measurably constrained (not theoretical).

- Decision rules (IF/THEN)
  - IF opponent’s escapes are powered by the free/secondary leg, THEN upgrade to **triangle (inside sankaku)** or **criss-cross** (address the second leg).
  - IF you need maximum immobilization before chasing exposure, THEN build **inside sankaku**.
  - IF you want a stable cross-family platform that can feed transitions without losing connection, THEN build **criss-cross**.
  - IF cross ashi feels “strong but loose,” THEN **tighten structure first** (triangle/criss-cross) before insisting on heel exposure.

- Failure-first diagnosis
  - Opponent freely turns/stands → you are missing **secondary-leg control / rotation constraint** → build triangle/criss-cross.
  - You feel “finish power” but lose position when you chase heel → you are skipping **control gates** → re-stabilize first.

- System tags: cross-ashi, ashi-family, inside-sankaku, criss-cross, control-before-finish, secondary-leg-control


### 5.2 Criss-Cross as a control platform (triangle logic + transition readiness) (06:39–16:40)

- Objective
  - Use **criss-cross** to hold cross-ashi alignment under turning pressure, then transition deliberately to the next exposure/finish structure.

- Key concepts
  - Criss-cross is **control-first**: its job is to keep the opponent from turning out while you select the next step.
  - Triangle (inside sankaku) is the recurring “tighten the clamp” solution when turning persists.
  - Priority order: **stabilize → expose → finish** (do not invert that order).

- Controls (what holds it together)
  - Maintain entanglement integrity while the opponent tries to rotate.
  - Treat “heel chase” as secondary; criss-cross is primarily about **denying the turn** long enough to escalate.

- Decision rules (IF/THEN)
  - IF opponent rotates to escape cross ashi, THEN use **criss-cross organization** to re-stabilize (deny turn) before advancing.
  - IF opponent’s turning persists despite criss-cross, THEN **triangle clamp** (inside sankaku) to hard-limit hip rotation.
  - IF stability exists but exposure does not, THEN **hold structure and transition intentionally** (no foot chasing).

- Failure-first diagnosis
  - You lose structure while reaching for foot/heel → you abandoned the control job → rebuild criss-cross, then re-earn exposure.
  - Opponent still turns freely → criss-cross isn’t tight enough → clamp with triangle.

- Completion checks
  - You can hold criss-cross while opponent actively attempts turning escapes.
  - You can transition to triangle without losing the knee-line/hip relationship.

- System tags: criss-cross, inside-sankaku, rotation-control, stabilize-then-escalate, transition-readiness


### 5.3 Reverse figure-four Achilles as tether → heel exposure (cowboy drill + belly-up/belly-down finish) (16:40–25:35)

- Objective
  - Use reverse figure-four Achilles as a **tether** to force predictable reactions that unlock **heel exposure**, then finish via belly-up or belly-down orientation.

- Key concepts
  - Reverse figure-four Achilles = **control handle** first; “finish” second.
  - Dilemma logic: Achilles pressure forces movement; movement reveals heel/exposure.
  - Cowboy drill = training method for **staying connected through rotation**.
  - Belly-up vs belly-down is an orientation selection after exposure exists (not a debate before it exists).

- Gates
  - The tether must hold through rotation (connection survives the turn).
  - Exposure improves as a result of the reaction (pressure must change the geometry, not just cause pain).

- Primary line (sequence intent)
  - Establish reverse figure-four tether → apply pressure to force reaction → follow rotation without disconnecting → convert to heel exposure → select finish orientation (belly-up or belly-down) based on angle.

- Decision rules (IF/THEN)
  - IF heel is hidden / exposure is poor, THEN apply **reverse figure-four Achilles** to force the reaction.
  - IF opponent rotates to relieve Achilles pressure, THEN **follow the turn** and enter exposure (do not reset).
  - IF belly-up finishing angle is poor/unstable, THEN convert to **belly-down** when rotation/angle supports it.

- Failure-first diagnosis
  - Achilles becomes “pain only” and control degrades → you treated it as squeeze-only → reframe as tether; rebuild structure first.
  - Opponent turns and you lose attachment → your follow mechanics failed → drill the rotation-follow (cowboy logic).

- Drills
  - Cowboy drill: partner rotates; you maintain attachment, finish alignment, and continuity.

- System tags: reverse-figure-four, achilles-as-tether, heel-exposure, cowboy-drill, belly-up, belly-down


### 5.4 Leg lace finishing (immobilize legs → expose heel → finish) (25:35–34:35)

- Objective
  - Use leg lace to **bind both legs** (two-leg control), reduce stand/turn exits, and create stable exposure/finishing windows.

- Key concepts
  - Leg lace is primarily **immobilization** that makes exposure reliable.
  - Finish comes after the opponent’s legs/hips cannot freely unwind.

- Controls
  - Binding control must prevent independent leg movement (escape engine removal).
  - Lace is a *position* (sustained), not a momentary catch.

- Decision rules (IF/THEN)
  - IF opponent escapes via turning/standing, THEN build **leg lace** to limit hip/leg independence.
  - IF control is tight and exposure opens, THEN finish.
  - IF exposure does not open, THEN **adjust/tighten lace** rather than abandoning it.

- Failure-first diagnosis
  - Lace is loose and opponent still rotates/stands → re-tighten binding before escalating.
  - You attempt finishing before immobilization is real → you skipped the control gate → re-establish lace dominance.

- Completion checks
  - Opponent cannot freely separate legs to generate mobility.
  - You can hold lace long enough to choose the next finish route deliberately.

- System tags: leg-lace, two-leg-control, immobilization, finish-after-control, cross-family


### 5.5 Half Dracula (control upgrade off lace / cross family) (34:35–38:05)

- Objective
  - Upgrade from leg lace toward higher immobilization by trapping the “free leg” so rotation/stand rescues collapse.

- Key concepts
  - Half Dracula = next-level **two-leg trapping** layered on existing leg control.
  - Primary value: deny the opponent’s ability to use the secondary leg to rescue, stand, or unwind.

- Gates
  - You do not sacrifice primary control to “reach” the trap.
  - The upgrade must measurably reduce free-leg contribution.

- Decision rules (IF/THEN)
  - IF lace exists but opponent still has meaningful free-leg mobility, THEN upgrade to **half dracula**.
  - IF escapes are driven by the free leg, THEN prioritize trapping that leg into the dracula structure.

- Failure-first diagnosis
  - Half dracula does not reduce movement → trap quality is insufficient → rebuild so both legs cannot move independently.
  - Connection loss during upgrade → you upgraded too greedily → stage the upgrade; preserve primary control.

- Completion checks
  - Free-leg rescue/stand options are clearly reduced.
  - You can hold control while preparing stronger trapping or exposure.

- System tags: half-dracula, two-leg-trap, immobilization-upgrade, leg-lace-to-dracula


### 5.6 Double Dracula + clamp logic (increase immobilization → create predictable exposure) (38:05–43:20)

- Objective
  - Lock both legs into higher-order immobilization so rotation is constrained and exposure becomes predictable.

- Key concepts
  - Double Dracula = stronger “both legs trapped” condition; reduces leg independence further than half dracula.
  - Triangle/clamp logic is a recurring rotation-denial tool: immobilize first, then expose/finish.

- Controls
  - Trapping must remove the opponent’s ability to reconfigure legs to regain mobility.
  - Clamp logic is used when turning persists.

- Decision rules (IF/THEN)
  - IF half dracula does not shut down leg rescues/rotation, THEN upgrade to **double dracula**.
  - IF opponent continues turning to hide exposure, THEN add **clamp logic** (triangle-style constraint) to deny rotation.

- Failure-first diagnosis
  - Opponent still rotates freely → immobilization is incomplete → deepen trap + add clamp before advancing.
  - You treat double dracula as a finish without exposure → keep it as immobilization until the finish is structurally supported.

- Completion checks
  - Neither leg can move independently in a way that frees the entanglement.
  - Turning is constrained enough that exposure appears without frantic chasing.

- System tags: double-dracula, immobilization, clamp-logic, rotation-constraint, control-to-exposure


### 5.7 Stretch Ashi as a response/transition (re-align without disconnecting) (43:20–50:05)

- Objective
  - Re-center the entanglement when current trapping no longer yields exposure, while keeping attachment and re-opening attack lines.

- Key concepts
  - Stretch ashi is a **re-alignment platform** inside the cross-family landscape.
  - The job is continuity: maintain control while changing alignment to solve the opponent’s turning line.

- Gates
  - Transition must preserve connection (no clean disengagement window).
  - The new alignment must produce a clearer re-attack path than the prior look.

- Decision rules (IF/THEN)
  - IF current trap/control stalls exposure, THEN transition to **stretch ashi** to re-align.
  - IF opponent turns to escape during the transition, THEN maintain attachment and re-apply rotation constraint immediately after switching.

- Failure-first diagnosis
  - Disconnection during transition → you prioritized alignment over attachment → stay connected first, then re-center.
  - Opponent regains turning freedom → you failed to re-apply constraint post-transition → re-clamp immediately.

- Completion checks
  - You can enter stretch ashi without giving a disengagement reset.
  - The new alignment yields a more direct re-attack/exposure line.

- System tags: stretch-ashi, transition, re-center, maintain-connection, re-attack


### 5.8 Heist to top (Topo Shiro) vs heel exposure dilemma (50:05–57:07)

- Objective
  - Force a binary choice: opponent cannot simultaneously deny the **heist-to-top** and protect the **heel**.

- Key concepts
  - Dilemma: defend the heist → expose heel; defend heel → allow heist.
  - “Topo shiro” = top-control outcome produced by the heist pathway.
  - The system blends lower-body threat with top/upper-body advancement to overload defense.

- Gates
  - You must maintain leg control during the heist attempt (threat stays live).
  - Opponent must be forced into a hip-commitment (a real choice).

- Decision rules (IF/THEN)
  - IF opponent falls to one hip to deny heist, THEN the opposite-side geometry yields **heel exposure**.
  - IF opponent falls to the other hip to protect heel, THEN they concede **heist → top**.
  - IF opponent locks/braces to stop leg threats, THEN threaten upper-body progress/passing, and be ready to fall back into legs when they unlock.

- Failure-first diagnosis
  - Heist gets stopped without exposure → dilemma not tight → force a clearer hip commitment.
  - You commit to only one layer (legs-only or top-only) → keep both threats live until they choose.

- Drills/Games
  - Dilemma game: partner starts locked; you alternate (a) heel exposure threat and (b) heist-to-top, scoring on forcing a clean “hip choice.”

- System tags: heist, topo-shiro, upper-lower-dilemma, decision-overload, come-on-top


### 5.9 Fall-across entry → toe hold ↔ knee bar dilemma (wrap-up decision layer) (57:07–59:53)

- Objective
  - Enter a controlled fall-across lane that reliably produces a two-threat dilemma: toe hold vs knee bar.

- Key concepts
  - Fall-across is a method to route into dilemma space, not a scramble.
  - Scoop-grip control initiates the fall across and stabilizes entry.
  - Dilemma framing: defending one line creates the other.

- Gates
  - Scoop-grip + leg control must be secured before falling.
  - You must be able to present both threats without losing the leg.

- Decision rules (IF/THEN)
  - IF opponent over-defends toe hold, THEN knee bar conditions appear.
  - IF opponent structures/locks legs defensively, THEN use threat progression to provoke unlock and enter the dilemma.

- Failure-first diagnosis
  - Fall-across without control → opponent escapes → secure initiating control first (scoop grip + leg control) before committing body movement.
  - You treat it as single attack → no forced choice → actively present paired threats.

- Completion checks
  - Entry is controlled (not chaotic) and preserves leg attachment.
  - Opponent is presented with a real two-option defense problem.

- System tags: toe-hold, knee-bar, dilemma, fall-across, scoop-grip, endgame
