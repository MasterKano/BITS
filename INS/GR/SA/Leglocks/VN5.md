# VIEWING NOTES – Systematically Attacking the Legs (Gordon Ryan)

## 4.0 Volume 4

### 4.1 Intro To Toe Holds (0:00)
- Key concepts
  - Toe hold is primarily framed as a fast-acting breaking threat that can precede heel hook completion because the hands apply pressure faster than full-body heel hook mechanics.
  - Toe hold is framed as generally less “catastrophic” than a heel hook, but still a legitimate break threat that reliably forces defensive reactions.
  - Primary strategic use: use toe hold threat to force predictable movement (reaction), then transition to higher-percentage finishes (often heel hooks) when the defender moves.
- Position–structure
  - Toe hold is treated as “hand-speed dominant”: the finish can come on before the defender’s body/hip rotation catches up, so the defender often must move early.
- Key cues
  - Expect the defender to “feel it early” and move; treat that early movement as the value, not a reason to chase a stubborn toe hold finish.
- System tags
  - toe-hold, reaction-attacks, hand-speed, threat-chaining

### 4.2 Belly Down Exposure (1:55)
- Key concepts
  - Belly-down exposure is presented as a solution when heel exposure is difficult (e.g., outside ashi where heel hook exposure is blocked), using toe hold as the accessible submission line.
  - Exposure priority is securing a deep, tight bite on the foot while preventing the defender from sliding the knee free (knee-line retention is treated as non-negotiable during the exposure phase).
- Position–structure
  - Bite-building sequence emphasized
    - Head position: bring head outside the partner’s foot to improve alignment and prevent shallow biting.
    - False grip: catch the foot/ankle with a false grip as a temporary control to stop the knee from sliding out during posture changes.
    - Chest coverage: cover the partner’s leg with the chest to physically block knee extraction while you re-lock legs and re-set grips.
    - “Chin outside heel” cue is used to deepen the bite and tighten the lock before joining hands.
  - Lock closure emphasis
    - “Close elbows together” is framed as the action that joins the hands and completes the toe hold lock.
- Key cues
  - If the defender can posture and slide the knee out during your belly-down entry, treat it as a structure failure: re-prioritize knee retention (false grip + chest coverage + re-lock) before insisting on the lock.
- System tags
  - belly-down-exposure, toe-hold-bite, false-grip, chest-coverage, knee-line-retention

### 4.3 Belly Up Exposure (6:31)
- Key concepts
  - Belly-up exposure is the alternative when belly-down is not viable (space/pressure constraints), with the attacker creating space and “shoveling hips underneath” to access the foot.
  - Belly-up exposure is presented as a platform that can either finish belly-up or transition belly-down once the foot is exposed and control is established.
- Position–structure
  - Space-making and entry theme
    - Defender may be trying to come forward; attacker must make space first (space is treated as a prerequisite for clean toe access).
    - “Shovel hips underneath” is emphasized as the method to get into the correct relationship to the foot (mirrors outside heel hook style hip-under entry).
  - Exposure mechanics emphasized
    - Catch toes and push toes down into a bent position to start building the lock.
    - The grip is not expected to lock instantly from the floor if the defender is pressuring; the attacker must create enough space to complete the lock.
  - Decision forks described
    - Option A: start belly-up, expose and lock, then transition belly-down to finish if belly-up finishing is constrained.
    - Option B: start belly-up, then use roll-through / repositioning mechanics to fall into outside ashi and finish later (toe hold exposure used to force a leg/hip response that enables transition).
- Key cues
  - If you cannot lock the grip because the defender is driving forward, the fix is not “stronger hands”; the fix is “more space + hips under.”
- System tags
  - belly-up-exposure, space-making, hips-under, toe-control, transition-to-belly-down

### 4.4 Belly Down Finishing Mechanics (9:50)
- Key concepts
  - Belly-down toe hold finishing is explicitly framed as “very similar to a heel hook” in terms of stacking lower-body wedge + upper-body lock tightness.
  - Finish is built by stacking lower-body control (back heel + knee pinch) with upper-body tightness (hands high + chin low), then applying the coupled break.
- Position–structure
  - Lower body stack
    - Strong back-heel engagement (used to pull/anchor and prevent slippage).
    - Strong pinch of the knees (controls the leg line and stabilizes the finish).
  - Upper body stack
    - Hands high = tighter lock (explicit “hand height tightens lock” rule).
    - Chin low = tighter lock (explicit “chin low tightens lock” rule).
  - Break direction (consistent theme)
    - “Toes down toward butt” + “heel up toward chin” framing appears as the coupled force line.
- Key cues
  - If the lock feels loose, diagnose in order: hand height → chin position → knee pinch/back heel, not “squeeze harder.”
- System tags
  - belly-down-finish, hands-high, chin-low, knee-pinch, back-heel, coupled-force

### 4.5 Belly Up Finishing Mechanics (11:59)
- Key concepts
  - Belly-up toe hold finishing uses the same coupled force line but emphasizes wrist roll and leg configuration differences compared to belly-down.
  - “Wrist roll forward” is presented as an added pressure amplifier in the belly-up finish line.
- Position–structure
  - Finish stack emphasized
    - Wrist rolls forward to increase pressure and reinforce toe-crunch direction.
    - Back heel engagement and knee pinch remain core stabilizers.
    - Toes to butt + lifting partner’s heel (via elbow/lock line) are the explicit break actions.
  - Orientation-specific contrasts stated
    - Belly-down: finish is often described as “right here in my elbows” (tight elbow position in the belly-down line).
    - Belly-up: wrist roll and finishing alignment adjustments are highlighted as the differentiator.
- Key cues
  - Belly-up finish still requires the same “tightness stack”; wrist roll is additive, not a substitute for hands-high/chin-low/knee pinch mechanics.
- System tags
  - belly-up-finish, wrist-roll, back-heel, knee-pinch, toes-to-butt, heel-lift

### 4.6 Toe Hold To Heel Hook (15:59)
- Key concepts
  - Toe hold is used as a forcing tool: when both athletes apply pressure, the toe hold threat often forces the defender to move first (“my foot is going to break” reaction), which is exploited to enter heel hook lines.
  - Transfer principle: toe hold creates the reaction window; heel hook capitalizes on the reaction path.
- Position–structure
  - Transfer is framed as existing “in multiple positions” (not one specific entanglement), implying the threat-chaining concept is portable across leg hubs.
- Key cues
  - When the defender’s reaction begins, treat it as the timing trigger to switch attacks; do not wait until the toe hold is fully maxed to “prove” it works.
- System tags
  - toe-hold-to-heelhook, reaction-window, timing-trigger, threat-chaining

### 4.7 Recapturing A Knee (18:47)
- Key concepts
  - “Knee comes out” is treated as a common problem; the system response is to re-establish wedging and reclimb the knee line rather than losing the position.
  - Two re-climb options are contrasted as speed vs robustness: a faster method that is less robust vs a slower reach-through method that is more robust.
- Position–structure
  - Re-wedging theme
    - Pull the knee to the chest to restore proper wedging.
    - Use the wedge to prevent the defender from freely pulling away (harder to fully separate once wedge is re-established).
  - Re-climb mechanics (high-level, transcript-faithful)
    - Open knees / extend body to create space to climb back up the leg.
    - Reconnect to the knee line once the wedge is restored, then return to attacking alignment.
- Key cues
  - Treat “knee slipping” as a mandatory repair signal; do not continue finishing attempts while the knee line is degrading.
- System tags
  - knee-recapture, wedge-restoration, reclimb, position-repair

### 4.8 Calf Slice Switch (24:00)
- Key concepts
  - Calf slice is introduced primarily as a switching/control solution when the knee line is lost, rather than as a primary finishing goal.
  - Finishing the calf slice is described as possible but framed as low-percentage unless the partner “gives” it; the emphasis is on using it to regain structure and continue the main leg system.
- Position–structure
  - Use-case and action theme
    - When the knee is lost, use calf slice positioning to create re-connection and stop the defender from freely turning/moving around.
    - From the regained connection, return to the preferred leg-attack system rather than chasing a marginal finish.
- Key cues
  - Treat calf slice as a “switch to regain control,” not a replacement for the primary heel hook / toe hold / knee bar pathways.
- System tags
  - calf-slice-switch, control-recovery, knee-line-repair, low-percentage-finish

### 4.9 Intro To Top Ushiro (29:38)
- Key concepts
  - “Top ushiro” is introduced as the solution when you cannot expose heels from outside ashi against a skilled opponent; it is used to create new exposure angles and threaten toe holds / heel hooks from a different hub.
  - The position is explicitly framed as risky if done incorrectly (back-take risk), so control layers must be in place before committing.
  - Definition given: “top ushiro” is described as “top rear facing.”
- Position–structure
  - Safety framing
    - The attacker’s back may appear exposed, but the defender must move toward that back; the system relies on controlling the leg/knee line so that back access is denied by the leg control constraint.
- Key cues
  - If you feel the knee slipping or your control degrading in top ushiro, immediately revert to a stronger control layer rather than “riding it out.”
- System tags
  - top-ushiro, hub-shift, exposure-angle, back-take-risk, top-rear-facing

### 4.10 Moving To Top Ushiro (31:12)
- Key concepts
  - The transition to top ushiro is framed around maintaining a wedge and preventing leg stripping while you heist up and rotate into the new orientation.
  - “Heisting up” and maintaining leg wedging are described as key ingredients because the position becomes less about pure wedging and more about controlling the leg while changing orientation.
- Position–structure
  - Transition mechanics emphasized (high-level, transcript-faithful)
    - Maintain the partner’s leg wedged between your chest and your own leg to prevent stripping during movement.
    - Heist up from the scissoring/entanglement structure into the top ushiro alignment.
    - Use an arm wedge to invert/turn the partner’s knee line in a way that makes clearing the knee from your knee line difficult.
    - Maintain control of the foot/leg between the arms (described as “bar it”) to prevent the defender from freeing the knee line during the transition.
- Key cues
  - If the defender can strip the leg as you move, you are transitioning without sufficient wedge/attachment; rebuild the wedge before attempting the movement again.
- System tags
  - transition-to-top-ushiro, heist, wedge-maintenance, anti-strip, knee-line-control

### 4.11 Heel Hook And Toe Hold Exposure (34:57)
- Key concepts
  - From top ushiro, toe hold is presented as the immediate accessible threat; once the defender reacts, the system can fall back into outside ashi to finish using previously established mechanics.
  - Achilles grip is again used as the “safety brake” when the knee line is threatened (stop the defender from pulling the knee out and attacking the back).
  - Exposure logic is framed as “threat → reaction → fall-back finish,” not “stay in top ushiro forever.”
- Position–structure
  - Threat and fall-back sequence emphasized
    - Place toe hold threat from top ushiro.
    - Fall back to outside ashi to finish (toe hold or heel hook) using the same finishing mechanics already defined.
  - Defender counter-space
    - Defender can use their free leg in multiple ways; the system expects this and uses structured control (knee line + foot control) to keep threats live.
- Key cues
  - If top ushiro feels compromised, do not force it; use toe hold as the forcing action and fall back to outside ashi where finishing mechanics are strongest and most familiar.
- System tags
  - top-ushiro-exposure, toe-hold-threat, fall-back-outside-ashi, achilles-safety, reaction-finishing

### 4.12 Double Trouble To Counter Locked Legs (37:33)
- Key concepts
  - Double trouble is introduced as a control configuration to address “locked legs / crossed feet” defensive structures that block standard leg-entry/exposure progress.
  - The system uses double trouble to separate the defender’s legs/feet and create immediate submission access (toe hold emphasized).
- Position–structure
  - Structural intentions described
    - Control both legs so the defender cannot freely crush forward and deny attacks.
    - When the defender’s feet are crossed/triangled, insert hand/structure into the crossing space to create separation and open toe hold access.
    - Use toe hold to force the first separation; once feet separate, additional attacks open.
- Key cues
  - When you see crossed feet as a defensive lock, treat “separate first” as the task before chasing heel exposure.
- System tags
  - double-trouble, crossed-feet, locked-legs, separation-first, toe-hold-access

### 4.13 Toe Hold Knee Bar Dilemma (40:26)
- Key concepts
  - A dilemma is explicitly defined: toe hold threat vs knee bar threat, used to force the defender into the losing defensive choice.
  - Toe hold is used to break the defender’s “locked legs” structure; knee bar becomes available as the defender changes leg configuration to defend the toe hold.
- Position–structure
  - Control and dilemma building (high-level, transcript-faithful)
    - Weave/wedge mechanics are used to keep the defender from freely turning or heisting while you attack.
    - V-grip / two-hands-on-leg control is referenced as part of securing the toe hold line while denying easy leg withdrawal.
    - Key safety constraint stated: if you let go of the controlled leg, the defender can heist and turn into 50/50; the system avoids this by maintaining control while switching threats.
  - Finish mechanics cues repeated in this context
    - Retraction of the elbow as hips come forward.
    - Knees splay as hips drive to create strong breaks (paired with toe-crunch direction).
- Key cues
  - Do not abandon leg control while attacking the dilemma; losing control is framed as enabling the defender’s best counter (heist to 50/50).
- System tags
  - dilemma, toe-hold-vs-kneebar, control-maintenance, anti-heist, 50-50-avoidance

### 4.14 Spinning The Leg (45:54+)
- Key concepts
  - “Spin the leg” is referenced as a transition action to move into cross ashi (“cross-ashi / cross-achi”) grabbing when finishing/exposure paths require a re-alignment or different hub.
  - The spin is framed as a structural reorientation tool: change the leg relationship to open the next family of attacks rather than forcing a stalled line.
- Position–structure
  - The spin is presented as a bridge from the current hub (top ushiro / outside ashi threat space) into cross ashi control space.
- Key cues
  - When exposure/finish is stalled and the defender’s leg configuration is giving you the wrong angle, “spin the leg” is used to re-set the angle and enter the next hub rather than continuing to grind.
- System tags
  - spin-transition, cross-ashi, hub-shift, angle-reset
