# VIEWING NOTES – Systematically Attacking the Legs (Gordon Ryan)

## 5.0 Volume 5 — Cross-Ashi: Criss-Cross / Reverse Figure-4 / Leg Lace / Dracula → Heist to Top

### 5.1 Cross Ashi family taxonomy (Outside Ashi vs Inside Sankaku vs Criss-Cross) (00:08–06:39)

- Key concepts
  - Cross Ashi is framed as a **high-finishing** family with strong breaking alignment and access to inverted finishing mechanics.
  - Three key “looks” introduced as distinct tools: **Outside Ashi**, **Inside Sankaku (triangle)**, **Criss-Cross Ashi**.
  - Triangle/Criss-Cross are presented as ways to **increase control and exposure** (especially by managing the secondary leg / hip line).
  - The “why”: enhance heel exposure and keep opponent from escaping by **standing/turning/spinning**.

- Position–structure
  - Cross Ashi baseline → rotation/breaking alignment is the core.
  - Inside Sankaku: triangle structure used to lock and constrain the opponent’s hip/leg relationship.
  - Criss-Cross: cross-style entanglement variant used as a **control + transition** platform.

- Decision rules (IF/THEN)
  - IF you need maximal control and to reduce opponent mobility, THEN build **Inside Sankaku (triangle)**.
  - IF you want a cross-family control platform that feeds other looks, THEN build **Criss-Cross**.
  - IF the opponent’s movement/escape is coming from the “other leg,” THEN shift to structures that **address the secondary leg** (triangle/criss-cross).

- Failure signatures + fixes
  - Failure: opponent can freely turn/stand off "free leg" action → Fix: transition to **triangle/criss-cross** to clamp and reduce mobility.
  - Failure: cross-ashi feels “powerful but loose” → Fix: pick a tighter sub-structure (triangle/criss-cross) before chasing exposure.

- Completion checks
  - You can name which sub-structure you are in (Outside vs Sankaku vs Criss-Cross) and why.
  - Opponent’s ability to stand/turn is meaningfully reduced (mobility constraint is real).

- System tags
  - #cross-ashi #ashi-family #inside-sankaku #criss-cross #control-before-finish #secondary-leg-control


### 5.2 Criss-Cross as a control platform (triangle logic + transition readiness) (06:39–16:40)

- Key concepts
  - Criss-Cross is framed as a **control-first** cross-family look that sets up further exposure rather than forcing immediate finishes.
  - Controlling the opponent’s ability to turn is treated as the priority before escalating.
  - The triangle structure remains a recurring “solution” when needing a tighter clamp.

- Position–structure
  - Criss-Cross: maintain cross-style entanglement while organizing your legs to limit opponent’s turning line.
  - Triangle (Inside Sankaku) is referenced as a tightening mechanism when you need stronger immobilization.

- Decision rules (IF/THEN)
  - IF opponent is turning/rotating to escape Cross Ashi, THEN use Criss-Cross/triangle logic to **re-stabilize**.
  - IF the position is stable but exposure is not, THEN hold structure and transition intentionally rather than “chasing the foot.”

- Failure signatures + fixes
  - Failure: you chase heel/foot and lose structure → Fix: **rebuild Criss-Cross control** first; re-earn exposure.
  - Failure: opponent continues turning freely → Fix: **triangle clamp** to shut down hip rotation.

- Completion checks
  - You can hold Criss-Cross without losing knee-line/hip relationship while opponent tries to turn.
  - You can transition into a tighter clamp (triangle) without disconnecting.

- System tags
  - #criss-cross #inside-sankaku #cross-family #rotation-control #stabilize-then-escalate


### 5.3 Reverse figure-four Achilles as tether → heel exposure (cowboy drill + belly-up/belly-down line) (16:40–25:35)

- Key concepts
  - Reverse figure-four Achilles is presented as a **control/tether** to create the reaction that exposes the heel.
  - Key dilemma: **Achilles pressure (reverse figure-four) forces movement** → heel becomes available.
  - “Cowboy drill” is introduced as a training mechanism for **finishing under rotation**.
  - Belly-up vs belly-down is framed as a **finishing orientation choice** once exposure exists.

- Position–structure
  - Reverse figure-four Achilles: used to lock/tether and increase control while setting the heel exposure sequence.
  - Cowboy drill: structured finishing reps emphasizing staying connected through rotation.
  - Belly-up / belly-down: two finishing orientations referenced as part of the endgame.

- Decision rules (IF/THEN)
  - IF heel is hidden / exposure is poor, THEN apply **reverse figure-four Achilles** to force the reaction that reveals it.
  - IF opponent rotates/turns to relieve pressure, THEN follow and connect into the **heel exposure** line (do not disconnect).
  - IF finishing is not clean belly-up, THEN transition to the **belly-down** orientation when the rotation/angle supports it.

- Failure signatures + fixes
  - Failure: Achilles pressure becomes “just pain” and you lose control → Fix: treat it as a **tether**, not a finish-only squeeze; maintain structure.
  - Failure: opponent turns and you lose the leg connection → Fix: “follow the turn” using the drill logic; stay attached through the rotation.

- Completion checks
  - Reverse figure-four reliably creates a predictable reaction that improves heel visibility.
  - You can complete controlled finishing reps through rotation without disconnecting.

- Drills/Games
  - Cowboy drill (explicit): use it to rehearse staying connected through the finish as the opponent rotates.

- System tags
  - #reverse-figure-four #achilles-as-tether #heel-exposure #cowboy-drill #belly-up #belly-down


### 5.4 Leg lace finishing (structure + exposing heel through leg control) (25:35–34:35)

- Key concepts
  - Leg lace is introduced as a **finishing-focused control** that improves stability and exposure.
  - The lace is treated as a way to **tie up the opponent’s legs** so they cannot easily turn/stand to escape.
  - Emphasis: build control first, then finish from a position the opponent cannot freely unwind.

- Position–structure
  - Leg lace: binding control that constrains the opponent’s legs/hips and limits escape mobility.
  - Finish path is presented as coming *after* the immobilization is established.

- Decision rules (IF/THEN)
  - IF opponent is escaping via turning/standing, THEN build **leg lace** to reduce their ability to move the hips/legs independently.
  - IF control is tight and exposure opens, THEN finish; IF exposure does not open, THEN adjust lace rather than abandoning control.

- Failure signatures + fixes
  - Failure: lace is loose and opponent still rotates/stands → Fix: re-tighten binding and re-establish the immobilization before escalating.
  - Failure: you attempt to finish before the lace truly immobilizes → Fix: treat lace as a **position**, not a moment.

- Completion checks
  - Opponent cannot freely separate their legs to create escape mobility.
  - You can hold the lace long enough to deliberately choose your finishing route.

- System tags
  - #leg-lace #two-leg-control #immobilization #finish-after-control #cross-family


### 5.5 Half Dracula (entry + role as control upgrade) (34:35–38:05)

- Key concepts
  - Half Dracula is introduced as a **next-level trapping** of the legs as a control upgrade off prior structures.
  - The focus is on increasing immobilization so the opponent cannot freely turn/stand to relieve pressure or clear the entanglement.
  - It is framed as a stepping stone toward stronger two-leg trapping (including “double” variants later).

- Position–structure
  - Half Dracula: arm/forearm-based trapping layered onto the leg control structure to increase immobilization.
  - Maintains the leg control while tightening the opponent’s ability to move the legs independently.

- Decision rules (IF/THEN)
  - IF leg lace control is present but opponent still has meaningful leg mobility, THEN upgrade to **Half Dracula**.
  - IF opponent’s movement is coming from the “free” leg, THEN prioritize trapping that leg into the Dracula structure.

- Failure signatures + fixes
  - Failure: half-dracula does not meaningfully restrict movement → Fix: re-build the trap so both legs cannot move independently.
  - Failure: you lose connection while upgrading → Fix: upgrade in stages; do not sacrifice primary control to “reach” the trap.

- Completion checks
  - The opponent’s ability to use the free leg to rescue/stand is clearly reduced.
  - You can maintain control while transitioning toward stronger trapping.

- System tags
  - #half-dracula #two-leg-trap #immobilization-upgrade #leg-lace-to-dracula


### 5.6 Double Dracula + triangle logic (tightening immobilization and controlling rotation) (38:05–43:20)

- Key concepts
  - Double Dracula is presented as a stronger immobilization layer that makes the opponent’s legs **even harder to free**.
  - Triangle logic reappears: clamping/locking is repeatedly used to deny rotation and create predictable exposure.
  - The objective remains: immobilize → expose → finish, rather than scramble finishing.

- Position–structure
  - Double Dracula: increased trapping on the legs (explicit “double” emphasis).
  - Triangle/clamping concepts referenced as complementary immobilization tools.

- Decision rules (IF/THEN)
  - IF half dracula is not enough to shut down the opponent’s leg rescue/rotation, THEN upgrade to **double dracula**.
  - IF opponent is still turning to hide exposure, THEN use clamp logic (triangle-style constraint) to restrict that turn.

- Failure signatures + fixes
  - Failure: opponent can still rotate freely → Fix: increase immobilization (double trap + clamp) before continuing.
  - Failure: you treat double dracula as a finish position without exposure → Fix: keep it as immobilization until the finish is structurally supported.

- Completion checks
  - Opponent cannot meaningfully use either leg independently to clear the trap.
  - Rotation is constrained enough that exposure begins to appear predictably.

- System tags

### 5.7 Stretch Ashi as a response/transition (re-centering and re-attacking) (43:20–50:05)

- Key concepts
  - Stretch Ashi is introduced as a transition option within the cross-family landscape when the exchange demands a different alignment.
  - Emphasis: transition in a way that maintains control and creates re-attack opportunities rather than losing connection.
  - Opponent’s turning line remains the central problem to solve.

- Position–structure
  - Stretch Ashi: shift in structure used to maintain control while changing alignment to the opponent’s leg.
  - Used as a platform to re-enter exposure/attack lines after prior immobilization phases.

- Decision rules (IF/THEN)
  - IF your current trap/control no longer produces exposure, THEN transition into **stretch ashi** to re-align.
  - IF opponent is turning to escape, THEN adjust structure (stretch ashi) while maintaining connection, rather than disengaging.

- Failure signatures + fixes
  - Failure: transition causes disconnection → Fix: prioritize staying attached and re-centering before escalating.
  - Failure: opponent regains turning freedom during transition → Fix: re-apply rotation constraint immediately after switching alignment.

- Completion checks
  - You can transition into stretch ashi without giving the opponent a clean disengagement.
  - The new alignment creates a clearer re-attack path than the prior look.

- System tags

### 5.8 Heist to top (Topo Shiro) vs heel exposure dilemma (50:05–57:07)

- Key concepts
  - A dilemma is presented: opponent must choose between **blocking the heist** or **protecting the heel** (can’t stop both).
  - The “heist” is framed as a route to come on top into a top-control outcome (“topo shiro”).
  - The exchange blends lower-body threat with upper-body engagement to overload defenses.

- Position–structure
  - Heist mechanics: build the motion to come up on top while maintaining leg control connection.
  - Topo shiro: top-control outcome referenced as a positional result of the heist dilemma.
  - Upper/lower interplay: leg attacks + upper-body threats used to keep the opponent reactive.

- Decision rules (IF/THEN)
  - IF opponent “falls” to one hip to deny the heist, THEN the opposite-side reaction **exposes the heel**.
  - IF opponent “falls” to the other hip to protect the heel, THEN they **allow the heist** and you come on top.
  - IF opponent is locked and bracing for leg attack, THEN threaten upper-body progress/passing and be ready to fall back into legs when they unlock.

- Failure signatures + fixes
  - Failure: your heist is easily stopped without creating exposure → Fix: tighten the dilemma: make them commit to a hip choice that opens the other outcome.
  - Failure: you commit to only one layer (only legs or only top) → Fix: keep both threats live so defense must choose.

- Completion checks
  - You can consistently force a “hip choice” that yields either heel exposure or top entry.
  - You can transition between lower-body and upper-body phases without losing control.

- Drills/Games
  - Dilemma game (explicit): partner starts with legs locked; you alternate threatening heel exposure vs heisting to top (“topo shiro”).

- System tags
  - #heist #topo-shiro #upper-lower-dilemma #decision-overload #come-on-top


### 5.9 Falling across to toe hold ↔ knee bar dilemma (wrap-up decision layer) (57:07–59:53)

- Key concepts
  - Falling across is used to enter a **toe hold / knee bar dilemma** previously referenced in the series.
  - The scoop-grip framing is used to initiate the fall-across entry into the dilemma space.
  - The emphasis is on using dilemmas to force the opponent into a losing defensive choice.

- Position–structure
  - Establish scoop grip on the outside of the primary leg → fall across into the attack lane.
  - Toe hold / knee bar dilemma is treated as a predictable endpoint once the fall-across is established.

- Decision rules (IF/THEN)
  - IF opponent defends one submission line (toe hold), THEN the defense creates the conditions for the other (knee bar).
  - IF opponent’s legs are locked/structured defensively, THEN provoke the unlock through the threat progression and enter the dilemma.

- Failure signatures + fixes
  - Failure: fall-across happens without control and opponent escapes → Fix: secure the initiating control (scoop grip + leg control) before falling.
  - Failure: you treat it as one attack rather than a dilemma → Fix: commit to the paired threats and force the choice.

- Completion checks
  - You can reliably enter the fall-across lane with control (not a scramble).
  - You can present the opponent with a true two-option dilemma (they must pick a defense).

- System tags
  - #toe-hold #knee-bar #dilemma #fall-across #scoop-grip #endgame
