# VIEWING NOTES – Systematically Attacking the Legs (Gordon Ryan)

## 3.0 Volume 3

### 3.1 Intro To Outside Ashi (0:00)
- Key concepts
  - Outside ashi is introduced as a primary breaking platform, but with a higher back-exposure/guard-pass risk profile than irimi ashi due to loss of the inside-leg wedge.
  - Safety priority: outside ashi must be paired with a reliable upper-body control solution (Achilles grip emphasized) to prevent the defender from turning the corner to the back.
- Position–structure
  - Outside ashi removes the inside-leg “physical wedge” that blocks the defender’s hip/shoulder line from moving around to the attacker’s back.
- Key cues
  - Treat outside ashi as “break-ready” only when back-exposure denial is solved (default solve = Achilles grip control).
- System tags
  - outside-ashi, risk-profile, back-exposure-denial, achilles-grip

### 3.2 Irimi Ashi Garami Vs Outside Ashi Garami (0:56)
- Key concepts
  - Irimi ashi advantage: inside leg physically blocks the defender’s cornering path; defender cannot easily pressure forward and “turn the corner” to the back.
  - Outside ashi risk: without the inside leg, defender can move around the corner more readily; attacker must compensate with Achilles grip to stay safe.
  - Transition rule: if the defender’s knee/hip alignment slips in a way that threatens back exposure, revert to Achilles control, re-stabilize, then re-attack.
- Position–structure
  - Irimi: inside leg wedge functions as a back-take barrier.
  - Outside: wedge removed; defender’s passing/back-take pathways increase if attacker’s legs are pushed down and hips are lifted.
- Key cues
  - “When in doubt” safety rule: default to Achilles grip (described as the most robust control layer) to prevent back exposure and stabilize the platform.
- System tags
  - irimi-ashi, outside-ashi, wedge-vs-no-wedge, safety-control, achilles-grip

### 3.3 Countering A High Leg (5:04)
- Key concepts
  - High-leg throw-over (defender stepping/throwing the leg high to clear or create interference) is treated as a primary defensive attempt against outside ashi control.
  - Immediate mechanical answer: wipe/clear the high leg as it comes over while maintaining hip connection and control integrity.
  - Secondary answer: threaten a submission to punish the high-leg attempt and force the defender to abandon it.
- Position–structure
  - High leg changes the defender’s leg line to interfere with control and can create a path to disengage or attack back exposure if allowed to settle.
- Key cues
  - React early: the moment the high leg starts to come over, clear it immediately rather than waiting until it has fully settled.
- System tags
  - high-leg-defense, outside-ashi, leg-clearing, timing

### 3.4 Countering A High Leg With Wrist To Wrist Toe Hold (8:07)
- Key concepts
  - Submission threat used to counter the high leg: wrist-to-wrist toe hold.
  - Breaking principle reiterated: toes to butt + heel to chin (coupled force model).
  - Finishing mechanics described with opposing elbow actions (one elbow drops/anchors while the other rises to reinforce the toe-crunch and heel-lift direction).
- Position–structure
  - Wrist-to-wrist configuration is used as a strong control frame to finish the toe hold once the defender’s foot is available from the high-leg reaction.
- Key cues
  - Maintain the coupled force direction throughout: do not “twist randomly”; keep the toe-crunch and heel-lift aligned with the intended break line.
- System tags
  - toe-hold, wrist-to-wrist, high-leg-punish, coupled-force

### 3.5 Controlling Outside Ashi And Denying Back Exposure (10:28)
- Key concepts
  - Achilles grip is repeatedly framed as the primary safety/control layer from outside ashi (explicit “king” framing).
  - Functional outcomes attributed to Achilles grip:
    - Denies back exposure when the defender tries to turn the corner.
    - Stops forward pressure from collapsing the attacker’s knee/hips into pass/back-take pathways.
    - Lets the attacker “climb” the leg back to preferred grips/positions once stability is restored.
  - Control rule: if the exchange starts going “downhill” (knee slipping, defender pressuring), switch back to Achilles control immediately.
- Position–structure
  - Key control objective: trap the defender’s knee as deep inside your knee line as possible to reduce their mobility and passing leverage.
  - Control hierarchy described
    - Highest robustness: Achilles grip.
    - Lesser/temporary controls: elbow pinch variants (useful but less robust than full Achilles grip).
- Key cues
  - If you feel back exposure risk or guard-pass risk rising, revert to Achilles grip first; rebuild the position, then return to heel exposure/finishing grips.
- System tags
  - outside-ashi-control, achilles-grip, back-exposure-denial, guard-pass-denial, knee-line-depth

### 3.6 Controlling Hips By Leg Scissoring (13:21)
- Key concepts
  - Leg scissoring is a primary method to pin hips and stop the defender’s forward pressure (“crushing” pressure) from outside ashi.
  - Scissoring is framed as a general solution when the defender’s hips want to come forward into you.
- Position–structure
  - Mechanical intent: top knee drives toward defender hips while bottom knee pulls inward (creates opposing forces that pin the hip line and deny forward drive).
  - Hip elevation is used to help maintain the pin and keep the entanglement active rather than flattened.
- Key cues
  - When forward pressure starts, prioritize hip pinning (scissor) over chasing heel exposure from a collapsing structure.
- System tags
  - hip-control, leg-scissoring, forward-pressure-denial, outside-ashi

### 3.7 Controlling Hips With A Cross Shin (15:45)
- Key concepts
  - Cross shin across the defender’s hips is introduced as an additional/alternative hip-stopping tool (often paired with scissoring).
  - Cross-shoulder post is used to create space so the defender cannot collapse the outside of the attacker’s knee and crush forward.
  - Result: defender cannot come up and apply crushing forward pressure; attacker can stabilize and continue attacking.
- Position–structure
  - Cross shin functions as a physical barrier across the hip line.
  - Cross-shoulder post monitors distance and prevents knee collapse while the cross shin is set.
- Key cues
  - If the defender’s forward pressure is collapsing your knee line, create space with the cross-shoulder post first, then place the cross shin.
- System tags
  - cross-shin, cross-shoulder-post, distance-management, forward-pressure-denial

### 3.8 Forward Roll (18:29)
- Key concepts
  - Forward roll is used as a dynamic solution when the defender’s forward pressure is overwhelming static hip-stopping tools.
  - Principle: “go with” forward pressure instead of carrying body weight; convert their drive into a roll-through and re-entry into attacking alignment.
- Position–structure
  - Typical trigger: defender drives forward; attacker uses cross-shoulder post momentarily, tucks head, rolls through to reset alignment and continue attacks.
- Key cues
  - When pressure is too heavy to hold in place, convert it into motion (roll-through) rather than accepting a crushed/flattened position.
- System tags
  - forward-roll, pressure-conversion, reset-alignment, outside-ashi

### 3.10 Belly Down Heel Exposure (23:39)
- Key concepts
  - Heel exposure prerequisite: create a disconnect between defender’s heel and the attacker’s hip (if heel stays connected to hip, exposure is blocked).
  - Exposure can be built belly-down or belly-up; belly-down is emphasized as a strong route when disconnection is achieved.
  - Disconnection method emphasized: leg scissoring creates separation even against strong back-heel resistance.
- Position–structure
  - Disconnection is the governing “gate” before exposure attempts; exposure is treated as impossible until heel-hip connection is broken.
- Key cues
  - First diagnostic: “Is their heel glued to my hip?” If yes, solve disconnection before trying to dig/expose.
- System tags
  - heel-exposure, disconnection-gate, heel-hip-separation, leg-scissoring

### 3.11 Post Ashi Heel Exposure (28:42)
- Key concepts
  - Post ashi is presented as a method to expose the heel while keeping the defender down and managing forward pressure.
  - Use-case: difficulty going belly-down to dig the heel (due to defender skill/position); post ashi provides an alternative exposure route.
  - Outcome logic described: if defender tries to roll through, belly-up breaking becomes available (described as effective once the post prevents foot-position disruption).
- Position–structure
  - Post ashi advantage: stepping on/against the defender’s hips creates stronger extension and exposure leverage.
- Key cues
  - If belly-down digging is failing, switch to post ashi to create exposure/finish alignment without needing extended belly-down commitment.
- System tags
  - post-ashi, heel-exposure, forward-pressure-management, belly-up-breaking

### 3.12 Post Feet Heel Exposure (33:09)
- Key concepts
  - Post-feet method is framed as similar to post ashi but generally less strong (less extension) while being easier to hold longer.
  - Trade-off model:
    - Post ashi: stronger extension/exposure but harder to maintain foot placement for long periods.
    - Post feet: weaker extension but longer hold time.
- Position–structure
  - Posting the foot outside the defender’s hip creates a lever that can lift the heel and help remove heel weight from the floor for exposure.
- Key cues
  - Choose post ashi when you need maximum extension now; choose post feet when you need to maintain the posting alignment longer while working exposure.
- System tags
  - post-feet, post-ashi-comparison, heel-unweighting, exposure-lever

### 3.13 Cross Shin Heel Exposure (35:24)
- Key concepts
  - Belly-up heel exposure method using the same cross shin concept previously used for hip control.
  - Unlocking legs is conditional:
    - If defender is leaning back, unlocking can be dangerous (they can come forward into counter-heel hooks).
    - If defender is pressuring forward into you, unlocking is safer (their pressure removes the immediate counter-heel hook threat).
  - Exposure failure/branching:
    - If defender straightens the leg, heel exposure becomes difficult.
    - If defender heel-slips and turns back, follow the turn and complete the break sequence from the new hip orientation.
- Position–structure
  - Cross shin + shoulder post creates the space to expose belly-up while controlling the defender’s forward drive.
- Key cues
  - Unlock only when the defender’s pressure direction makes it safe; treat “leaning back” vs “pressuring forward” as the decision gate.
- System tags
  - cross-shin, belly-up-exposure, conditional-unlock, heel-slip-branch

### 3.14 Stress Test (39:12)
- Key concepts
  - Stress test questions are explicitly framed as control gates before relying on finishing:
    - Can the defender straighten the leg?
    - Can the defender move backward (drag out/away) to clear the position?
    - Can the defender come forward and force heel slip (especially if hands are low)?
  - Back-heel engagement + correct hand position are described as the primary answers to leg-straightening and backward movement.
  - High hands are framed as mandatory to prevent forward-pressure heel slip.
- Position–structure
  - Belly-up exposure testing includes checking that hips-off-floor prevents the defender from moving away and straightening freely.
- Key cues
  - If any stress-test gate fails (straighten, move back, force slip), fix platform mechanics (back heel, hand height, hip elevation) before committing to break attempts.
- System tags
  - stress-test, straightening-denial, backward-denial, hands-high, back-heel, hip-elevation

### 3.15 Countering A Heel Slip With A Roll (42:32)
- Key concepts
  - Heel slip is acknowledged as inevitable at times; response is to use the defender’s forward momentum to roll-through and re-expose on the opposite side.
  - Primary rule: if the heel slips as the defender drives forward, do not panic or chase; roll them through immediately and rebuild exposure.
  - Re-exposure mechanics include switching to an Achilles grip as a control handle and pulling the leg line behind the defender’s knee (quadriceps/knee-line re-attachment described).
- Position–structure
  - Heel slip tends to occur under forward pressure; the defender commonly tries to flank the knee and come forward to escape the breaking line.
- Key cues
  - Treat “heel slip + forward drive” as an automatic roll-through trigger.
- System tags
  - heel-slip, roll-through-recovery, momentum-use, re-exposure

### 3.16 Heel Exposure On A Kneeling Opponent (44:48)
- Key concepts
  - When the defender builds to a knee, heel exposure is harder because body weight loads the foot (heel becomes “weighted” and harder to lift/expose).
  - Solution is framed as kuzushi/off-balancing to put the defender’s butt on the floor and force knee/hip realignment that makes the foot weightless again.
  - Sequence elements described
    - Create space (“pump the knees”) to open the reap/leg line.
    - Defender resists by pointing knee toward attacker; attacker knocks them down so knee must point back inside.
    - Shoulder post is used to monitor distance and prevent immediate pressure during the off-balance.
    - Invert the knee to catch once the knee turns back in; switch to a false grip as needed to proceed.
  - Cross-shin exposure method is referenced as a follow-on to lift the foot and proceed into breaking.
- Position–structure
  - Decision model: kneeling base requires first removing base (butt to floor) before expecting consistent heel exposure.
- Key cues
  - If the foot is “heavy” because weight is over it, solve base first (off-balance/knockdown) before digging for the heel.
- System tags
  - kneeling-opponent, kuzushi, base-removal, shoulder-post, knee-inversion, heel-unweighting

### 3.17 Reiterating Achilles/Heel Hook Dilemma (47:23)
- Key concepts
  - Dilemma restated as a branch-selection rule:
    - Booted foot → heel is easier to expose (heel hook line opens).
    - Pointed toes → Achilles exposure/finish is easier (heel is hidden).
  - Functional use: rotate between Achilles and heel hook attempts based on the defender’s foot response; each attack forces the defensive reaction that enables the other.
- Position–structure
  - Achilles used as a controlling threat when heel digging is difficult; heel digging pursued when booting creates heel availability.
- Key cues
  - If heel digging is stalled by toe-pointing, pivot back to Achilles; if Achilles is resisted by booting, pivot to heel exposure.
- System tags
  - dilemma, achilles-vs-heelhook, foot-response-reading, branch-selection

### 3.18 Achilles - Irimi Ashi Vs Outside Ashi (48:32)
- Key concepts
  - Achilles mechanics are described as largely similar across irimi and outside ashi, but platform constraints change available options.
  - Outside ashi expectation: defender tries to separate the attacker’s legs; if defender raises hips/creates separation, attacker can pummel legs back inside to return to irimi.
  - Preferred outside-ashi upgrade for Achilles finishing: transfer to post ashi, then move hips out/away before extending.
- Position–structure
  - Shared mechanics: elbow positioning, hip movement out/away, then bridge/finish.
  - Platform difference: outside ashi generally offers fewer options than irimi (stated as a relative constraint).
- Key cues
  - If outside ashi becomes unstable due to separation attempts, either (1) pummel back to irimi or (2) upgrade to post ashi for strongest Achilles extension.
- System tags
  - achilles-mechanics, irimi-vs-outside, post-ashi-upgrade, pummel-back-inside

### 3.19 Finishing Achilles Lock Vs Defensive Roll (50:54)
- Key concepts
  - Defensive roll is treated as a strong response to poorly angled/low-heat Achilles attempts; common failure is finishing with incorrect elbow/angle alignment.
  - Branching solutions when the defender rolls
    - Convert to heel exposure (attack transition).
    - Convert to belly-down Achilles (framed as the strongest Achilles finishing orientation).
  - Belly-down Achilles finishing mechanics described (stacked cues)
    - Heist up to create height.
    - Scissor legs; knees wide.
    - Head up/coming forward as hips drive in (head/hip coordination).
    - Reap/angle change can be used during the defensive roll to arrive at belly-down alignment.
- Position–structure
  - Defensive roll changes orientation; attacker can use the roll to move into belly-down line rather than being dragged into a weak belly-up angle.
- Key cues
  - If the defender rolls and your Achilles angle is wrong, do not insist belly-up; convert to belly-down alignment and finish off the heist + scissor + head-up/hips-in stack.
- System tags
  - achilles-finish, defensive-roll-response, belly-down-achilles, branch-selection

### 3.20 Heel Knee Bar (55:05+)
- Key concepts
  - Heel knee bar definition: maintain a heel hook grip for control, but finish with knee bar mechanics (extension), often arising when the defender straightens the leg and rotates out.
  - Framed origin: often comes from a flaw/limitation in controlling mechanics (inability to keep a bend in the defender’s leg), but becomes a reliable finishing path.
  - Grip function clarified
    - Heel hook grip does not create the knee bar break.
    - Heel hook grip stabilizes position and acts as a “return deterrent”: if the defender turns back the wrong way to avoid the knee bar, they re-enter heel hook danger (“break their own leg” framing).
  - Finishing mechanics emphasized
    - Knees splayed/open.
    - Hips drive forward.
    - Hug ankle to chest as hips extend through.
  - Continuation options
    - If angle changes unfavorably, switch leg position to the opposite side to re-align the finish.
    - If defender keeps spinning, follow through into other entanglements (transition toward cross-ashi mentioned).
- Position–structure
  - Heel knee bar appears when the defender’s leg straightens; attacker uses the same control shell but switches the break mechanic to extension.
- Key cues
  - Treat “leg straightened + turn-out” as the trigger to convert from heel hook expectation into heel knee bar finishing.
- System tags
  - heel-knee-bar, control-vs-break, leg-straightening-trigger, deterrent-grip, finishing-mechanics
