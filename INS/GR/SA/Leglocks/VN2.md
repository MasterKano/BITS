# VIEWING NOTES – Systematically Attacking the Legs (Gordon Ryan)

## 2.0 Volume 2

### 2.1 Intro To Irimi Ashi Garami (0:00)
- Key concepts
  - Irimi ashi is presented as a primary “hub” entanglement because it gives strong control while reducing back-exposure risk compared to looser leg entanglements.
  - Strategic use-case: expose the heel reliably from a stable structure, then (often) transfer to outside ashi for higher-percentage catastrophic breaks.
- Position–structure
  - Priority connections are framed as “body-to-body + leg-to-leg” rather than “hands pulling a leg”: the structure should hold even without heavy hand effort.
- Key cues
  - The goal of the initial platform is: deny defender ability to freely come up and create back exposure, while keeping consistent heel exposure windows.
- System tags
  - irimi-ashi, hub-platform, heel-exposure, back-exposure-denial

### 2.2 Setting An Irimi Ashi Garami (1:53)
- Key concepts
  - Do not rely on arm-pulling against the defender’s leg; use your leg structure to create the pull and keep the entanglement tight.
  - “Heel-to-knee connection” is treated as a key retention concept (prevents the defender from peeling your foot off and separating your structure).
  - “Hip-to-hip connection” is treated as a second key retention concept (closes distance so the defender can’t create disengagement space).
- Position–structure
  - Entry/build sequence (as described)
    - Pull the opponent’s leg just far enough to shoot your knee behind their knee (set the bite) rather than yanking the leg with arms.
    - Use your knee pulling into your chest to draw them in (structure-driven pull).
    - Bring the foot up to the hip (establish the hip connection).
    - Pinch knees together with a slight extension on the controlling leg (stabilise the entanglement and prevent easy separation).
- Key cues
  - If your heel and knee drift apart, the defender’s hands can peel your foot off the hip easily; treat “heel-to-knee” as a constant maintenance task.
- System tags
  - irimi-ashi, heel-to-knee-connection, hip-to-hip-connection, entanglement-tightness

### 2.3 Stress Test (5:21)
- Key concepts
  - Stress testing is used to confirm the ashi structure is tight enough to survive real defensive motion long enough for breaking mechanics to matter.
  - The defender’s primary objective is framed as clearing the knee line by straightening and dragging the leg out; the attacker’s objective is to keep tightness and deny separation.
- Position–structure
  - Stress test behaviours referenced
    - Defender uses hands to pull/walk the leg backward to create separation and extract.
    - Defender rolls belly-down to change the alignment and try to disengage.
  - Attacker structural answers referenced
    - Pinch knees together and suck knees to chest to deny separation.
    - Raise hips slightly as the defender comes up (maintain bite and prevent “come-forward crush” pressure).
    - Maintain a strong back-heel action with the controlling leg as the defender rotates.
- Key cues
  - The test standard is: the structure should hold even with minimal hand use; if it fails, fix the leg/hip connections rather than “gripping harder.”
- System tags
  - stress-test, knee-line-denial, entanglement-integrity, retention-under-motion

### 2.4 Basic Achilles Lock (10:28)
- Key concepts
  - Achilles finishing is framed around keeping hands high and chin low while hips move away (avoid the common error of hips coming forward and chin drifting away).
  - Grip choice matters primarily for keeping hands high under defensive hand-fighting (avoid being pulled down).
  - The system frames achilles as both a finish and a controlling tool: if the defender can peel the foot off the hip, the position must be repaired before insisting on the finish.
- Position–structure
  - Finishing alignment cues referenced
    - Hands high (prevent defender from locking hands on your grip and dragging it down).
    - Chin low (keeps the breaking line tight).
    - Hips push away from opponent’s hips (creates the pressure line; belly-down orientation is referenced as a strong finishing option).
    - Elbow tight/in (used as a “keep the grip high” reinforcement when using certain grip configurations).
- Key cues
  - If the defender is peeling your foot off the hip, treat it as a positional failure signal: re-secure platform first, then finish.
- System tags
  - straight-ankle, hands-high, chin-low, hips-away, grip-management

### 2.5 Countering A Basic Extraction (14:18)
- Key concepts
  - “Kick and pull” extraction attempts are expected; the attacker response is framed as improving depth/tightness rather than chasing with arms.
  - The finish tightens as hips move away (you can create tightness without initially locking hands, then lock once the structure is loaded).
- Position–structure
  - Defensive behaviour referenced
    - Defender kicks foot deep then pulls back repeatedly to try to clear knee line and create disengagement.
  - Attacker response referenced
    - Reinforce the bite by pulling the knee to the chest (structure-driven), then load the finish by moving hips away.
    - After hips are far enough away to create tightness, bring hips in slightly as needed to maintain optimal alignment (tightness management rather than a single static pose).
- Key cues
  - When you feel the defender extracting, think “increase structural bite + hips away” rather than “chase the foot with arms.”
- System tags
  - anti-extraction, knee-to-chest-bite, hips-away-loading, tightness-management

### 2.6 Countering Forward Pressure With Ashi Garami And Elbow Position (16:48)
- Key concepts
  - Forward pressure (defender driving into you) is handled by (1) correct ashi structure and (2) immediate elbow-to-hip positioning once the achilles is locked.
  - Elbow-to-hip prevents the defender from “booting the foot through” while coming forward (boot + forward drive as a combined defensive theme).
- Position–structure
  - Key alignment referenced
    - The moment you lock the achilles, bring elbow down to hip (elbow/hip/ribcage glued concept appears later as well).
    - This elbow placement blocks the defender’s ability to kick the foot through/strip the bite as they drive forward.
- Key cues
  - If the defender is able to come forward and boot successfully, treat it as an elbow-position failure: correct elbow-to-hip immediately.
- System tags
  - forward-pressure-defense, elbow-to-hip, anti-boot, straight-ankle-control

### 2.7 Countering Forward Pressure With Post Ashi (19:18)
- Key concepts
  - When the defender peels the foot off the hip and/or forward pressure becomes significant, switching to post ashi is presented as a primary solution.
  - Post ashi is framed as producing a stronger break because you can push off the opponent’s hips while maintaining the heel/leg control.
- Position–structure
  - Switch trigger (as described)
    - Defender peels the foot off the hip.
    - Defender drives forward in a way that threatens to “shoot the leg through” and kill the bite.
  - Post ashi value
    - Creates a stable platform to push hips away and re-establish/maintain finishing alignment when the standard hip-foot connection is compromised.
- Key cues
  - When the original hip-foot connection is compromised, do not insist; switch platforms to preserve the breaking line.
- System tags
  - post-ashi, platform-switching, forward-pressure-defense, stronger-break-platform

### 2.8 Countering Forward Pressure With Belly Down Achilles (22:05)
- Key concepts
  - Belly-down achilles is presented as a strong conversion when the defender comes forward and the attacker can build height and drop hips to load pressure.
  - The finishing theme is “head up as hips come down” (create the belly-down pressure line).
- Position–structure
  - Conversion mechanics referenced
    - As defender comes forward, build height/post, widen knees, take elbow down toward hip, then bridge/drive into the belly-down finishing line.
    - Emphasis on keeping elbow/ribcage/hip glued while changing orientation (retain the integrity of the grip line).
- Key cues
  - When converting belly-down, treat “height + elbow discipline + hips down” as the non-negotiable sequence.
- System tags
  - belly-down-achilles, forward-pressure-conversion, elbow-discipline, posture-and-hip-line

### 2.9 Switching To Butterfly Ashi (25:27)
- Key concepts
  - Butterfly ashi is introduced as an answer when the defender peels the foot off the hip and steps/sits over the foot (a common forward-pressure + foot-strip reaction).
  - Post-butterfly ashi is referenced as a powerful variant option once you arrive in butterfly alignment.
- Position–structure
  - Scenario trigger referenced
    - Defender peels the foot off the hip (because it blocks their hip), then steps over/sits over the foot, forcing the position into a butterfly-foot relationship.
  - Key movement emphasis referenced
    - Move hips out and away while closing the right knee toward the floor (hip/knee coordination is framed as the stability mechanism).
- Key cues
  - If the defender repeatedly peels the foot and tries to scoot away, butterfly alignment is framed as a direct structural answer to stop that cycle.
- System tags
  - butterfly-ashi, post-butterfly, anti-foot-peel, hip-angle-management

### 2.10 Butterfly Ashi To Other Leg Entanglements (29:36)
- Key concepts
  - Butterfly ashi is treated as a transition hub: from the butterfly control line, you can transfer to outside ashi on either leg and access other entanglement families (including ushīrō entries mentioned).
  - Transfer logic includes switching the attacked leg (outside ashi on one leg → outside ashi on the opposite leg) as a deliberate system tool.
- Position–structure
  - Transition options referenced
    - Remove foot and transition to outside ashi on the same leg, or shoot heel across and pull into outside ashi on the opposite leg.
    - Spin/backstep pathways are referenced to reach cross ashi variations depending on how the defender squares/turns.
    - Ushīrō (including top ushīrō references) is mentioned as part of the transition space from butterfly/achilles grip control.
  - Control emphasis referenced
    - Control the space between opponent’s knees (knee-space control) as a stability principle while transitioning.
- Key cues
  - Butterfly is not a destination; it is used to regain control and then transfer into the entanglement you want (often outside ashi) on the leg that remains available.
- System tags
  - butterfly-ashi, transition-hub, leg-switching, outside-ashi-transfer, ushiro-entries

### 2.11 Achilles Heel Hook Dilemma (34:49)
- Key concepts
  - A dilemma is framed around defender foot behaviour once a controlling achilles is established:
    - If the defender “boots” hard, that is treated as a cue to move to heel hook exposure.
    - If the defender points toes (reducing heel availability), that increases achilles finishing availability.
  - Practical implication: you do not “choose” achilles vs heel hook in isolation; the defender’s foot response informs the correct attack branch.
- Position–structure
  - The controlling achilles is used as a handle to force predictable defensive foot behaviours that reveal the next best attack.
- Key cues
  - “Boot = heel hook cue” is explicitly stated as a recognition rule.
- System tags
  - achilles-heelhook-dilemma, foot-response-reading, branch-selection, threat-chaining

### 2.12 Finishing Mechanics (38:22)
- Key concepts
  - Finishing from irimi ashi is possible, but the common pattern is to transfer to outside ashi for higher-percentage breaking; both are addressed.
  - A “five major mechanics” model is referenced for reliable breaking alignment.
  - Core finishing requirement is a coupled-force idea: toes driven toward butt while heel is pulled toward chin (explicitly stated).
- Position–structure
  - Mechanical components explicitly referenced (best-effort capture)
    - Maintain heel-to-knee connection while establishing the breaking structure.
    - Use strong back-heel action with the controlling leg (pulling engagement).
    - Place/track the opponent’s heel into a consistent reference point (described using a “wristwatch” placement cue).
    - Apply coupled force: toes down toward butt + heel up toward chin.
    - Hip elevation/bridging as the final pressure amplifier (bridge without collapsing to the shoulder; aim between shoulder and elbow as described).
- Key cues
  - Do not treat breaks as slow “step-by-step” in live exchanges; the mechanics must stack smoothly and continuously once the window opens.
- System tags
  - irimi-finishing, coupled-force, heel-placement-cue, bridging-alignment, break-mechanics

### 2.13 Stress Test (45:32)
- Key concepts
  - Stress testing is repeated as a requirement when switching from achilles control into heel hook finishing: the platform must be resilient against the defender’s best responses.
  - High-hands requirement is reiterated as a safety/finish enabler (if hands are high and defender comes forward, they “break their own leg” framing is used).
- Position–structure
  - Testing focuses include
    - Defender attempts to come forward and crush.
    - Attacker confirms hands stay high and the ashi remains tight enough to finish immediately after the defender’s attempt resolves.
- Key cues
  - If you cannot keep hands high through the defender’s forward attempt, you are not ready to rely on that finish line live.
- System tags
  - stress-test, hands-high, forward-pressure-proof, transition-validation

### 2.14 Countering A Heel Slip With High Hands (46:54)
- Key concepts
  - Heel slip is framed as a common defender tactic when they can come forward and your hands are low.
  - Primary prevention is hands high immediately upon locking; this is positioned as both an anti-slip and a self-punishing trigger for forward pressure by the defender.
- Position–structure
  - Failure condition referenced
    - Low hands + imperfect ashi (common in live training) increases heel-slip likelihood when the defender comes forward.
  - Prevention condition referenced
    - Immediately raise hands high on lock; ensure ashi structure is set before relying on the finish.
- Key cues
  - “Hands low = heel slip risk” is presented as a simple recognition rule.
- System tags
  - heel-slip, hands-high, forward-pressure-punishment, finishing-discipline

### 2.15 Countering A Heel Slip With A Roll Through (48:28)
- Key concepts
  - Roll-through is presented as a reset/reboot solution when heel slip + forward pressure threatens to crush the attacker’s position.
  - The roll-through branches based on defender base: if they land seated/hip, reboot to irimi; if they build to knee, transition options open (ashi re-entry, X/reverse X references appear).
- Position–structure
  - Roll-through outcomes referenced
    - Roll partner through and reboot the system back into irimi ashi.
    - Use pendulum/figure-four style mechanics to sit the partner to the hip line (anti-crush recovery).
    - If partner comes up to a knee, transition to different control/entanglement families (reaping entry referenced; X/reverse X referenced).
- Key cues
  - When the position is “no good” (heel slipped and you’re being crushed), prioritise rebooting the platform over stubbornly clamping and getting flattened.
- System tags
  - roll-through, system-reboot, base-reading, anti-crush-recovery, transition-branching

### 2.16 Switching To Outside Ashi (52:34)
- Key concepts
  - Outside ashi is framed as the desired finishing platform for strong breaks; switching is used when the opportunity appears (leg can “sneak out” into outside ashi alignment).
  - Switching reduces the need for extended multi-roll sequences; instead of riding many rolls, you convert to outside ashi and finish from a stronger structure.
- Position–structure
  - Switch mechanics referenced (best-effort)
    - Bring knee to chest to maintain control, block at the hip, then switch to outside ashi where the break is stronger and more immediate.
- Key cues
  - When the positional window appears to transition to outside ashi, take it rather than forcing prolonged roll-chasing.
- System tags
  - outside-ashi, platform-switching, stronger-breaks, anti-multi-roll

### 2.17 Single Roll Theory (54:34)
- Key concepts
  - “Single roll theory” is presented as controlling the entanglement so the defender can roll through only once (not repeatedly).
  - The number/speed of defender rolls is framed as being determined by the quality of your ashi structure (platform determines escape bandwidth).
- Position–structure
  - Control mechanics referenced
    - Maintain hips connected to hips.
    - As defender initiates roll, point the knee toward the floor; bringing knees down/out to the floor line is used to shut down repeated rolling.
    - After the single roll, follow through and switch to outside ashi on the opposite side (single-roll → follow → outside ashi).
- Key cues
  - If the defender is rolling multiple times, treat it as a platform-control failure: your ashi is allowing too much rotational freedom.
- System tags
  - single-roll-theory, roll-limiting, hip-connection, knee-to-floor-control, follow-and-switch

### 2.18 Countering A Post Knee (59:41)
- Key concepts
  - Defender building to a posted knee is treated as a common intermediate base that changes the attacker’s control tasks and transition options.
  - A false grip on the defender’s knee is used as a control handle while opening the attacker’s knee to create threading/transition space.
- Position–structure
  - Control sequence referenced (best-effort)
    - Defender posts on a knee.
    - Attacker takes a false grip on the knee and opens their right knee to make space.
    - Use that space to thread the heel/leg across while pulling the defender’s knee across toward the attacker’s hip line (re-align for outside ashi landing).
- Key cues
  - When the defender is stable on a knee, treat knee control as the handle that lets you realign the entanglement rather than fighting the whole body.
- System tags
  - post-knee-base, false-grip-knee-control, threading-space, re-alignment

### 2.19 Figure 4 Switch If They Refuse To Roll (1:02:22)
- Key concepts
  - If the defender will not roll through, a full figure-four configuration is used to change the constraints and force a more favourable alignment.
  - The configuration emphasis is “heels down, knees flare/splay, hips lift,” combined with pointing knees opposite the defender’s preferred roll direction to deny their escape.
- Position–structure
  - Structural effects referenced
    - Figure-four lock tightens the leg configuration.
    - Heels down + knees flared + hips up creates a stronger control frame and changes the defender’s ability to turn back in and peel the foot off the hip.
- Key cues
  - If the defender is absorbing pressure but not giving you the roll you want, treat figure-four as the switch that changes the problem rather than endlessly “popping” pressure.
- System tags
  - figure-four-switch, anti-refusal, roll-denial, control-tightening

### 2.20 Breaking Mechanics (1:05:13)
- Key concepts
  - Belly-up breaking mechanics are contrasted with belly-down: belly-up has specific slippage problems when sweaty if you rely on certain forearm placements.
  - The solution is framed as changing the breaking direction and stacking leg mechanics (back heel + hips up + knees splayed) while controlling escape direction by pointing knees opposite the defender’s path.
- Position–structure
  - Mechanical stack elements referenced
    - Strong back-heel action.
    - Hips up.
    - Knees splay, then knees point opposite the defender’s escape direction.
    - Use elbow action (opening/arching) to straighten/align the leg as part of the breaking sequence (referenced in the context of managing slip and alignment).
- Key cues
  - If you feel slippage in sweaty conditions, treat it as a mechanics problem (direction + structure), not a “squeeze harder” problem.
- System tags
  - breaking-mechanics, belly-up-variation, anti-slip, escape-direction-control

### 2.21 Outside Ashi Breaking Mechanics (1:07:52)
- Key concepts
  - Outside ashi breaking mechanics are presented as highly similar to irimi side-finishing mechanics, with outside ashi framed as the “break right away” platform.
  - A five-step style breakdown is implied (explicitly enumerated items appear), emphasising top-knee-down, knee pinch, back-heel hip pull, toe-crunch/heel-lift, and final bridge.
- Position–structure
  - Platform structure referenced
    - Feet often triangled behind the opponent’s hips; both legs are to the outside, enabling strong back-heel and hip-pull.
  - Breaking sequence elements explicitly referenced
    - Knees pinch with emphasis on the top knee coming down toward the floor.
    - Strong back heel to pull hips in.
    - Crunch toes toward butt while bringing heel to chin (coupled force).
    - Hips come off the ground slightly, then bridge over the bottom shoulder to finish.
    - Chin/heel distance cue is referenced (keep alignment consistent through the bridge).
- Key cues
  - Outside ashi is treated as the platform where correct mechanics yield immediate breaking power; do not delay once the structure is fully set.
- System tags
  - outside-ashi, outside-heelhook, top-knee-down, hip-pull, coupled-force, bridge-finishing
