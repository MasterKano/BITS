# System: Backside 50/50 (High-Offence Asymmetry Upgrade)

## Purpose
- Convert 50/50-type exchanges into a more asymmetrical, attacker-favoured configuration with improved heel exposure and constrained defender rotations.
- Act as a primary upgrade hub after following rolls from Cross/Inside Ashi or standard 50/50.
- Provide a stable platform for decisive finishing and structured follow-ups when standard entanglements become symmetric or unstable.
- Encode the core 50/50 resolution logic: separation + inside-knee structure + timing (attack until you’re defending) determines whether you arrive in Backside with advantage or get forced into defence.

## Key Positions
- Backside 50/50 base
  - Attacker orientation rotates “behind” the defender’s knee/hip line, improving knee-line stacking and reducing defender’s ability to unwind freely.
- Backside entry off roll-following
  - Common arrival after defender rolls out of Cross/Inside or standard 50/50; the goal is to arrive with wedge + knee-line, not just “stay attached.”
- Backside with inside-knee advantage (safety gate)
  - Inside-knee structure limits defender’s access to your upper body and reduces their ability to separate your legs while you finish.
- Backside with cross-waist control (asymmetry amplifier)
  - Cross-waist variants deny upper-body access, narrow defender’s defensive options, and stabilise breaking alignment once achieved.
- Backside ↔ Far-Hip transitions (safety shell)
  - Used when defender’s counters increase exposure to your own feet or when separation battles are being lost; Far-Hip becomes the stabilising “safe lock” that still attacks.
- Backside → finish / sweep-to-top
  - Platform for submissions and for sweep/top outcomes when defender’s reactions create easy top conversion.

## Key Cues
- Land in a hub, not in a scramble
  - When following a roll, aim to settle into Backside deliberately; if you cannot name your wedge + knee-line status on arrival, you did not “stick the landing.”
- Knee-line and hip-line stacking (structure before break)
  - Backside is still a knee-line game: clamp, wedge, hips close; do not rush breaking mechanics if knee-line is drifting.
- Attack until you’re defending (Backside timing rule)
  - If they begin separating your legs or gaining dominant hand access, defence now (reconnect / rebuild inside-knee / re-wedge), offence later.
- Break symmetry immediately (preferred resolution route)
  - If you enter any 50/50-like exchange, treat Backside as a preferred route to remove mirrored danger and reduce their rotational freedom.
- Heel exposure through constrained rotation (alignment > grip)
  - Use body orientation and hip control to limit their turn/roll options; heel visibility should be created by constraint, not by pulling harder.
- Toe-hold ↔ separation as an entry-enabler (when access is blocked)
  - If their leg configuration blocks access to the second leg/foot needed for clean separation, use toe-hold threat to force leg opening, then re-enter Backside separation/finishing sequences.
- Follow-the-roll mechanics (weight + hips forward)
  - When they roll, bring hips forward so they carry your weight through the rotation; re-establish wedge on the far side, then unwind into finishing alignment.
- Far-hip capture to prevent “running away”
  - If they try to create distance during roll-following or during Backside transitions, control the far hip to prevent escape and force re-entanglement into your preferred structure.
- Protect your own feet while attacking
  - Backside is safer than generic 50/50, not invulnerable; if your heel line becomes accessible, prioritise re-wedging, inside-knee structure, or transition to Far-Hip.

## Decision Rules
- Entry and stabilisation
  - If you arrive off a roll without a clear wedge: stabilise first (hips close + knee-line clamp + inside-knee structure), then re-start offence.
  - If they are forward and climbing to your upper body: rebuild inside-knee and deny head/elbow access before trying to finish.
  - If they lock legs to deny separation: convert to two-leg control (double trouble / leg lace pathway) or force opening via toe-hold dilemma, then return to Backside finishing.
- Finish vs transition selection
  - Finish when:
    - knee-line is stable,
    - your heel is protected,
    - their rotation is constrained,
    - and your finishing alignment is “locked,” not improvised.
  - Transition to Far-Hip when:
    - they are separating your legs,
    - they are winning hand access faster than you are building break pressure,
    - or your heel line is being exposed during the hand fight.
  - Sweep/top when:
    - their defensive reactions create predictable top entry and you can stabilise without giving leg re-entries.

## Drills & Games
- Follow-the-roll → Backside landing drill
  - Start in Cross/Inside or standard 50/50; defender performs a controlled roll; attacker must follow and stick the landing in Backside with a named wedge.
- Backside stabilisation reps (no finish)
  - Attacker holds Backside with clean knee-line + inside-knee structure for a set time before any breaking mechanics.
- Backside separation game (50/50 constraint)
  - Defender scores by separating attacker’s legs; attacker scores by preventing separation and exiting to a stable Backside asymmetry.
- Toe-hold ↔ separation dilemma drill (positional tool)
  - Start in 50/50 where second-leg access is blocked; attacker must use toe-hold threat to force opening, then re-enter Backside stabilisation.
- Backside decision rounds
  - Start in Backside; attacker must choose one of three outcomes:
    - finish (if clean exposure exists),
    - transition to Far-Hip (if own feet at risk),
    - sweep to top and stabilise.
- Backside vs re-pummel game
  - Defender’s only goal is to pummel legs back toward symmetry; attacker maintains Backside alignment, re-establishes inside-knee, and denies separation.

## Common Mistakes
- Following the roll but failing to settle, allowing defender to re-pummel into standard 50/50 or escape knee-line.
- Over-focusing on hands/grips while losing body alignment and wedges.
- Treating Backside as “automatic finish” and rushing breaking mechanics before structure is stable.
- Allowing upper-body access (head/elbow/2-on-1) and continuing offence while they win separation.
- Ignoring toe-hold as a separation tool when access is blocked; repeatedly reaching for the second leg without changing the configuration.
- Ignoring sweep/top outcomes and tunnelling on submissions when defender is structurally safe.
