# System: Heel Hook (Inside vs Outside Mechanics + Priorities)

## 1. Purpose
Heel hooks are treated as the **highest-leverage rotational submission family** in the system, with clear prioritisation:
- **Inside heel hooks** are generally preferred for mechanical strength and tolerance of minor errors.
- **Outside heel hooks** remain essential (especially from Outside Ashi and many standing/transition contexts), but demand tighter positional and hip-pin detail.

This system frames heel hooks as the endpoint of a larger positional process:
- win knee line,
- establish wedges + hip control,
- expose heel,
- apply controlled rotational mechanics,
- and (in this instructional context) convert defensive reactions into **back takes / top dominance** when the finish is denied.

## 2. When to Use It
Use heel hooks when:
- ruleset permits,
- heel exposure is clean and you have knee-line control,
- you can deny the defender’s primary rotation escape routes.

Do not use heel hooks as:
- “panic attacks” from collapsing positions,
- or in symmetric positions where your own heel is not protected.

## 3. Core Structural Requirements
1) **Knee line ownership**
   - Non-negotiable for catastrophic rotational breaking.
2) **Hip control and wedges**
   - Your legs must prevent their hips from freely turning.
3) **Heel exposure is real**
   - Do not confuse “touching the heel” with controlling the heel line.
4) **Your heel safety (in symmetry)**
   - In 50/50/mutual, heel safety precedes offence.

## 4. Mechanics: Shared Principles
- Heel hooks are coupled-force rotational submissions:
  - torso/hips generate rotation,
  - arms/forearm act as the controlling wedge around the heel,
  - legs prevent knee + hip from turning out of danger.
- Key cue:
  - if you feel like you must “crank with arms,” your structure is not doing its job.

## 5. Inside vs Outside: Strategic Roles
### 5.1 Inside heel hook (preferred when available)
- Higher finishing leverage and generally more forgiving alignment.
- Commonly appears from:
  - Cross/Inside Ashi,
  - Backside 50/50,
  - certain Far-Hip transitions.

### 5.2 Outside heel hook (essential competence)
- Commonly appears from:
  - Outside Ashi,
  - certain Mutual Ashi moments (only when structurally safe),
  - standing/transition contexts.
- Requires disciplined hip pinning to stop the defender rotating out.

### 5.3 POD overlay: heel-safety dilemma (Achilles ↔ heel hook)
When defending, recognise the opponent’s dilemma they impose:
- Achilles threat typically wants your foot **extended**.
- Heel hook threat wants your heel **available**.

Your defensive response is a matching “foot-shape” discipline:
- If they’re committing to Achilles: **boot the foot** (reduces extension and Achilles bite).
- If they’re committing to heel hook: **point toes / hide heel line** (reduces purchase on heel).

Structural mitigation (preferred):
- connect your heel to their hip (“back-heel to hip” style connection) so you can manage both threats without guessing.

## 6. Exposure and Entry Logic (System View)
Heel hooks should be reached through a procedure:
1) establish an Ashi hub (Irimi/Outside/Cross/Inside/Backside/Far-Hip),
2) stress-test control (can they back out, stand, roll, pummel?),
3) use tether/dilemma to force reaction (Achilles/toe hold when appropriate),
4) expose heel line,
5) finish only when rotation is constrained.

## 7. Dilemmas and Chains
### 7.1 Achilles ↔ Heel Hook
- Achilles tethers and forces turns/rolls that expose heel.

### 7.2 Toe Hold ↔ Heel Hook
- Toe hold forces rotation/unlocking that reveals heel line.

### 7.3 Heel Hook ↔ Follow-the-roll (land in a hub)
If the defender rolls/spins to defend the heel:
- follow the leg,
- arrive in a named hub (Backside / Far-Hip / re-established Outside/Cross),
- then resume offence from a stable structure.

### 7.4 POD overlay: heel-hook threat → half-roll → calf-slice → come-up/back take
In this instructional context, heel hooks are also used to **force predictable roll behaviour** that yields upper-body access.
- If the defender rolls all the way through and the leg clears: treat it as a come-up moment (bodylock / passing pursuit).
- More commonly: defender commits to a **half roll**, knee starts to slip, and you convert to **calf-slice** + come-up sequences.
- Control detail that matters during the transition:
  - maintain a sticky connection (false grip/Achilles tether; “shoelaces glued to stomach” concept),
  - invert knee inside to recover inside position as you come up,
  - then climb to hips/upper body (turtle/back-attack layer).

## 8. Defensive Responsibilities
- Apply progressively in training; rotational breaks are high risk.
- In symmetric positions:
  - protect your heel first (“attack until you’re defending” still assumes heel safety is addressed).
- If knee line is slipping, abandon the finish and recapture or exit.

### 8.1 POD overlay: 50/50 defence trigger
- Keep feet crossed/hidden as your default “no free heel” posture.
- You are “on offence until defence is required.”
- The moment your legs begin to separate (your heel becomes accessible), stop offence:
  - come forward to control head + far elbow to clear the heel-grip threat,
  - re-cross/hide feet,
  - then return to offence.

## 10. Transition Map
- Heel hook attempt → Backside 50/50 (common roll-follow endpoint)
- Heel hook attempt → Far-Hip (safety + continued offence)
- Heel hook attempt → Toe hold / Achilles (when heel slips but leg control remains)
- Heel hook attempt → Sweep/top/back (if defender over-rotates or rolls into come-up)

## 11. Drilling Progressions
### 11.1 Technical
- Heel exposure reps from each hub (Outside/Cross/Backside/Far-Hip).
- “Stress test first” reps: exposure only after the control check.

### 11.2 Progressive resistance
Defender cycles:
1) roll/spin,
2) turn/kneel,
3) back-out,
4) pummel into symmetry.
Attacker:
- must maintain knee line and land in a planned hub after each defence.

### 11.3 Positional rounds
- Start in a hub with heel exposure available.
- Goal: controlled finish or controlled dominant transition; no reckless cranking.
- Add POD constraint rounds:
  - “Half-roll only” defender: attacker must convert to calf-slice come-up and climb to upper body.

## 12. Common Failure Patterns
- “Chasing heel without wedges”
  - Fix: re-wedge hips/knee line; heel comes after.
- “Losing knee line mid-finish”
  - Fix: stop and recapture; do not pull harder.
- “Attacking first in symmetry with exposed heel”
  - Fix: heel safety first; then offence.
- “Roll happens and you don’t land in a hub”
  - Fix: follow leg + choose landing (Backside/Far-Hip/Outside) before resuming finishing.
