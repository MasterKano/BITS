# System: Knee Line and Wedge Control

## Purpose
- Define the universal control requirements that make leg entanglements functional: own the knee line, control the hips, deny rotation/escape routes, and prevent free leg separation.
- Provide a consistent checklist for when to attack, transition, or reset/exit.
- Reduce “grip-first” behaviour by prioritising structural wedges over hand strength.
- Encode the core SATL principle: control wins first (knee-line + wedge + hip control), then finishing mechanics become reliable; without control, finishes are “imaginary.”

## Key Positions
- Knee line (conceptual reference)
  - Defender’s knee relative to attacker’s hip line is the main indicator of whether breaks are real or imaginary.
  - Knee-line ownership is not binary; the defender will constantly “test” the line via back-out, roll, high-leg, and re-pummel. Your wedge set must answer those tests.
- Primary wedge families (common across Ashi variations)
  - Thigh clamp / knee pinch around defender’s upper leg (prevents sliding escape and knee retraction).
  - Leg scissor / clamp controlling defender’s thigh/hip rotation (denies turning, denies high-leg, reduces back exposure).
  - Cross-shin / hip frame across waist/hip line (blocks step-over/high-leg and controls pelvic rotation).
  - Far-hip control / hook (stabilises rotation, prevents them “running away,” and improves your foot safety).
  - Two-leg control (double trouble / leg lace) when available (limits defender’s base, heist, spin, and locked-legs defence).
  - Inside-knee safety wedge (symmetry-specific)
    - Inside-knee alignment that restricts opponent’s access pathways to your upper body and slows their separation attempts in 50/50-type battles.
- Hand/upper-body components (secondary to wedges)
  - Heel exposure management and finishing connection once wedges are stable.
  - Hand fighting primarily as protection of your own heel and protection of your wedge (preventing them from peeling hands to create separation).

## Key Cues
- Non-negotiables (in order)
  - Knee line first: if the knee is escaping, solve that before “finishing harder.”
  - Hips close: close the distance; extended hips = weak control + easy spins + more heel slip.
  - Know your wedge: you should be able to name the exact wedge preventing each escape direction.
  - Control the far/secondary leg: treat it as an active escape tool (stand/heist/re-pummel/lock-legs), not a passive bystander.
  - Separation awareness: in symmetry (50/50/mutual-like shapes), leg separation is the decisive event; if they separate your legs, defence now.

### Directional control model (defender’s four exits)
- Back out / retreat (knee slides away)
  - Cue: chase hips; tighten knee pinch; re-angle to keep knee trapped deep.
  - Upgrade cue: if the knee is consistently slipping, you need a better wedge, not a stronger grip; add cross-shin/hip frame or transition to a hub with superior hip control.
- Forward pressure (crush and come up)
  - Cue: build frames + leg structure that denies chest-to-head collapse; prioritise hip control over grip changes.
  - Practical bias: forward pressure often exposes their base/hip line; use it to tighten wedges and force them into a roll/turn rather than accepting a “crush stall.”
- Rotate / roll / spin (turns to unwind torque)
  - Cue: follow the leg (hips rotate with them); arrive in a known hub with wedge still present rather than “staying attached” with arms only.
  - “Single roll” mindset: the roll is not the problem; failing to arrive in the next position with wedge + knee-line is the problem.
- Re-pummel (re-thread legs to enter their own entanglement / create symmetry)
  - Cue: win inside position with your legs; control the far leg; avoid leaving a free lane for their pummel.
  - If they are pummelling successfully, treat it as a warning that your wedge is incomplete; either add a wedge (cross-shin / scissor / far-hip) or transition to a safer hub.

### Stress-test logic (before committing to high-risk finishes)
- Briefly reduce hand dependence and confirm wedges hold under:
  - controlled back-out attempt,
  - controlled forward pressure,
  - controlled rotation/roll,
  - controlled re-pummel attempt.
- Pass criteria (simple)
  - Knee line stays captured.
  - Your hips stay close (no extended “hanging”).
  - You can name what stops: back-out, high-leg/step-over, roll, and re-pummel.
  - Your feet remain protected enough that you are not forced into reactive defence mid-finish.

### When to switch legs (continuity + dilemma creation)
- Switch legs when structure is stable and the opponent’s defence is “local” to the current target (they are committing hands/turn/roll to protect that one line).
- Leg switching should not be a reach; it must be structurally supported by wedge and hip control so the new target appears immediately.
- Preferred timing: mid-reaction (as they turn/roll/high-leg), when their base and hands are already committed.

### Symmetry-specific rule (50/50-type battles)
- If they begin separating your legs, defence now (reconnect / re-wedge / inside-knee structure), offence later.
- When access to the second leg is blocked by their leg configuration, force separation via dilemmas rather than “reaching harder” (toe-hold ↔ separation is the canonical example).
- Your objective is not “to stay in 50/50,” it is to exit symmetry into an asymmetrical hub with knee-line still owned.

### Grip discipline (wedge-first, then climb)
- Use grips to finish and to protect wedges; do not substitute grips for wedges.
- If forced to start with a weak grip due to geometry (hands blocked by floor/position), connect first and climb to tighter circles only after wedge is stable.
- Heel slip / hand peel response: do not panic-chase; re-establish wedge/hip closeness, then re-lock with a tighter-circle option or change finishing posture.

### When to reset / exit
- If you cannot clearly maintain:
  - knee line ownership,
  - hip control/back-safety,
  - far-leg awareness,
  - and (in symmetry) leg connection/separation control,
  - then reset to a simpler hub or exit (guard/stand) rather than forcing low-percentage pressure.
- Reset is not failure; reset is the decision that preserves safety and prevents mutual shootouts.

## Drills & Games
- Knee-line retention drill (scripted)
  - Start in a stable entanglement; defender’s only goal is to back-out; attacker’s only goal is hip chase + knee re-trap (no submissions).
- Four-direction stress-test rounds
  - Defender cycles each direction for 20–30 seconds; attacker must maintain wedges or transition to a stronger hub.
- No-hands wedge check (light)
  - Attacker builds entanglement, briefly reduces hand reliance, and must keep defender controlled using legs/hips only; reintroduce hands after control is validated.
- Far-leg awareness game
  - Defender’s goal is to stand/heist using the far leg; attacker scores only if they block the far leg before attacking.
- Follow-the-roll drill
  - Defender performs 1–3 controlled rolls; attacker must maintain knee line and land in a predetermined hub (Outside/Cross/Backside/Far-Hip) with a named wedge.
- Locked-legs breaker game (two-leg control entry)
  - Defender’s goal is to lock legs together to deny separation; attacker’s goal is to convert to two-leg control (double trouble / leg lace) before attempting any finish.
- Symmetry separation game (50/50 constraint)
  - Score for (a) preventing your legs being separated, (b) forcing their legs to open, (c) exiting to an asymmetrical hub while maintaining knee-line.
- Reset competency drill
  - Start in messy entanglement; attacker must choose: rebuild to clean hub, exit to guard, or exit to stand—graded on correct decision and safety.

## Common Mistakes
- Grip-first finishing while the knee line is already escaping.
- Hips drifting away (extended posture) while trying to “hold” the foot.
- Ignoring the far leg, allowing stands, posts, locked-legs defence, and re-pummels that strip control.
- Fighting the roll with arms instead of rotating the hips and following the leg into a known wedge.
- Unclear wedge logic (cannot identify what prevents each escape direction).
- Continuing offence while your legs are being separated in symmetry (late recognition → forced defence too late).
- Reaching for the secondary leg/foot without solving the configuration problem first (instead of forcing separation via dilemmas).
- Failure to reset when structure becomes ambiguous, leading to counters and mutual shootouts.
